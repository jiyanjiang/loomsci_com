When Impossibility Becomes a Blueprint for Trustworthy AI

Drafted May 25, 2026 · Published May 26, 2026 Artificial Intelligence Source: arXiv:2605.23024

Cast your mind back to your days as an undergraduate in computer science—or philosophy, or mathematics—and you will recall the first time you truly absorbed an impossibility theorem. Not the kind that says a task is merely difficult, but the kind that says no amount of cleverness will ever succeed: Turing’s halting problem, Gödel’s incompleteness, Arrow’s voting paradox. There is a chill to those moments. But there is also, if you sit with them long enough, a strange kind of clarification. The boundary, once drawn, makes everything inside it navigable. A preprint (arXiv:2605.23024) by Dongxin Guo now proposes that this very shift—from impossibility as a dead end to impossibility as a design rule—may be exactly what trustworthy AI needs.

The thesis is not yet another algorithm for fairness or interpretability. It addresses something deeper: the limits that are not accidents of current practice but consequences of the computational architecture itself. Its flagship result is something Guo calls the Deterministic Horizon. For a given transformer model, this is an accuracy ceiling that no amount of additional training data, no tuning of the loss function, no increase in adapter rank can surpass. It is, in effect, a reasoning-depth limit inscribed in the number of layers and the width of the embedding. Compute the horizon before deployment, and you know where the model’s abilities will saturate, regardless of how you train it thereafter.

That claim would be remarkable enough on its own, but Guo measures the horizon across twelve widely used transformer architectures and finds it consistently falls between nineteen and thirty-one. In other words, past a certain number of reasoning steps—no matter what the input—the model’s performance collapses. And it does not degrade gently. The thesis derives an information‑theoretic conversion that shows accuracy decaying super‑exponentially beyond the horizon. Fine‑tuning on optimally chosen traces can recover, at best, under four percentage points. “Every fundamental limit of AI is also a design rule,” Guo writes, and this is the concrete, computable number that gives that slogan its teeth.

The mechanism behind the horizon is a capacity invariant of the residual stream. Without reaching for the full mathematics, the idea is intuitively graspable: the residual stream in a transformer acts as a channel through which information about earlier reasoning steps must be preserved if later layers are to build on them. The width and depth of the network impose a hard information‑theoretic ceiling on how much of that history can survive. Past a certain chain length, the signal of earlier steps becomes irrecoverable, and the model begins to reason not from the full context but from a partial, unreliable echo. This is not a defect of particular training runs. It is a property of the architecture, one that can be calculated from the parameter count alone.

The implications for system design are stark and immediately useful. Before a model is ever exposed to users, an engineer can compute the horizon and know that any task requiring more than, say, twenty‑eight consecutive reasoning steps in a given architecture is beyond the model’s reach, regardless of how much it was fine‑tuned. That changes the conversation about reliability: instead of hoping that scaling will eventually fix all deficiencies, designers can embed hard bounds into the specification of the system itself. A medical diagnostic tool that must chain several inferential steps could be designed so that no input forces the model beyond its horizon; a coding assistant could be paired with a verifier that catches any response that demands reasoning chains past the horizon. These are not panaceas, but they are constructive design constraints—exactly the opposite of the despair one ordinarily associates with impossibility theorems.

Guo does not stop with transformers. The thesis assembles a catalogue of sixteen design specifications drawn from impossibility results across subfields. Multi‑stage retrieval pipelines, it proves, require at least as many independent quality metrics as stages; if you collapse them into a single score, you lose the ability to discriminate. Standard truthful auctions fail for agents whose valuation depends on the prompt they receive—a scenario relevant to any AI‑mediated marketplace. Zero‑knowledge verification of neural inference, a privacy‑preserving ideal, carries a measured computational overhead of one hundred ten to one hundred ninety times per non‑linear activation, a cost that cannot be wished away by better cryptography. For each of these, the thesis pairs the computable boundary with a quantified cost of violation and a constructive rule for staying inside the envelope. Four of the sixteen remain open problems—a reminder that the catalogue is a research programme, not a finished manual.

Sixteen precise impossibility rules map every scenario where trustworthy AI fails, along with the costs and design fixes. These specifications transform the fuzzy goal of “trustworthy” into a clear, computable blueprint for building safer AI. (Source: arXiv:2605.23024)

An important question sharpened by earlier work on aligning large language models adds depth to this picture. A study by Xiao et al. (arXiv:2405.16455) showed that reinforcement learning from human feedback can produce a phenomenon called preference collapse, where a model converges to a narrow, overly homogeneous set of outputs even when the training data expresses genuine diversity. The authors of that work demonstrated that the bias is algorithmic, not just a consequence of poor reward modelling. Viewed through the lens of Guo’s framework, such a result is not a mere debugging challenge but a manifestation of a deeper impossibility trade‑off: any alignment scheme that enforces a single global preference ordering will, under realistic conditions, lose information. The tension between faithfulness to diverse human values and the need for a tractable training objective is not something we can engineer out entirely; it is a border that must be designed around.

The thesis does not come without caveats. Some of the claimed results, notably an unconditional circuit‑complexity lower bound for modular exponentiation against constant‑depth prime‑modulus circuits, lack the full step‑by‑step derivation that a specialist would demand; the author acknowledges that these rely on known methods and must be verified. More fundamentally, the framework’s practical applicability remains to be demonstrated in a concrete deployment. Knowing the horizon of a language model is one thing; integrating that knowledge into a safety‑critical pipeline without introducing new failure modes is another. The next phase of work will require engineers and theorists working together to translate the catalogue of specifications into production‑grade guardrails.

Nonetheless, the thesis offers something that the field has been missing: a systematic, cross‑disciplinary foundation for the intuition that trustworthy AI will not be achieved by ignoring limits but by honouring them. For systems that are asked to write software, draft legal documents, or produce clinical notes, the margin for error shrinks every year. Having a map of the territories that are mathematically out of reach—and knowing precisely why they are out of reach—may be the most honest starting point we can have. It does not promise that the resulting AI will be perfectly safe, but it promises that we will stop chasing illusions. In a field that has sometimes drifted toward magical thinking about scale, that is a clarifying gift.

Deterministic horizons, capacity invariants, impossibility‑as‑specification—these are not merely theoretical curiosities. They are the kind of boundary stones that, once driven into the ground, make the field beyond them available for cultivation. The task now is to build systems that live inside the boundaries, and to do so with the same rigour that revealed the boundaries in the first place. That line of work may prove, in the end, to be the real art of intelligence.

— Yanjiang
