When AI Whispered: A Markov Chain Solves Erdős' Old Puzzle

Drafted May 04, 2026 · Published May 19, 2026 Mathematics Source: arXiv:2605.00301

A Markov chain with von Mangoldt weights, suggested by GPT-5.4 Pro, computes the Erdős sum of primitive sets in a sweeping new proof.

Solving a problem that has vexed mathematicians for decades with the help of a large language model. It might sound like science fiction. But that’s exactly what a team of researchers has done. In a preprint (arXiv:2605.00301), Boris Alexeev at OpenAI and his collaborators—including Terence Tao of UCLA—present a new method for bounding Erdős sums of primitive sets, one that was directly suggested by the output of GPT‑5.4 Pro.

Number theory is full of vast, interlocking structures. Yet one particularly stubborn puzzle concerns sets where no number divides another. Call such a set primitive. In 1935, Paul Erdős posed a deceptively simple question: among all primitive sets, which one makes the infinite sum ∑ 1/(n log n) as large as possible? He conjectured that the primes themselves take the crown. For eighty‑seven years the conjecture stood open, a lodestar for anyone fascinated by the hidden architecture of the integers.

A proof finally arrived in 2022, courtesy of Jared Duker Lichtman, who is also a co‑author of the new preprint. His argument was deep but intricate, weaving together delicate analytic estimates. Now, the same circle of researchers has returned with a weapon nobody expected: a random walk.

The heart of their method is a von Mangoldt downward chain. Imagine a traveller standing at some large integer N. At each step, she moves to a smaller divisor of her current position, but not arbitrarily; the chance of stepping from a number n to a proper divisor n/q is proportional to Λ(q)—the von Mangoldt function, which vanishes except on primes and prime powers, giving these 'stepping stones' a privileged role. The chain eventually reaches a fixed absorbing state—in the simplest case, the number 1—and any primitive set A, being composed of numbers that never divide one another, can intersect the chain's path at most once. The expected number of steps before the traveller is gobbled up by A turns out to be intimately related to the Erdős sum itself. In effect, the chain’s journey through the divisibility lattice becomes a measuring tape, and the amount of time it takes to hit A encodes the very sum that Erdős dreamed of taming.

This is not a metaphor for the sake of poetry. The correspondence is precise, and it flows from a single, almost miraculous cancellation: the Markovian transition probabilities, chosen with von Mangoldt weights, cause telescoping cancellations that collapse complicated sums into the clean form Erdős hunted. The authors deploy the chain as a universal wrench. In one stroke, they prove two 1966 conjectures of Erdős, Sárközy, and Szemerédi—one on primitive sets of large numbers (Problem #1196) and another on divisibility chains (Problem #1217). The same machinery also yields a short new proof of the original primitive‑set conjecture, and even dispatches a refined version of the Banks–Martin conjecture that had long been seen as a potential “master theorem” for the field.

Yet even as the chain snaps these famous problems into place, a quieter tension lingers. There are already several proofs of the primitive‑set conjecture, and the new one—elegant as it is—does not emerge fully armored. The authors' argument leans on the clever construction of sub‑invariant weights and adjoint chains—delicate balance sheets that must come out exactly right to keep the Erdős sum in check. While the core machinery is sound, some of the more intricate estimates that support these claims would benefit from additional detail in the current preprint. These are not mere quibbles: the whole edifice depends on the chain’s statistical regularity, and any crack in that foundation could let the Erdős sum slip away. The authors are aware of the issues and have promised a revision. This is the kind of honesty that separates a serious preprint from a finished theorem.

The tension is instructive. It mirrors an older debate about what counts as a canonical proof of the primitive‑set conjecture. Lichtman's 2022 proof was correct but long; the new chain‑based proof offers a shorter route, though some technical details still need to be filled in. Which flavour of certainty should we prefer? The answer may be that mathematics, like physics, thrives on multiple independent routes to the same peak. A flawed shortcut can still reveal new terrain, and the von Mangoldt chain—if eventually patched—may open doors to other problems that resisted attack.

Perhaps the most arresting feature of the story is not the chain itself but its origin. The preprint states plainly that the method was “suggested from output of GPT‑5.4 Pro.” This is not a gimmick. It is a glimpse of a future in which language models, trained on the whole corpus of human mathematics, begin to offer genuinely novel ideas. The researchers did not blindly trust the machine; they recognized a pattern—a Markovian structure with number‑theoretic weights—and turned it into a rigorous tool. The AI did not prove the conjectures; it whispered the shape of a strategy, and the humans did the rest. This division of labour feels almost allegorical: the AI as a voice in the crowd, naming a direction that nobody had thought to look.

So what remains? The repairs are under way. If the chain can be made fully rigorous, it will stand alongside Lichtman’s original proof not as a replacement but as a complementary lens. The von Mangoldt walk, with its stochastic flavour, conjures a picture of divisibility as a landscape sculpted by the logarithms of primes—a scale‑invariant terrain where the most ordinary numbers become rare stopping points. That image, even before it is sanctified by a complete proof, is already worth the journey.

Could this chain, born from a language model’s suggestion, one day reshape the way mathematicians think about divisors? That’s the sort of prediction I would welcome. The road is not yet paved, but the direction is thrilling.
