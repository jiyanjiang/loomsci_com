When Better AI No Longer Means Better Models of Vision

May 19, 2026 Bioscience Source: arXiv:2504.16940

As artificial vision models surpass human-level accuracy, their internal representations diverge from the primate brain's visual pathways, breaking a decade-long alignment.

For a decade, deep neural networks were the diligent translators of vision. Feed them an image of a cat, and they would not just produce the label “cat” — they would generate a cascade of internal activations that mirrored, layer by layer, the signal flow through the primate ventral stream. This was not poetry; it was quantitative. The firing rates of real neurons in macaque inferotemporal cortex could be predicted from a network’s intermediate representation with startling fidelity. It was as if the machines had learned to speak the brain’s native tongue — and the promise was clear: as AI got better at recognizing objects, it would become an ever sharper model of the biological mind that had evolved to do the same.

Until it stopped.

A preprint (arXiv:2504.16940) by Drew Linsley, Pinyuan Feng, and Thomas Serre at Brown and Columbia Universities now shows that the long‑assumed alignment between artificial and biological vision has not merely plateaued. It has begun to unravel. Once modern neural networks pushed past roughly seventy percent accuracy on ImageNet — a canonical object‑recognition benchmark — their ability to predict neural responses in primate IT cortex began to weaken. The same trend emerged when the team measured how well model decisions matched the image regions that humans deem important for recognition. By three independent yardsticks, the better the machines got, the less they resembled us.

Think of the old models as naturalists, identifying birds by wing shape and plumage. The new models, trained on terabytes of uncurated internet imagery, behave more like forensic technicians — they fixate on texture, context, and background grain rather than an object’s contour. A statistical breakdown of model hyperparameters bears this out. Before the alignment peak, architectural depth was the strongest predictor of a network’s fidelity to biology. After the peak, the recipe changed: the crucial factors became pre‑training on internet‑scale data and the shift toward transformer backbones. The very ingredients that deliver superhuman accuracy — pre-training on internet‑scale data and the shift toward transformer backbones — are the ones that pull synthetic vision away from the primate path.

This is not a subtle drift; it is a sign change. The correlation that once promised a shared journey toward understanding has flipped. For seven decades of AI history, vision scientists assumed that engineering progress would naturally yield biological insight. That assumption now looks like a fossil — a convenient shortcut that has run its course. The machines are no longer our mirror.

It is tempting to lay all blame on ImageNet or the sheer scale of data. But the study is strictly correlational; it cannot prove that chasing benchmark accuracy causes the decoupling. A more cautious reading is that the same forces that drove accuracy higher — the architectural pivot from convolutional networks to vision transformers, driven by the efficiencies of parallel GPU clusters — also shattered the alignment. The divergence could be collateral damage, not a direct consequence. Yet the fact that the pattern repeats across three distinct benchmarks — neural, behavioral, and core recognition — makes coincidence increasingly strained.

An important perspective comes from earlier work on multiview object consistency. Bonnen and colleagues, in a 2024 study, found that models that achieve higher accuracy at recognizing objects across varied viewpoints actually diverge more from human perceptual similarity judgments. The more omnidirectional the synthetic vision, the less it tracks how we see. Their result, appearing independently and in a different paradigm, lends corroboration: the decoupling may be a signature of a fundamental strategic shift that modern AI has embraced and the primate brain has not. If two separate groups, using different benchmarks, both find that better engineering breaks the biological tie, then the link was never an axiom — it was an era-specific accident that is now closing.

What, then, should vision science do? Linsley and colleagues argue that it must chart its own course. Instead of hoping that the next trillion‑parameter model will accidentally recapitulate the retinal processing streams or the recurrent circuitry of the ventral visual pathway, the field should build algorithms explicitly grounded in neural and behavioral data. This is not a repudiation of machine learning; it is a declaration of independence. The authors call for moving beyond the convenient symbiosis in which engineers provide new architectures and neuroscientists passively test them. If you want to understand how a bird flies, you stop studying a 747.

This recommendation is itself a paradox, because the most powerful models in vision science are, for now, still neural networks. The challenge is to change how they are built — to replace the uncurated image stream with the constraints that biology imposes: the statistics of natural scenes as seen through a foveated retina, the temporal dynamics of saccadic eye movements, the developmental timeline of an infant’s visual system. The harmonized vision transformer described in the paper — a model trained not just on labels but also on human‑like feature importance maps — offers a glimpse of what becomes possible when biological constraints are woven into the optimization rather than treated as an afterthought. Under that regime, the alignment is partly recovered, though not yet to the level of the pre‑peak era.

The image that lingers is of a student who has outgrown the teacher. For years, deep networks learned to see by watching us — not literally, but through our curated datasets, our human‑filtered categories. Then they learned from the internet itself, a far wilder visual diet. The fluency they gained in that maelstrom came at a cost: they no longer see the world as we do, and they cannot tell us how we do. The Rosetta Stone has been replaced by a code we wrote but can no longer read.

Perhaps the most unsettling implication is philosophical. If the models that succeed at object recognition are not the ones that resemble the brain, then the brain’s own visual strategy — refined over hundreds of millions of years — may be optimal not for generic pattern matching, but for the particular embodied, active, and temporally unfolding way that we encounter the world. The machines are not wrong; they are solving a different problem. And the problem they solve, image classification on demand, may be a poor proxy for what the visual brain actually does.

What makes this moment so charged is that we have been looking at the wrong oracle. For a time, it gave us the answers we wanted. Now it speaks in tongues — wildly proficient, beautifully efficient, yet no longer a guide to the mind that gave it birth. The path forward is not to make the student look like us again. It is to remember that we are not our students, and to rediscover what it means to see with a primate’s eye.
