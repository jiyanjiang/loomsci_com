When Market Friction Learns to Hedge

May 19, 2026 Other Sciences Source: arXiv:2511.23295

A signature-based hedge steers the optimal trading strategy through market friction, transforming the river of price history into a tractable mathematical path.

Think of a riverboat captain navigating a serpentine waterway. The current shifts without warning, sandbars appear and vanish, and every turn of the wheel sends ripples that reshape the path for the next vessel. This is the world of hedging path-dependent options — financial contracts whose payoff depends not just on where a stock price ends, but on the entire winding trail it took to get there. The captain’s craft is mathematics, and until recently, the best tools demanded heroic simplifications: ignore the ripples, pretend the river flows smoothly, assume tomorrow’s turn doesn’t depend on yesterday’s wake.

A team of mathematicians — Eduardo Abi Jaber at École Polytechnique, and Donatien Hainaut and Edouard Motte at Université Catholique de Louvain — has proposed a new steering mechanism. Their preprint (arXiv:2511.23295) introduces a signature approach to pricing and hedging path-dependent options when trading itself moves the market, a friction every real trader knows intimately. The result is more than a technical fix; it is a philosophical reorientation. The market’s friction, long seen as an obstacle to perfect hedging, turns out to be a gift that makes the mathematics not only possible but elegant.

To understand why, we need to step back and ask: what makes path-dependent hedging so difficult? A vanilla call option depends only on the final stock price; an Asian option, by contrast, pays you the difference between the average price over the last month and a strike. To hedge it, you must trade not based on a snapshot, but on the entire price history. Worse, every trade you make feeds back into the market — your own buying drives up the price, your selling depresses it, leaving a signature on the very path you are trying to tame. This is market impact, both temporary (the immediate splash) and permanent (the lasting shift of the riverbed). The resulting control problem is nonlinear, non-Markovian, and, in the hands of tradition, intractable without severe approximations.

The team’s insight is to borrow a tool from rough path theory, a branch of mathematics that treats a path as an object in its own right, encoded by a sequence of iterated integrals. A signature is like the DNA of a trajectory: the first level records the change in value, the second records the area swept out by the price curve, the third captures higher-order correlations, and so on, ad infinitum. It is a kind of Taylor series for paths — not approximating a function, but compactly storing the entire history. Indeed, a path’s signature, if you take enough terms, uniquely determines the path itself, up to a fine detail called tree-like equivalence.

Abi Jaber, Hainaut, and Motte use this mathematical microscope to reframe hedging with market impact. Instead of trying to compute the optimal trading strategy as a function of the price history in some ad hoc way, they ask: what if the strategy itself can be written as a linear combination of the signature of the relevant variables? The answer is startling. Under a mean-quadratic variation criterion — a way of balancing expected hedging error against the cost of trading — the problem reduces to an infinite-dimensional Riccati equation on what is known as the extended tensor algebra. If that phrase sounds like the incantation of a mathematician who has lost the plot, consider a more familiar analogy from physics. In classical control theory, decelerating a car to a stop with minimal fuel yields a Riccati equation whose solution gives the optimal braking force as a linear feedback of the current speed and position. The signature approach extends this logic to the full memory of the road: the optimal hedge becomes a linear function not of the current state, but of the entire signature of the joint price-trading path. The past is not forgotten; it is inscribed in the coefficients of an infinite-dimensional controller.

This shift from Markovian myopia to signature memory has a beautiful practical consequence. Market impact, far from making the problem messier, actually smooths the optimal strategy. Imagine steering that riverboat with violently erratic wheel turns: every jerk creates a backwash that destabilizes the hull. Friction — the water’s resistance — penalizes such abruptness, encouraging a fluid, gentle arc. In the mathematics, the same thing happens. Without impact, the optimal hedge can oscillate wildly, requiring many signature terms to capture its delicate dance; with impact, the strategy becomes sluggish and regular, so that a low-truncation signature — keeping only the first few levels — already yields highly accurate approximations. The team’s numerical experiments bear this out. Across a battery of payoff types — a European quadratic option, an Asian quadratic, even a European call — the signature-based hedge, truncated to just a handful of terms, matches or surpasses traditional delta hedging and even approaches the performance of strategies obtained by solving the full Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation, the gold standard of stochastic control. The histogram of hedging errors tightens; the mean-quadratic variation criterion improves.

But wait. If the signature is infinite, how can a few terms be enough? This is where the riverboat analogy reaches its limit, and the mathematics demands a harder look. The Riccati equation lives on the tensor algebra, an infinite-dimensional vector space whose basis elements are all formal products of path components. Solving it exactly is, in general, impossible; the authors approximate by truncating the space — keeping only products of bounded length — and then numerically solving a finite-dimensional projected Riccati equation. The trouble is, no convergence proof exists. As the adversarial dialogues conducted within the paper’s broader critical review reveal, an important question raised by earlier work on signature trading (Futter et al., 2023) is whether a rigorous verification theorem can bridge the gap between the infinite-dimensional formulation and the truncated numerical recipe. The authors themselves acknowledge this: “We emphasize that we have not provided a general existence and uniqueness proof for the infinite-dimensional Riccati system… this is a highly nontrivial problem.” In other words, the mathematical cathedral is built on a foundation that may, as of now, be as sound as a sandbar.

Moreover, while the approach gives explicit formulas for the case without permanent impact — a substantial advance that generalizes classical results — the full problem with permanent feedback remains coded in a Riccati system whose well-posedness is unknown. Another tension emerges when comparing this work to the fast-growing literature on signature-based machine learning. Bayer et al. (2025) showed that deep-signature neural networks can price and hedge American options under rough volatility; Jaber et al. (2025) used signatures with shallow and deep learning to hedge path-dependent claims with memory. Those methods are data-driven, black-box, but empirically powerful. The current paper offers an analytically explicit alternative — a transparent formula, not a trained neural net — but stops short of benchmarking its numerical accuracy against those competitors. This sits in an interesting conversation: is an explicit, interpretable strategy more valuable than a more accurate black box? The paper does not settle that debate, but it sharpens the question.

Perhaps the most provocative claim is that the Riccati solution provides a “linearization” of the control problem. The adversarial dialogue with the authors of “Signature Trading” pushed back: their own earlier work had already observed that the cost functional becomes quadratic in the signature, and that the control appears linearly. The present paper’s novelty lies less in the observation itself than in the systematic derivation of the Riccati structure and the explicit characterization of the coefficient dynamics, including the surprising role of the shuffle product, a combinatorial rule for multiplying tensors that mirrors the intertwining of past and present increments. Still, the claim that the framework is entirely new must be tempered: it stands on shoulders that are, in some cases, only recently built but no less real.

Where does this leave us? The paper is not a finished product but an invitation. It opens a door to treating path-dependent hedging with market impact as a mathematically principled extension of classical linear-quadratic control, a perspective that could unify a scattered literature. The signature, once a tool for analyzing rough equations, becomes a candidate for the fundamental state variable of an intelligent trading system. Yet the road from this elegant theory to a trader’s desk remains long and uncertain. Convergence proofs must be supplied; truncation error bounds must be established; the computational cost of even a low-truncation Riccati equation in high dimensions must be wrestled down.

Still, the direction is clear. Market friction, which traders curse as slippage, turns out to be the very force that makes mathematical order possible. The signature of every trade — the record of the river’s every bend — is not a burden to be forgotten, but the raw material of rational hedging. Perhaps, in the end, the deepest lesson of this work is not about options at all. It is about the nature of memory. In a world that constantly tempts us to simplify, to discard the past and pretend the next move depends only on the present, the signature whispers a different truth. The path matters, all of it, and, thanks to friction, we can finally afford to listen.
