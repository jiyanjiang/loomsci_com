Reading Bodies, Missing Minds: AI’s Struggle with Theory of Mind

Drafted May 18, 2026 · Published May 19, 2026 Artificial Intelligence Source: arXiv:2511.15887

AI can recognize a smile but misses the mind behind it, exposing a gap between textbook knowledge and real-time social understanding.

Picture this: you’re sitting across from a friend. She smiles, but her arms are crossed. Her foot taps a nervous rhythm against the floor. No words are spoken, yet you know—she’s uneasy, perhaps hiding something. Reading a slouch, a glance, an almost imperceptible neck‑touch is so automatic that we rarely notice we’re doing it. But notice it we should, because a new benchmark from a team led by Youngjae Yu at Yonsei University and Seoul National University (arXiv:2511.15887) reveals just how badly today’s most advanced AI stumbles when it tries to do the same. In the space of a few video frames, the machines see a body, but they miss the mind.

The researchers constructed Motion2Mind, a carefully curated dataset built from nearly 500 hours of television and film. They sampled short clips, had human experts annotate fine‑grained nonverbal cues—a tightened jaw, a palm displayed, a glance away—and then paired each cue with the psychological state it most likely signals: anxiety, superiority, deception, relief. The reference point is an expert‑curated dictionary of 222 distinct nonverbal cues and 397 mind states, inspired by the work of former FBI agent Joe Navarro. The result is a three‑stage test of artificial theory of mind: Detection—can the model spot the cue? Knowledge—does it know what that cue generally means? Explanation—can it map the cue, in context, to the correct mental state?

The results, as you might already suspect, are humbling. If you ask a large language model with vision capability a simple “what does a jaw‑tighten mean?” question, it performs almost as well as a human. The Knowledge stage is no great challenge; models have absorbed countless psychology articles and body‑language manuals. But when the same model is dropped into a 32‑frame clip and asked what is happening right now in this specific moment, the performance gap opens wide. Detection—the simple act of recognising that a cue is present—is surprisingly poor. Explanation, where context finally matters, is worse. And most tellingly, the AI exhibits a pattern the authors call over‑interpretation: faced with an ambiguous movement, it confidently assigns a psychological meaning where a human would admit uncertainty. “That’s just a smile, nothing more” is not an answer the model is inclined to offer.

It would be easy to tell a simple story here: machines can’t read bodies, they lack theory of mind, the end. But the Dialectic style demands we probe deeper—and the benchmark itself, for all its elegance, leaves some important doors ajar.

An important question raised by earlier work on multimodal theory of mind (Jin et al. 2024) is whether the mental states captured in this dictionary are truly the ones that define theory of mind in humans. The 397 categories in Motion2Mind are overwhelmingly affective and attitudinal—fear, confidence, disgust, interest. What’s largely absent are the belief‑rich, intentional states that philosophers and developmental psychologists place at the heart of ToM: she thinks I know the cake is hidden here, or he intends to deceive me. This limitation isn’t fatal—emotions are undeniably part of social intelligence—but it means the benchmark tests a specific slice of mind‑reading, not the full recursive nesting that separates human social cognition from pattern matching.

A further tension, sharpened by work on belief prediction in nonverbal settings (Bortoletto et al. 2024), concerns the role of context. The Motion2Mind Explanation task asks the model to guess the mental state from a brief clip, but in real life we use far richer contextual memory—we know what happened five minutes ago, we know the person’s history. The constraint to 32 frames, while necessary for experimental control, may artificially inflate the difficulty and obscure what AI could do with more information. Conversely, Chen et al. (2024) have argued that over‑reliance on curated TV clips may not generalise to the messier, more ambiguous social signals we navigate in daily life. The actors in a sitcom telegraph emotion more clearly than any of us do in a meeting room; a benchmark built on such footage risks both fooling us into thinking AI is improving faster than it is, and simultaneously masking the depth of its blindness.

One could object: “So what? We don’t need AI to read body language—we have humans for that.” But that misses the larger conceptual stake. Theory of mind is not a niche social skill; it may be the central cognitive capacity that separates human‑like cooperation, communication, and morality from mere stimulus‑response behaviour. If large language models can’t infer a hidden mental state from a glance, what does that tell us about their ability to understand intentions in a medical consultation, to detect deceit in a financial transaction, or to genuinely empathise in a therapeutic conversation? The gap between Knowledge and Explanation that Motion2Mind exposes is, at its core, a gap between having a textbook and knowing when to close it.

The fascination of the work thus lies not in proving AI’s weakness, but in laying bare the nature of the chasm. The machines don’t struggle because they lack data; they struggle because they lack what psychologists call the intentional stance—the automatic ascription of beliefs, desires, and goals to another agent. A smile, in the intentional stance, is not a facial‑muscle configuration; it is a window into a mind that may be pleased, anxious, or merely polite. When a model sees all of these as equally probable and selects the one with the highest training‑data association, it is doing something fundamentally different from what a six‑month‑old infant does when she tracks her mother’s gaze toward a hidden toy.

To be clear, the Motion2Mind framework does not claim to measure the intentional stance; it claims to benchmark nonverbal cue understanding. And on those terms, it succeeds. It provides a publicly available, expertly annotated resource that disentangles the components of visual ToM in a way no previous benchmark has attempted. The Detection‑Knowledge‑Explanation triad is a clean analytical razor, and the finding that models over‑explain when they should stay silent is as unsettling as it is instructive. For the first time, we have a systematic picture not only of what AI gets wrong, but of how it gets wrong—over‑confident, pattern‑driven, context‑blind.

What this work challenges is the seductive illusion that social intelligence emerges naturally from scaling up multimodal transformers. The gap is not a few percentage points of accuracy that the next model will erase; it is a qualitative difference in the kind of reasoning that unfolds when we try to understand another being. Until AI can genuinely model beliefs, intentions, and layered deceptions, it will remain a spectator at the edge of the human social world—able to describe the dance, but never to hear the music that moves it.
