A Shape That Protects: Can Topology Be Independent of Symmetry?

Drafted May 08, 2026 · Published May 21, 2026 Matter Science Source: arXiv:2605.06383

What does it mean for something to be protected in physics? For decades, the answer in the world of topological matter has been simple: symmetry. If a crystal has the right symmetries—if its atomic arrangement respects certain mirror planes, rotational axes, or inversion centers—then exotic states can emerge at its edges, corners, and hinges. Break the symmetry, and the protection vanishes. The gapless modes can be gapped out. The topological phase collapses.

But what if this answer is incomplete? What if there is another kind of protection, one that doesn't care about the neat periodic arrangements of atoms, but cares instead about something deeper—the very shape of the space the system inhabits?

This is the provocative question posed by a preprint (arXiv:2605.06383) from a team spanning India, Sweden, and Germany. Shahroze Shahab at the National Institute of Technology Rourkela, Hui Liu at Stockholm University, Daniel Varjas at Stockholm, and Ion Cosma Fulga at the IFW Dresden and the Würzburg-Dresden Cluster of Excellence ct.qmat have proposed a new class of topological phases where the protection comes not from crystalline symmetry, but from the system's global topology—specifically, its genus, or number of holes. They call them genus-protected topological phases, or GPT phases. And the idea, if it holds, challenges one of the foundational assumptions of the field.

The geography of protection

To understand why this matters, we need to visit a landscape where most physicists feel at home: the familiar territory of higher-order topological phases.

Imagine a square piece of topological insulator. It's an unremarkable shape—Edges, corners, flat surfaces. In the right phase, it hosts zero-energy modes at its corners. These are the celebrated corner states of higher-order topology. But here's the catch: those corner states exist because the square respects certain crystalline symmetries. If you were a malicious experimentalist—and physicists often imagine themselves as exactly that—you could deform the edges, smear the corners, and generally make a mess of the surface. As long as you don't close the bulk gap, the symmetry-breaking surface perturbation would remove the corner modes. They are protected by symmetry, and symmetry can be broken.

This is the liability of symmetry-protected topological phases. They are hostages to the crystal lattice. And for years, physicists have accepted this as the price of admission. But Shahab and colleagues are asking: is there a way to escape this hostage situation entirely?

The answer, they argue, lies in geometry—not of the lattice, but of the space itself.

Think of a flat disk. It has one continuous edge. Gapless modes living on that edge can be brought together and annihilated simply by sliding them around the perimeter until they meet and gap out. But now imagine a Corbino disk—a disk with a hole punched through its center. Suddenly, there are two edges: an inner boundary and an outer boundary. Modes living on one edge cannot be brought to the other without crossing the bulk. The hole in the disk—the hole that gives it genus one—creates a topological obstruction. The modes are protected, not by symmetry, but by the sheer geometry of having nowhere to go without traversing the insulating interior.

Topological edge modes persist on rings and doughnuts but disappear on disks and spheres. This matters because it shows that a material's shape can lock in protected quantum states, key for building stable quantum computers. (Source: arXiv:2605.06383)

The shape itself has become the guardian.

A menagerie of holes and handles

The team's construction generalizes this simple picture with remarkable versatility. A two-dimensional system with one hole—a Corbino disk geometry—can host Majorana zero modes that are robust against any purely-surface perturbation. It doesn't matter if you deform the boundaries, add impurities along the edges, or generally abuse the surface. As long as the fundamental symmetries—time-reversal or particle-hole symmetry—are preserved, and the bulk gap remains open, those zero modes survive.

A cylinder made from an odd number of quantum wires produces two distinct edges, each with an odd number of exotic quantum particles. This arrangement exploits a hole in the material to shield these particles from disruptions, a key step toward building stable quantum computers. (Source: arXiv:2605.06383)

What happens with more holes? The classification blossoms. A 2D system with two holes—genus two—can support four distinct GPT phases, classified by a rich algebraic structure. Move to three dimensions, and a torus—genus one—hosts four distinct phases. A double torus, with its two handles and more complex surface topology, supports sixteen.

Think of it like a musical instrument. A single flat drumhead can sustain certain standing waves. Add holes—as in a violin body with its f-holes—and the acoustic modes multiply. The shape of the resonating cavity determines what notes can exist. In the same way, the topology of space—its connectivity, its handles, its boundary structure—determines what topological modes can inhabit it.

This is not a metaphor. The mathematical invariance that the team constructs—invariants involving nested Wilson loops, reflection-matrix determinants that flip sign between trivial and nontrivial phases—tells you precisely how the genus encodes the protection. The invariants are geometric, not symmetry-based. They probe the global shape of the system, not its local crystalline arrangement.

An uncomfortable question from earlier work

But there's a shadow over this elegant construction, one that sharpens when you look at it through the lens of earlier topological classification schemes. An important question raised by prior work on extrinsic higher-order topological phases—particularly the classification developed in arXiv:2201.07205—is whether the protection claimed here is genuinely independent of the underlying crystalline phase, or whether it subtly inherits protection from the parent lattice.

Here is the tension: the explicit models that Shahab and colleagues construct—the Benalcazar-Bernevig-Hughes model for 2D, the class-D Hamiltonians for systems with dislocations, the quantum spin Hall models with disclinations—all begin from lattices that are themselves topological. The hole is punched into something that was already topologically nontrivial. The genus does not create the topology from nothing; it reshapes what was already there.

This is not a fatal objection. The team is clear that their construction requires the underlying system to be in a topological phase—the genus is an additional ingredient, not a standalone generator. But the earlier work forces us to ask: where does the protection truly reside? If the parent phase is necessary, then the genus is not entirely independent. It is a modifier, not a creator.

The team's response, implicit in their framework, is that the genus does something that the crystalline phase alone cannot do: it makes the boundary modes robust against symmetry-breaking perturbations at the surface. The parent phase provides the bulk topology; the genus provides the surface resilience. It is a partnership, not a solo act.

Still, the question lingers. The boundary between "genus-protected" and "symmetry-protected-with-shape-enhancement" is not always sharp.

The weight of experimental reality

Here we encounter another productive tension. The geometries required for GPT phases—tori with specific hole structures, Corbino disks with precisely placed dislocations, three-dimensional solids with handles—are not readily available in the condensed matter laboratory. Photonic and acoustic platforms, where lattices can be fabricated with nearly arbitrary geometries, may be more promising. But in solid-state systems, where topology first found its experimental home, the path to realizing a genus-protected phase is steep.

This is not a reason to dismiss the idea. Theoretical classifications often run ahead of experimental capability—that's how physics works. The question is whether the idea is sufficiently fertile to inspire new experimental directions. And here, the GPT concept has genuine potential. It suggests that the next generation of topological devices might engineer shape as actively as they engineer band structure.

But we should be precise about what is and isn't demonstrated. The team's calculations show that the invariants flip sign, that the zero modes persist, that the surface perturbations fail to gap them out. They do not show that this is feasible in a real material with disorder, finite temperature, and all the other indignities of the laboratory. The gap between a beautiful Hamiltonian and a working device is wide, and it is bridged by more than clever mathematics.

The question is not whether the idea is elegant—it is. The question is whether the protection is robust enough to survive the transition from idealized geometry to messy reality.

What this challenges

The deeper significance of this work lies not in a specific experimental prediction, but in the conceptual shift it demands. For decades, the field of topological matter has been organized around a central dogma: symmetries classify phases. The ten-fold way, the symmetry indicators, the exhaustive catalogs of topological band structures—all depend on the intimate relationship between crystal symmetry and topological protection.

The GPT concept suggests that this picture is incomplete. It says: look beyond the lattice, look at the space itself. The connectivity of the system—how many holes it has, where its boundaries are, which loops are contractible and which are not—can encode topological information independently of the crystalline arrangement.

This is a significant philosophical reorientation. It means that topology is not merely a property of Hamiltonians with certain symmetries; it is a property of the spatial arena in which those Hamiltonians operate. The shape of the container matters. The holes in the system are not defects to be ignored; they are features that generate new physics.

The classification tables that occupy the end of the preprint—two phases for a genus-1 2D disk, four for genus-2, four for a 3D torus, sixteen for a 3D double-torus—are not just bookkeeping. They are evidence that the shape is doing real topological work, that the genus is a genuine control parameter.

If this is right, then the toolkit for engineering topological phases expands dramatically. We could imagine designing the shape rather than engineering the crystal structure. We could imagine topological protection that survives amorphization, that persists in disordered systems, that cares only about the macroscopic geometry and not the microscopic lattice.

That is a provocative vision. It is not proved by this preprint. But the preprint opens the door to proving it—or disproving it—in the rigorous language of invariants and explicit models.

The architecture of space

There is a deeper principle at work here, one that resonates beyond condensed matter physics. In field theory, we are accustomed to thinking of space as a passive backdrop—a coordinate system on which the dynamics unfold. But the GPT concept pushes against this passivity. The shape of space is an active participant. It constrains, enables, protects. It has topological agency.

This is not a new idea in physics—general relativity taught us that spacetime geometry is dynamical, that matter tells space how to curve and space tells matter how to move. But in the world of quantum materials, where we typically control Hamiltonians with exquisite precision while treating the sample geometry as an afterthought, it is a quietly revolutionary suggestion.

Perhaps what Shahab, Liu, Varjas, and Fulga have done is remind us that topology is about holes and handles and connectivity—not about the neat periodic arrangements that happen to fill them. The lattice is a convenience. The shape is fundamental.

The four distinct phases on a 3D torus, the sixteen on a double torus—these numbers are not mere combinatorics. They are echoes of a deeper truth: that the universe has opinions about what can exist in it, and those opinions are written in the geometry of space itself.

We should not overstate the case. The GPT phases described here are model-dependent; the invariants are constructed for specific Hamiltonians; the path to experiment is uncertain. The shape is a giver of protection, but it is not a giver of everything. The philosophical implications are profound—for decades, condensed matter physicists have assumed that topology follows symmetry—but the concrete, workable physics is still being built.

What this preprint gives us, ultimately, is not a final answer but a sharper question. It takes the old, comfortable assumption—topological protection comes from crystalline symmetry—and holds it up to the light, asking whether we might have been looking at only half the picture. The shape of space, the holes that punctuate it, the handles that connect it—these may be topological actors in their own right, independent of the lattice that fills them. The evidence is in the invariants that change sign when a dislocation is introduced, in the zero modes that persist when edges are deformed, in the classification tables that grow with the number of holes.

That is not the end of the story. It is, perhaps, the beginning of a new chapter—one in which we learn to think of geometry not as a stage for topological physics, but as one of its principal players.
