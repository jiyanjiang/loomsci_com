EUV Holography Learns to Print Curved Patterns at 40 nm

May 21, 2026 Matter Science Source: arXiv:2605.21430

Imagine a printmaker who, for years, can only produce repeating wallpaper patterns—stripes, grids, perfect lattices. Every stamp she carves is periodic; she can rotate it, tile it, overlap it, but she cannot draw a freehand curve. Then one day someone hands her a transparency etched with an intricate, non‑repeating design. When light shines through it, the whole picture—curves, junctions, angled lines—appears on the paper below, crisp and whole. She has traded her stamps for a holographic stencil.

Now you know how it has been for nanofabrication with extreme ultraviolet (EUV) light. A team spanning the Chinese Academy of Sciences’ Institute of Microelectronics and the Paul Scherrer Institute has demonstrated a lensless EUV technique that can print arbitrary curvilinear patterns—not just periodic ones—at a resolution of 40 nm (arXiv:2605.21430). The achievement moves holographic lithography from a niche curiosity that was stuck at hundreds-of‑nanometre resolution to a genuinely practical prototyping tool, and it does so without the astronomical cost of conventional EUV projection optics.

The crux of the tale is the switch in consensus from “lensless EUV patterning can only produce periodic gratings” to “arbitrary freeform patterns are now achievable”—and the convergence of inverse‑design algorithms and advanced mask fabrication that led to that change. To appreciate the shift, it helps to recall why the chipmaking industry uses EUV light in the first place. Producing the world’s most advanced integrated circuits—those at the 7‑nm node and beyond—demands light of extremely short wavelength so that individual transistor features can be drawn smaller than the tiniest dust speck. But at a wavelength of 13.5 nm, ordinary glass lenses are useless; they absorb the light rather than bending it. The industry’s solution has been a staggeringly expensive system of mirrors: ultra‑smooth, multi‑layer reflective optics that deliver a demagnified image of a mask onto a silicon wafer. This projection system costs hundreds of millions of dollars, putting it far beyond the reach of university labs and startup fabs.

A conventional EUV projection lithography system is compared with the proposed EUV-HL system. By using diffraction-based imaging, EUV-HL removes the need for bulky projection optics, greatly simplifying the setup while maintaining high resolution. (Source: arXiv:2605.21430)

For small‑scale research, a simpler lensless route has long existed: interference lithography. Shine two coherent EUV beams onto a wafer, and the overlapping light carves a regular grating. By varying the beam angles and exposure steps, you can create simple periodic patterns. But you cannot draw a curved transistor channel or a tapered optical waveguide; the method is intrinsically restricted to what the printmaker would call stamps—periodic motifs. Holographic lithography promises to escape that constraint. In principle, a carefully designed mask diffracts the incoming EUV wave to produce any desired intensity pattern in the far field, just as Gabor’s original holography reconstructed three‑dimensional images from a recorded interference pattern. In practice, designing that mask at 13.5 nm has been fiendishly difficult because the extremely short wavelength means that even nanometre‑scale variations in the mask’s topography scramble the outgoing wavefront—a phenomenon known as the mask‑3D (M3D) effect—and the computation of the diffraction pattern becomes prohibitively expensive for a large mask.

Here’s where the new work departs from earlier attempts. The team, led by Yayi Wei from CAS and Iacopo Mochi from Paul Scherrer Institute, built an inverse‑design framework that treats the mask as a thin, shift‑invariant layer for the purpose of the optimization. In plain terms: instead of solving the full three‑dimensional Maxwell equations for every point on a centimetre‑scale mask—a task that would choke any supercomputer—the model captures the dominant M3D fingerprint in a convolution kernel that applies equally across the whole layout. The kernel encodes how a tiny feature on the mask will scatter light into the far field, including the phase shifts that earlier thin‑mask approximations blithely ignored. Once the kernel is computed, the optimization runs like a deconvolution problem: given the target pattern, what mask transmission best reproduces it after the kernel has done its work? The framework also accounts for the finite spectral width of the synchrotron EUV source, summing the contributions of different wavelengths as if they were independent exposures—a trick that stops chromatism from washing out the final image.

Making the actual mask then becomes a fabrication challenge. The team used electron‑beam lithography to pattern a 200‑nm layer of hydrogen silsesquioxane (HSQ) deposited on an 80‑nm silicon‑nitride membrane. HSQ is a negative‑tone resist that converts to a silica‑like material after exposure, so the written pattern survives the harsh EUV beam that would destroy ordinary polymers. The membrane, suspended like a gossamer drumhead, serves as the stencil. When the team took these masks to the Swiss Light Source and illuminated them with 13.5 nm radiation, the aerially projected images matched the simulations with impressive fidelity, and the printed resist patterns—developed on the wafer below—showed curvilinear glyphs, tight tip‑to‑line connections, and elbows with critical dimensions down to 40 nm. Some features were as fine as 50 nm, roughly half the length of a thousand atoms placed end‑to‑end. That is nearly an order of magnitude finer than any previous demonstration of EUV holographic lithography, and it matches the resolution that many small‑scale researchers actually need.

What makes the result so significant is the combination of three qualities that, until now, had never coexisted. First, the resolution is genuinely sub‑50 nm—comparable to what early‑stage R&D groups require for exploring new transistor architectures or photonic circuits. Second, the design freedom is curvilinear, not just rectilinear: the researchers printed gently meandering lines that would be impossible to synthesize from a set of overlapping gratings. Third, the optical setup remains lensless and therefore accessible. There is no need for the billion‑dollar mirror systems that dominate industrial EUV lithography. Together, these three properties turn EUV‑HL into a uniquely flexible prototyping tool.

The implications stretch beyond today’s lab. Because the method doesn’t care how complex the pattern is—it just runs the inverse‑design algorithm—it can be extended to beyond‑EUV (BEUV) wavelengths, the even shorter light that chipmakers will likely need once traditional EUV runs out of steam. At those wavelengths, no transparent lenses exist, and even multi‑layer mirrors become fantastically difficult to fabricate because the required layer thicknesses shrink to just a few atomic layers. Holographic transmission masks, by contrast, only require a thin membrane that can be patterned with an electron beam. The current paper already shows that the approach works at 13.5 nm; scaling to 6 nm or below becomes, in principle, an engineering challenge rather than a fundamental impossibility. A lensless pathway to arbitrary nanopatterning at BEUV could keep alive the decades‑long progression of Moore’s Law for another node or two—or, more importantly, give university‑scale researchers a direct way to play with such extreme wavelengths without needing a national‑lab‑sized budget.

Holographic lithography, with its roots in Denis Gabor’s invention in the late 1940s—for which he later won the Nobel Prize—has always carried an air of sorcery. Gabor conceived holography not as a way to make computer chips but as a means of improving electron microscopy by recording the diffracted wave and later reconstructing it optically. The idea that a two‑dimensional transparency can code the entire three‑dimensional wavefront is a piece of physical elegance that still feels counterintuitive: how can a flat piece of glass remember which way the light was going? The answer lies in the fact that every path the light could have taken is encoded in the relative phases of the transparency’s many tiny pixels, and those phases conspire, when the mask is illuminated, to reassemble the original wave. In the present context, the algorithm effectively writes the phase conspiration for a specific far‑field pattern, not for a three‑dimensional scene—a computational descendant of Gabor’s insight.

Wei and Mochi’s team have shown that this conspiration can be computed in a practical amount of time, even for the punishing wavelength of EUV, and that the resulting masks can be fabricated with sufficient precision to print real devices. The work does not claim to replace the industrial juggernaut of projection EUV lithography, which is optimized for extreme throughput and yield. But it does fill a gap that has long frustrated nano‑prototyping: the lack of a flexible, lens‑free tool that can draw any shape at deep‑ultraviolet resolution. If the method matures, a small team might one day design a new quantum‑device geometry, run an inverse‑design code, expose a wafer at a synchrotron beamline, and hold a working prototype in hand within days—an agility that the giant fabs cannot offer.

The open question is whether the accuracy of the shift‑invariant approximation can be pushed even further, so that more aggressive curvilinear patterns with dense features can be printed without artefacts. The mask‑3D effect, which the convolution model now captures reasonably well for moderate feature densities, becomes increasingly non‑local when features are packed too tightly. The team’s framework is a compromise between physical fidelity and computational tractability, and future improvements may require a hierarchy of kernels or on‑the‑fly corrections. But for the designs that most research groups need today—isolated structures, test patterns, and moderate device arrays—it already works. And as electron‑beam lithography and membrane technology continue to improve, the minimum feature size will likely inch downward further.

Perhaps the most satisfying aspect of this work is that it resurrects a core idea from the dawn of nanotechnology—holography—and outfits it with modern computational muscle to solve a problem that conventional optics cannot touch. It is a reminder that sometimes the most elegant solution to an intractable engineering problem is not to build a better lens but to let the light do its own bookkeeping, and then gently steer it with a transparency that knows, in a mathematical sense, exactly what picture it wants to paint. The printmaker has not only acquired a new stencil; she has found a way to etch the stencil with an invisible hand guided by the target image itself.

Gabor would probably smile at the sight of his century‑old insight helping to shape the next generation of chips. The question now is: what other patterns, hidden in the mathematics of diffraction, are waiting for their algorithm?
