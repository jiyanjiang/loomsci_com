A Single Impurity Teaches Kagome Electrons to Dance

Drafted May 04, 2026 · Published May 24, 2026 Matter Science Source: arXiv:2503.07015
A single impurity in a kagome superconductor breaks mirror symmetry, triggering chiral quasiparticle interference that spirals clockwise or anticlockwise.

For physicists studying a family of materials known as kagome metals, one of the most puzzling signals has been a swirling pattern that appears in scanning tunneling microscope images. The wavy interference fringes, produced by electrons scattering off defects, sometimes twist in a distinct left- or right-handed way—a handedness, or chirality, that seems to break the mirror symmetry of the underlying crystal. The question has lingered: where, exactly, does this chiral dance come from? A team led by Hiroshi Kontani at Nagoya University, working with colleagues at the Yukawa Institute for Theoretical Physics in Kyoto, now offers an answer. In a preprint (arXiv:2503.07015), they trace the origin of the chiral interference to an intricate three‑way conversation between a current that flows without resistance, a particular pattern of atomic displacements, and the mere presence of a single impurity.

The materials at the heart of the story are the kagome superconductors AV₃Sb₅, where A can be caesium, rubidium, or potassium. The name “kagome” comes from a traditional Japanese basket‑weaving pattern of interlaced triangles, and the atoms in these compounds arrange themselves in exactly that geometry—a lattice of corner‑sharing triangles that fosters exotic electronic behaviour. At low temperatures, the electrons in AV₃Sb₅ refuse to settle into a simple metallic state. Instead, they pass through a cascade of correlated phases: first a charge‑density wave, where the electron cloud develops a frozen ripple with a period longer than the original lattice, and then, at even lower temperatures, a so‑called loop‑current phase. In this phase, electrons begin to circulate around the small triangular motifs much like persistent microscopic current loops, spontaneously breaking time‑reversal symmetry. This is not a flow that dissipates energy; it is a coherent orbital current that, once established, can maintain itself without any external power source.

When scanning‑tunnelling‑microscopy (STM) experiments probe these loop‑current states, they record quasiparticle interference (QPI) patterns—ripples in the local density of states that encode how electrons scatter from defects. In many loop‑current materials, these patterns are symmetric, but in the kagome metals they often exhibit a pronounced chirality: the interference fringes are not mirror‑symmetric, as if the scattered electrons “prefer” to spiral in one direction. Earlier experiments spotted this asymmetry and noted that it could be reversed by applying a modest magnetic field, but a clear theoretical link between the chirality of the loop‑current order and the chirality of the quasiparticle interference (QPI) signal remained elusive. “The underlying origin of QPI chirality remains poorly understood,” the authors write in the preprint. This is the gap that Seigo Nakazawa, Rina Tazai, Youichi Yamakawa, Seiichiro Onari, and Hiroshi Kontani set out to close.

Their starting point is a recognition that the loop‑current phase does not exist in isolation. It always rides on top of a charge‑density wave, and in the kagome metals this charge‑density wave takes a particular form known as the star‑of‑David pattern. (The name comes from the shape formed when a small hexagon of atoms is surrounded by six larger hexagons, reminiscent of the six‑pointed star.) This pattern enlarges the unit cell and creates distinct atomic sites—labelled A, B, and C in the paper—that sit in different local environments relative to the charge‑density‑wave backdrop. Moreover, the loop‑currents themselves can point in three possible directions that are rotated by 120° with respect to each other. Physicists call this three‑way choice a Z₃ nematicity: the system has to pick one of three equivalent but distinct orientational states, much as a nematic liquid crystal aligns its rod‑shaped molecules along a particular axis even though the crystal itself has rotational symmetry.

The team's central insight is that the QPI chirality is not a direct fingerprint of the bulk loop‑current direction itself. Instead, it is an emergent property that appears when a single impurity lands on a specific site and breaks the remaining mirror symmetries of the charge‑density‑wave state. By carefully calculating the local density of states around isolated impurities in a large‑scale tight‑binding model, Nakazawa and his colleagues discovered that even an extremely dilute concentration of defects—below one part in a thousand—can give rise to a clear chiral QPI signal. The handedness of that signal is determined by which sublattice site hosts the impurity: an impurity at an A‑site produces a negative chirality, an impurity at a B‑site produces a positive chirality, and an impurity at a C‑site, by contrast, yields an achiral, mirror‑symmetric pattern. This striking site‑dependence follows from how the Z₃ nematic director sets the surviving mirror symmetries around each impurity site within the star‑of‑David charge‑density‑wave cell.

To understand how this works, it helps to zoom in on the definition of chirality the team used. From the calculated QPI intensity at three symmetry‑related wave‑vectors, they extracted a scalar quantity that is positive for one handedness, negative for the other, and zero when the pattern is symmetric. Their calculations show that for an impurity at the A‑site, the three intensities satisfy I₁ > I₂ > I₃, giving a negative value; for a B‑site impurity, the inequality becomes I₁ < I₂ < I₃, producing a positive value; and for a C‑site impurity, I₂ and I₃ are equal, so the signal is achiral. “A single impurity at site Z induces a quasiparticle interference chirality chi_Z = ±1, determined by the direction of the Z₃ nematicity, itself set by the relative position of the loop‑current order in the star‑of‑David charge‑density‑wave phase,” the team writes. This clean result provides a recipe for interpreting STM images: the spatial distribution of chiral signals on the surface, when mapped with atomic resolution, directly reveals the local nematic arrangement of the loop‑current phase.

This finding already resolves a long‑standing puzzle, but the paper goes further. The team explored what happens when one applies a small magnetic field perpendicular to the kagome plane. They found that the Z₃ nematic state can be smoothly switched from one orientation to another through an adiabatic path, without ever needing to raise the magnetic field enough to destroy the ordered state. The key is that the orbital magnetisation, which is proportional to the product of the three nematic components, changes monotonically along the switching path. Consequently, a gradual change in the external field can nudge the system from, say, the nematic configuration labelled “I” to its oppositely oriented counterpart “−III”, flipping the sign of the chiral QPI signal as it goes. This theoretical prediction is consistent with recent experiments that observe field‑induced shear lattice strain in AV₃Sb₅—a deformation that naturally accompanies a change in the nematic director. “Even a small magnetic field can smoothly switch the chirality, leading to field‑induced shear lattice strain consistent with recent experiments,” the authors note, their theory providing an elegant bridge between the microscopic electronic order and the macroscopic mechanical response.

The calculated QPI patterns themselves are rich. For a model containing over a thousand sites, the team demonstrates that the local density of states near an isolated impurity becomes strongly modulated over surprisingly long distances. This long‑range modulation is itself chiral, reflecting the handedness of the underlying loop‑current phase. When the charge‑density wave is absent or when the loop‑current order is removed, the patterns revert to being perfectly symmetric around the impurity, confirming that both ingredients—the charge‑order template and the time‑reversal‑breaking currents—are necessary to yield the chiral fingerprint. The impurity thus acts as a local probe that converts the global broken symmetries into a visible, measurable pattern, much as a pebble dropped into a river can reveal hidden currents by the shape of the ripple it creates. Unlike a pebble, however, the impurity does not simply display an existing flow; its precise location in the enlarged unit cell selects which aspects of the hidden order become visible. This is not will, but a consequence of how spatial symmetry breaking in the surrounding state leaves some mirror planes intact and destroys others.

What emerges from the preprint is a coherent picture of how multiple broken symmetries conspire to produce what an STM tip sees. The loop‑current phase supplies the necessary time‑reversal breaking. The star‑of‑David charge‑density wave provides the enlarged unit cell with inequivalent impurity sites. The Z₃ nematicity, which picks one of three possible current orientations, sets the axis along which the chirality will manifest. And the magnetic field, acting through the orbital magnetisation, gives experimentalists a knob to rotate that axis, switching the handedness of the QPI signal at will. The framework does not require large impurity concentrations or exotic new forces; a single atom, appropriately placed, is enough to read out the state.

For researchers trying to understand the cascade of electronic phases in kagome metals, this work offers two immediate practical directions. First, it suggests a direct experimental test: if one could controllably place single impurities at the A, B, and C sublattice sites—perhaps using atomic‑scale manipulation techniques—then scanning‑tunnelling spectroscopy should reveal the predicted site‑dependent chiralities. Spotting a negative signal on an A‑site and a positive on a B‑site, while C‑sites remain neutral, would be a crisp confirmation of the theory. Second, the predicted magnetic‑field switching mechanism can be probed by combining STM with small, in‑situ magnetic fields to track the sign reversal of the chiral QPI as the nematic director rotates. Both experiments are within the reach of current technology, and they would transform the loop‑current phase from an intriguing theoretical idea into a phenomenon whose internal structure can be mapped with near‑atomic detail.

For theorists who have been puzzling over the chiral signals in kagome metals, Nakazawa and his colleagues have finally provided a concrete link between the abstract loop‑current chirality and the concrete QPI patterns. The work clarifies why the overall loop‑current orientation alone is not sufficient to predict the handedness seen in STM, and it reveals the crucial role played by the charge‑density‑wave template and the impurity site. The road ahead is not about hunting for a single, bulk‑wide chirality but about learning to read the local, site‑by‑site variations that together give the loop‑current state its unique and switchable signature.
