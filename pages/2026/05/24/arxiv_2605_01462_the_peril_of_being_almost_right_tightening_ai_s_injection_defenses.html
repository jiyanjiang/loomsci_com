The Peril of Being Almost Right: Tightening AI's Injection Defenses

Drafted May 05, 2026 · Published May 24, 2026 Artificial Intelligence Source: arXiv:2605.01462
LocalAlign defends language models by training against near-correct prompt injections, tightening the boundary to stop attacks that barely miss.

Imagine you are the bouncer at an exclusive club. Your job is simple: let in the members, keep out the gatecrashers. But what if a gatecrasher arrives wearing an almost-perfect copy of a member's jacket — the same cut, same color, same fabric, just a slightly different label stitched inside? Suddenly your job is no longer simple. Unlike a human bouncer, however, a language model does not have the luxury of intuition — it relies entirely on statistical patterns learned from data, which makes the impostor far harder to detect.

This is the problem facing large language models (LLMs) that are embedded into systems interacting with user data, retrieved web content, and external tools. These systems must follow a trusted instruction — say, "summarize this email" — while simultaneously ingesting untrusted data that might contain malicious commands. The attacker's goal: slip a hidden command into the data that the model mistakenly treats as the primary instruction. It is called prompt injection, and it is the security community's newest headache.

Now, a team led by Yuyang Gong at Wuhan University, with collaborators at Nanyang Technological University, has proposed a defense that addresses the most insidious form of this attack. Their work, which appears in a preprint (arXiv:2605.01462), is built on a surprisingly simple observation: the most dangerous injected commands are not the ones that scream for something ridiculous, but the ones that whisper for something almost correct.

The existing state-of-the-art defense, exemplified by a method called SecAlign, works by fine-tuning the model to explicitly preserve a boundary between the trusted "command" field and the untrusted "data" field. The model learns to prioritize the trusted instruction and ignore malicious content in the data. It is a reasonable approach, and it works well against attacks that ask for something blatantly wrong — like requesting an obviously incorrect number.

But here is the catch: real-world attackers are smarter. Instead of injecting a command that produces a clearly wrong response, they craft one that nudges the model toward a response that is very close to the correct one — just off by a hair's breadth. Existing defenses, trained only on a hand-crafted set of obviously wrong targets, leave a wide boundary around the correct response. Think of it as a fence around a fortress: if the fence is set far away, a clever intruder can squeeze through by staying close to the fortress walls. This is not a weakness of the fence, but a geometric fact about how far the defenders have chosen to stand from what they protect.

When Close Is More Dangerous Than Far

Gong and colleagues call their approach LocalAlign, and it is inspired by adversarial training — the same technique used to make neural networks robust against small, carefully crafted perturbations. The core idea is straightforward: instead of training against targets that are semantically distant from the correct response, generate adversarial examples in which the injected command induces a response that stays near to the correct response while still being wrong.

To understand why this matters, consider a concrete example. A model is asked to evaluate a statement: "Is the sky blue? Answer True or False." The correct response is "True." A standard attack might try to make the model say "98" — a clearly wrong answer. The existing defense learns to reject such blatant injections. But a more subtle attacker might try to make the model say "False" — wrong, but contextually close. That is far harder to defend against, because any reasonable model trained on general knowledge already knows that "False" is a plausible response for many statements. The fence around "True" needs to be tight enough to exclude "False" while still allowing it for other questions.

Using near-but-wrong targets, like "False" instead of "True," tightens training so models reject injected commands more accurately. This makes the defense resilient against both optimized adversarial suffixes and data-specific target attacks. (Source: arXiv:2605.01462)

The team's first step was to confirm that this intuition holds across a large dataset. On the AlpacaFarm benchmark, they measured the relationship between the distance of an attacker target from the correct response and the likelihood that the target would be generated. The data revealed a clear negative correlation: attacker targets with smaller distance to the correct response tend to have higher generation likelihood. This means that the most dangerous attacks are precisely the ones that existing defenses are least prepared for — the near misses.

Attacker responses that closely mimic correct answers are more likely to be generated by the model. This finding drives a new training approach that uses near-target adversarial examples to improve defense against prompt injections. (Source: arXiv:2605.01462)

How to Build a Tighter Boundary

The technical heart of LocalAlign is a method for automatically generating near-but-wrong adversarial examples. Remarkably, the team found that this can be done using a simple prompt and a single inference step — no expensive optimization, no gradient calculations, no iterative search. They simply ask the model to generate a plausible incorrect answer that is close to the correct one. This design makes the training process efficient and broadly scalable.

But there is a complication: the quality of these generated examples varies substantially across samples. Some are so close to the correct response that they are nearly indistinguishable; others are a bit further off. Treating all examples equally would dilute the training signal. To address this, the team introduced a margin-aware alignment algorithm that quantifies each sample's distance to the correct response and assigns larger training weight to the nearest ones. This ensures that the model pays the most attention to the attacks that are hardest to spot — the ones that sit just outside the boundary.

The results speak for themselves. When tested against GCG, a gradient-based attack that optimizes adversarial suffixes across many steps, LocalAlign proved substantially harder to crack. Even after extensive optimization, the attack loss for LocalAlign remained higher than the initial attack loss of the SecAlign defense. In practical terms, this means an attacker would need far more effort to find a successful injection — and in many cases, the attack simply fails.

The defense also generalizes to target-side unseen attacks, where the attacker's goal is data-specific, such as steering a recommendation from "Product 1" to "Product 3." Here too, LocalAlign consistently outperformed existing defenses, suggesting that the tight-boundary approach captures something fundamental about the geometry of prompt injection.

What This Means for AI Safety

The philosophical implications are worth pausing over. Security in AI systems is often conceived as a binary gate: a command is either trusted or not. This work suggests that such binary thinking is insufficient. The real battlefield is not the yes-or-no of injection detection, but the continuous geometry of the model's response space. Defending a single point — the correct response — requires erecting a barrier in the surrounding landscape, and the shape of that barrier matters enormously.

It also highlights a deeper truth about adversarial examples: they thrive not in the far reaches of possibility, but in the gray zones of plausibility. An attacker does not need to make the model do something completely unreasonable; they only need to nudge it slightly in the wrong direction. The most powerful defense, then, is one that polices those small nudges with extreme vigilance.

LocalAlign does not claim to have solved prompt injection. The team acknowledges that the quality of near-target adversarial examples can vary, and not all attack vectors are equally covered. Moreover, as models grow larger and more capable, attackers will develop new techniques that may evade this defense as well. But the direction is now clear: instead of building a moat far from the castle, we should build a wall that hugs the castle walls. The geometry of trust matters as much as the walls themselves.

In a world where AI systems increasingly make decisions about what we see, read, and buy, getting the boundary right is not just a technical challenge — it is a social one. LocalAlign offers a principled framework for thinking about where to draw the line.
