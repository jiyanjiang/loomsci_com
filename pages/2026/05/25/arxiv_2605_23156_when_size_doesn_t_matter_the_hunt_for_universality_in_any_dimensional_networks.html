When Size Doesn't Matter: The Hunt for Universality in Any-Dimensional Networks

May 25, 2026 Artificial Intelligence Source: arXiv:2605.23156

Try this thought experiment. You are teaching a machine to recognise molecules. Some have five atoms, some have five hundred. The machine must treat all of them equally, finding patterns that survive regardless of how much stuff is in the input. Now imagine that the machine not only works for any number of atoms, but that its inner structure is proven to be capable of representing any sensible chemical rule — a true universal approximator. That would be like a musical instrument that can play any melody, no matter how long or short, without the musician needing to retune. This is the aspiration of any-dimensional universality, and a new preprint (arXiv:2605.23156) from Shengtai Yao, Eitan Levin, and Mateo Díaz proposes a mathematical framework to make it precise.

Neural networks on the edge of dimension. A classic result in deep learning says that a sufficiently deep or wide network can approximate any continuous function on a fixed, compact input domain — a statement about finite dimensions. But many real-world problems are not fixed: social networks grow, point clouds can have arbitrary numbers of points, and graphs refuse to sit still. An “any-dimensional” model is one that accepts inputs of any size — sequences, sets, graphs — and respects the symmetries that should hold irrespective of how many atoms, nodes, or pixels arrive. The catch? Traditional universal approximation theorems were never written for this variable-size regime. They assume a compact subset of a fixed-dimensional space. When the dimension itself is a variable, what does “universal” even mean?

The limit that contains everything. Yao and colleagues’ central idea is to embed all inputs of all finite sizes into a single infinite-dimensional limit space — a mathematical universe where every finite input, and every conceivable limit of growing inputs, lives under one roof. It is a bit like looking at a sequence of photographs with ever more pixels; the limit is the perfectly sharp image, even though no finite camera ever captures it. Formally, they identify an any-dimensional function — a family of functions defined on sets of growing size — with a single, well-behaved function on this limit space. The limit space carries a natural topology, and it contains rich families of compact sets: the objects over which universal approximation can be stated and proved.

What makes this construction especially elegant is how it handles permutation invariance — the requirement that the order of the input items doesn’t matter. By building the limit space out of probability measures — a formal way of saying “a cloud of points without a list” — the symmetry is baked into the geometry itself. The network only has to be continuous on this space of measures; permutation invariance follows for free.

What the framework reveals — and what it hides. Armed with this new lens, the team then examines several existing any-dimensional architectures and finds that, surprisingly, many of them fail to be universal. They lack the expressive power to approximate all continuous invariant functions on the limit space. However, the paper does not simply diagnose failure; it proposes simple modifications that restore universality. The message is both cautionary and constructive: the tools exist to design networks that truly scale across dimension, but they require a more careful mathematical foundation than was previously assumed.

An important question raised by earlier universal approximation work — such as the 2019 studies by Sannai et al. and by Segol and Lipman et al. — is whether standard fixed-size universality claims, with their reliance on specific activation functions and depth-width tradeoffs, actually cover the kinds of compact sets that appear in practice. Yao and colleagues’ limit-space approach sharply reframes this question. Their results uncover new, broader families of compact sets on which universality can be established — but the preprint does not yet provide concrete, intuitive examples of sets that fall outside the reach of finite-dimensional theorems. What do these genuinely new compact sets look like? A nervous experimentalist would want to see one before trusting the framework.

Another natural question, sharpened by the earlier work of Maehara and colleagues, concerns the admissible activation functions. For universality in the limit-space topology, which activation functions — besides standard ReLU — produce universal networks? The paper leaves this largely unspecified. This isn’t a fatal gap; it is the kind of boundary that every new framework must eventually map. But it reminds us that the limit space, while powerful, is not yet a fully paved road.

The hunt beyond the fixed. Viewed through the lens of the known universality results, this work is less a final answer than an invitation. It says: if you want a network to truly handle inputs of any size, you must stop thinking in terms of padding, binning, or resizing. You must think in terms of convergence to a limit, and you must design architectures that are continuous in that infinite-dimensional sense. This is not a small shift; it reorients the entire enterprise of invariant deep learning.

That philosophical turn is the deepest contribution of the paper. Universality, in the any-dimensional setting, ceases to be a property of a network's width or depth alone. It becomes a statement about how sequences of finite data cohere into a single mathematical object — a measure, a distribution, a limit — and how a neural network can learn to operate on that object directly.

The tension between the grand vision and the unfinished details — missing concrete examples, unspecified activation functions — is productive, not discouraging. It suggests that the field is opening a door onto a richer landscape. Perhaps one day, when a chemist feeds a molecule of any size into a neural network and gets back a reliable prediction of its properties, we will look back and say that the map was first drawn here, in a preprint that dared to build a limit space for the limitless.
