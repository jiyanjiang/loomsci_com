Learning to Hand Off: A Provably Convergent Theory for Multi-Agent Workflows

May 20, 2026 Artificial Intelligence Source: arXiv:2605.19140

What would it take for a fleet of specialized AI agents — one that reasons about mathematics, another that writes code, a third that verifies logical correctness — to hand tasks back and forth without anyone watching the whole conversation? Not just to do it, but to do it with a guarantee that the handoffs will eventually converge on something useful? This is not a question from a philosophy seminar. It is the practical reality of modern multi-agent AI pipelines, and until now, it has lacked a rigorous theoretical foundation.

A preprint (arXiv:2605.19140) from Jiayu Li, Enpei Zhang, Dawei Zhou, Elynn Chen, and Yujun Yan proposes exactly that foundation. The researchers introduce what they call an interface-constrained semi-Markov decision process — a mouthful of a name that captures something deceptively simple: when agents hand off control through a shared artifact, and each agent can see only its own slice of the world, how do you learn a coordination strategy that provably works?

The question matters because it describes the operating conditions of nearly every ambitious multi-LLM system being built today. A reasoning agent passes a partial proof to a verification agent. The verifier hands back a flagged inconsistency. The reasoner revises and tries again. A code-generation agent receives a specification, produces a candidate program, and passes it to a testing agent. The tester runs it, finds a failure, and sends the error trace back. Nobody — no central coordinator, no omniscient overseer — ever sees the full joint trajectory. Each agent sees only its own local observations and the artifact changing hands.

This regime, the researchers argue, is not an edge case. It is the default when AI workflows cross organizational boundaries, vendor platforms, or trust domains. And it demands a learning theory that takes interface constraints seriously.

The Problem Nobody Named

Consider what happens in a multi-agent mathematical reasoning pipeline. One language model generates a candidate proof step. Another checks it against the axioms. The artifact — the growing proof — passes back and forth. Each handoff is a decision epoch: the reasoning agent decides what to add, the verification agent decides whether to accept or reject. Between handoffs, each agent does whatever internal computation it needs. But the coordination — the decision about when and how to hand off — is where the learning must happen.

The difficulty is that from the perspective of any single agent, the world looks partially observable. The reasoner does not see the verifier's internal deliberation, only the verdict. The verifier does not see how the reasoner arrived at a particular step, only the output. And yet, somehow, the pair must converge on a workflow — a pattern of handoffs — that reliably produces correct proofs.

Li and colleagues formalize this as an interface-constrained semi-Markov decision process. The "semi-Markov" part matters: decisions happen not at fixed time steps, but at handoff events. The "interface-constrained" part is the critical innovation: each agent observes only a local function of the shared artifact, plus its own private state. Think of it like two chefs sharing a single pot of soup. One chef — call her the salt chef — can taste the soup and add salt, but cannot see the spice rack. The other — the spice chef — can see the spice rack and add spices, but cannot taste salt. They hand the pot back and forth. Each decision to hand off is a moment of trust: "I've done what I can with what I know; your turn."

But here the analogy meets its limit. Unlike chefs sharing a physical pot, the agents in this framework do not even share a common language for describing the artifact's full state. The artifact — a proof, a program, a routing decision — may be high-dimensional and complex, but what crosses the interface is drastically compressed: a verdict, a score, a suggested revision. The compression is not a bug. It is the entire point. Interface constraints are what make multi-agent systems scalable, modular, and privacy-preserving. The question is whether they also make them learnable with guarantees.

The Number That Crosses the Boundary

The researchers' key move is startlingly economical. Instead of trying to learn a joint policy that coordinates all agents simultaneously — which would require sharing trajectory data across interfaces, violating the very constraints they set out to respect — they design an algorithm called IC-Q in which cross-agent coordination at every handoff is exactly one scalar.

One number. At the moment of handoff, each agent communicates a single value. That value, a learned Q-function estimate, encapsulates everything that agent believes about the long-term value of the current state and proposed next action. The receiving agent uses that scalar to update its own estimates and decide what to do next. The algorithm is asynchronous, decentralized, and requires no agent to observe another agent's internal state or historical trajectory.

The elegance of this solution is not merely aesthetic. It is what makes the theoretical guarantees possible. Because the coordination signal is a single scalar, the error sources can be cleanly decomposed — and that decomposition is the heart of what makes this work novel.

The finite-sample bound that Li and colleagues prove decomposes the total error into three independently controllable sources. First, there is the neural function-approximation error: how well can each agent's Q-network represent the true value function? This is the familiar supervised learning problem, and it improves with better architectures and more data. Second, there is the interface representation gap: how much information is lost when the artifact's rich internal state is compressed into whatever the other agent can observe? This is the fundamentally new quantity — it measures not how well one agent learns, but how much the interface itself constrains what can be learned. Third, there is a mixing-time residual: how quickly does the Markov chain induced by the agents' behavior policy reach its stationary distribution? This captures the cost of temporal correlation in the data.

Each of these error terms scales along its own axis, meaning a practitioner can in principle diagnose whether the bottleneck is poor function approximation, a lossy interface, or insufficient exploration. The researchers validate this decomposition term-by-term in a controlled synthetic experiment, showing that when each error source is varied independently, the total error tracks the theory's predictions.

What Earlier Work Left Unasked

An important question raised by earlier work on multi-agent orchestration is whether the assumptions underlying provable guarantees can survive contact with realistic interface design. The paper by Gottweis and colleagues on AI co-scientist systems (arXiv:2502.18864) demonstrated empirically that multi-agent LLM workflows can achieve impressive results in scientific discovery — but that work, like most in the field, relied on heuristically designed handoff protocols with no convergence theory. The gap between what works in practice and what can be guaranteed in theory has been wide.

Li and colleagues' framework begins to close that gap, but it also surfaces tensions that honest readers will notice. The interface representation gap — the quantity that measures how much coordination-relevant information is destroyed by the interface compression — is not constructively bounded for realistic high-dimensional settings. The proof assumes such a bound exists, but how to compute it for a given multi-LLM pipeline is an open problem. This is not a failure of the theory so much as an honest acknowledgment of where theory meets engineering. The bound tells you what quantities matter; it does not tell you how to measure them in every case.

A related question arises from work by Chen and colleagues on high-dimensional transfer learning under latent heterogeneity. That work showed that when latent structures are shared across tasks but not directly observed, learning is possible — but the guarantees depend sensitively on how well the latent structure can be inferred from partial observations. In the IC-SMDP setting, the latent structure is the full state of the shared artifact, and what each agent observes is a compressed projection. The quality of that projection determines everything. If the compression is too aggressive — if the interface throws away information that turns out to be coordination-relevant — no amount of Q-learning can recover it. The theory quantifies this, but the designer must still choose the interface representation wisely.

Zhang and colleagues, in their work on cross-market transfer pricing (arXiv:2505.17203), examined a different kind of interface constraint: the mismatch between data distributions in different markets. Their minimax framework provides guarantees that hold even when the target environment differs from the source, by explicitly modeling the distribution shift. Li and colleagues' work shares this philosophical commitment to bounded rationality — to providing guarantees that do not require perfect information or stationary environments. But the IC-Q framework currently assumes the induced Markov chain is stationary, an assumption that may be violated in deployments where user behavior, data distributions, or agent capabilities drift over time.

These questions are not criticisms so much as invitations. The framework is precise enough that its limitations are visible, which is a virtue in theoretical work. A theory that hides its assumptions behind impenetrable formalism is less useful than one that states them clearly and invites improvement.

The Cathedral That Will Not Be Built in a Day

The cathedral of provably reliable multi-agent AI will not be built in a single paper. Li and colleagues have supplied something rarer than an engineering milestone: a language for thinking clearly about what it means for agents to coordinate under partial observability with finite-sample guarantees. The error decomposition — approximation, representation gap, mixing time — gives researchers and practitioners a checklist. When a multi-agent system fails to converge, the question is no longer "why doesn't this work?" but "which term in the bound is dominating?"

This is the power of theory done well. It does not promise to solve every practical problem. It promises to make the problems legible. And legibility is the precondition for progress.

The researchers demonstrated their framework on four tasks: a controlled synthetic environment that validates the bound term by term, multi-LLM mathematical reasoning, multi-agent routing, and multi-agent CPU programming. In each case, IC-Q matched the performance of a centralized oracle — a learner that could observe the full joint trajectory — without any agent ever seeing another agent's private observations. The single scalar crossing the interface at each handoff was sufficient.

What this work ultimately shows us is that the constraint we thought was the problem — the interface, the privacy boundary, the organizational firewall — may not be an obstacle to provable learning. It may be precisely the structure that makes the learning problem tractable, by forcing it to decompose into pieces that can be analyzed and guaranteed independently. The researchers have not solved every problem in multi-agent orchestration. They have done something more foundational: they have written the first chapter of a theory that makes the remaining problems precise enough to solve.
