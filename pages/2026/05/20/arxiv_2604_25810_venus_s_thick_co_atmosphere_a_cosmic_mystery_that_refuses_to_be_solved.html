Venus's Thick CO₂ Atmosphere: A Cosmic Mystery That Refuses to Be Solved

Drafted April 29, 2026 · Published May 20, 2026 Cosmos Source: arXiv:2604.25810

What if the most obvious clue about a planet's history is actually a lie? For decades, planetary scientists have looked at Venus and seen a cautionary tale: a world that once resembled Earth, with oceans and a temperate climate, before a runaway greenhouse effect boiled its surface and left behind a choking atmosphere of 90 bar of CO₂. The story is clean, intuitive, and almost certainly incomplete.

A preprint (arXiv:2604.25810) from Tereza Constantinou, Oliver Shorttle, and Harrison Nicholls at the University of Cambridge challenges this narrative at its foundation. Their work suggests that Venus's massive CO₂ atmosphere—the thing that seems most diagnostic of a catastrophic past—may be entirely indifferent to whether the planet ever had oceans at all. It is, in the language of dynamical systems, an equifinal outcome: many different histories, same destination.

The paper is a reminder that in planetary science, as in detective work, the most obvious suspect is rarely the guilty one.

The Earth-Venus Paradox

Earth and Venus are almost the same size, almost the same mass, almost the same distance from the Sun. And yet one world teems with life, oceans, and a carbon cycle that has kept its climate stable for billions of years; the other is a hellscape of 460°C surface temperatures and sulfuric acid clouds. The carbon inventories of the two planets are comparable—but the carbon is distributed in opposite ways.

Earth locks its carbon in the crust as carbonate rocks: limestone, dolomite, the white cliffs of Dover. Venus holds its carbon in the atmosphere as CO₂. The difference is so stark that it seems to demand an explanation involving liquid water, plate tectonics, and biological activity—the very things that regulate Earth's carbon cycle and that Venus conspicuously lacks.

But what if the absence of those processes doesn't tell us what we think it tells us?

Three Roads to Hell

The Cambridge team tested three possible pathways to a Venus-like CO₂ atmosphere, using a coupled climate-weathering framework that tracks carbon through the planet's entire system. The first pathway is the classic story: a primary atmosphere outgassed from a global magma ocean early in Venus's history. The second is secondary volcanic degassing over billions of years, in a planet with no plate tectonics (a "stagnant-lid" regime). The third is the most dramatic: a formerly temperate Venus that stored carbon in its crust, then released it catastrophically after a runaway greenhouse transition.

Each pathway, they found, can produce a Venus-like atmosphere. None is uniquely required.

Volcanic outgassing produces a wide range of possible Venus-like atmospheres, with or without the recycling of gases into the planet's interior. This matters because it shows that multiple different starting conditions can lead to similar thick, carbon-dioxide-rich atmospheres, complicating efforts to reconstruct Venus's history. (Source: arXiv:2604.25810)

The primary magma-ocean pathway is the most violent. As this global ocean of molten rock cooled and crystallized, it released enormous quantities of CO₂ and other volatiles. The team's models show that this process can generate more than a hundred bar of CO₂—easily enough to match Venus's current atmosphere. But the fraction retained after early atmospheric escape remains uncertain. A planet that lost its early atmosphere to hydrodynamic escape might end up with very little CO₂; one that retained it might end up with far too much.

The secondary degassing pathway is more gradual. In a stagnant-lid regime, volcanoes punch through a stationary crust, releasing CO₂ from the mantle over billions of years. With a mantle composition similar to Earth's depleted mid-ocean ridge basalts (MORB), this process produces only about 25 bar of CO₂—far short of Venus's 90 bar. But the team identified three ways to close the gap: a mantle enriched in carbon, higher rates of magmatic delivery to the surface, and the recycling of undegassed carbon back into the planet's interior. Each of these is plausible for a planet that evolved differently from Earth.

The third pathway is the one that carries the most narrative weight, and perhaps the most uncertainty.

The Habitable Venus That Never Was

Imagine an early Venus with liquid water oceans, a temperate climate, and a functioning silicate weathering cycle. Earth's carbon cycle is built on this kind of system: atmospheric CO₂ dissolves in rainwater, reacts with silicate rocks, and is deposited as carbonate minerals on the seafloor. The process is self-regulating—a kind of planetary thermostat that has kept Earth habitable for billions of years.

The Cambridge team modeled this hypothetical Venus, receiving 75% of present-day solar luminosity (appropriate for early Venus), and found that silicate weathering could have stored about 20 bar of CO₂ as crustal carbonates over hundreds of millions of years. That's a substantial reservoir—roughly a quarter of Venus's current atmosphere—but it's far from the full 90 bar.

Then came the runaway. As the Sun brightened, surface temperatures rose. When they crossed the boiling point of water—around 100°C at Venus's surface pressure—the oceans evaporated, the weathering cycle collapsed, and the planet entered a positive feedback loop that drove surface temperatures to hundreds of degrees.

This is where the story gets interesting. Once the surface temperature reached about 460°C, the crustal carbonates that had been stable for hundreds of millions of years began to decompose. The team found that within tens of millions of years—a geological blink—nearly all of the stored CO₂ was released back into the atmosphere. The reservoir that took hundreds of millions of years to build was emptied in geological moments.

But even this dramatic release, combined with ongoing volcanic degassing, only brought the atmosphere to about 25–30 bar of CO₂. To reach Venus's current 90 bar, the planet would have needed additional carbon from other sources—perhaps from a more carbon-rich mantle, or from the recycling of carbonates into the interior through processes we don't fully understand.

The Equifinality Principle

The central insight of the paper—and the source of its provocative power—is that a Venus-like CO₂ atmosphere does not uniquely diagnose a temperate past. A planet that was never habitable, that never had liquid water on its surface, could still end up with a thick CO₂ atmosphere through magma-ocean outgassing and volcanic degassing alone. Conversely, a planet that was once temperate and Earth-like could produce a thick CO₂ atmosphere through carbonate decomposition, but only if other conditions (mantle carbon content, volcanic activity, recycling) were also favorable.

This is equifinality in action: the same final state reached through different initial conditions and different evolutionary pathways. The atmosphere of Venus, in this view, is not a historical document that reveals its past to anyone who can read it. It is a palimpsest, written and rewritten so many times that the original text is lost.

The team's work does not prove that Venus was never habitable. It does not prove that Venus was always a hellscape. What it does is more subtle and more important: it shows that we cannot use the current atmosphere as evidence for either scenario. The CO₂ blanket is consistent with both a temperate past and a perpetually hostile one.

The Deeper Question

This is not merely a technical debate about planetary carbon cycles. It raises a deeper question about how we interpret planetary data: when we look at a world and see a signal that seems to tell a clear story, how do we know that story is true? The problem is not unique to Venus. It applies to exoplanets, where we have even less information—just a transit depth, a spectral feature, a calculated density. Every exoplanet atmosphere we measure is an equifinal outcome, consistent with many different histories.

The Cambridge team's approach offers a kind of philosophical humility dressed as geophysical modeling. They do not claim to have solved the mystery of Venus. Instead, they have shown that the mystery is deeper than we thought. The question is no longer "Was Venus once habitable?" but rather "What evidence could possibly answer that question?" The atmosphere alone cannot tell us. We need something else—perhaps isotopic ratios in Venus's atmosphere, perhaps samples from its crust, perhaps a better understanding of how planets evolve over billions of years.

The paper ends with a quiet provocation. If Venus's CO₂ atmosphere is equifinal, then the search for Earth-like exoplanets faces a similar problem. A planet with a thick CO₂ atmosphere could be a failed Earth, a never-Earth, or a post-Earth. The spectrum tells us the composition, but not the history. We are left not with answers, but with better questions—and in science, that is often the most valuable discovery of all.
