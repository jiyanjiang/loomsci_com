When a Current Pulse Teaches a Magnet to Forget

Drafted May 08, 2026 · Published May 20, 2026 Matter Science Source: arXiv:2605.06645

The best way to make a magnet remember something is, paradoxically, to make it forget. This is the central tension of magnetic recording, a technology that has spent half a century chasing ever smaller bits while wrestling with a stubborn physical trilemma: you cannot simultaneously have a strong, stable signal, a bit that resists spontaneous flipping, and a weak writing field that can still nudge it over. Optimize one, and you sabotage another. Shrink the bit, and the thermal jostling of the lattice becomes a liability—thermal stability demands a deep energy well, but writing into that well then requires a correspondingly large magnetic punch. The trilemma, in the concise language of storage engineers, is inescapable.

For years, the most elegant escape route has been Heat Assisted Magnetic Recording (HAMR). The idea is simple: during the write cycle, briefly heat the recording medium above its Curie temperature—the point where its ferromagnetism collapses—so that a modest magnetic field can steer the bit. Once the medium cools, its coercivity roars back, locking the data in place for years. It works, but the engineering is bulky: HAMR relies on laser optics and plasmonic antennas to confine light into hot spots smaller than the wavelength itself. That optical infrastructure is ill-suited for on-chip or embedded architectures, where space, power, and simplicity matter more than anything else.

Now a team led by Hossein Taghinejad at the University of California, Berkeley, has demonstrated a purely electronic version of HAMR—no lasers, no plasmonics, just a pulse of current through a two-dimensional magnet. Their work appears in a preprint (arXiv:2605.06645), and it opens a door to memory devices that can be written with nothing more than a CMOS‑compatible electrical signal.

The material at the heart of their study is an intercalated 2D magnet, Ni₁/₄TaSe₂—nickel atoms wedged between layers of tantalum diselenide. The team patterned a thin crystal into a Hall bar and measured its anomalous Hall resistivity, a signal that directly reports the magnetization direction. The device sits in a tiny external magnetic field of just 2 millitesla, roughly a hundred times weaker than the coercive field normally needed to flip the bit. Then they inject a short, dense current pulse—around a hundred thousand amperes per square centimeter, a density that, in ordinary wires, would mean glowing heat, but here it lasts only a millisecond. The pulse is not meant to twist the magnetization through spin‑torques; it is meant to act as a transient furnace.

Think of a padlock whose shackle refuses to turn unless the whole mechanism is briefly softened. You force the bolt, but the heat threatens to melt not just the lock but half the door. That is the trilemma’s harsh arithmetic. HAMR’s insight was to apply heat in a surgically precise spot, and the Berkeley team’s twist is to generate that heat electrically, right inside the magnetic layer itself. Unlike a physical lock, where melting would destroy it, the magnetism here is not a material that deforms; it is a collective dance of electron spins, and heating above the Curie temperature simply suspends that dance long enough for a whisper of a field to change the choreography.

The team’s evidence that Joule heating is the driver is subtle and multilayered. They track how the longitudinal resistance of the device shifts as they ramp up the DC current, and they see that the apparent Curie temperature—the point where magnetism vanishes—drops with increasing current density, and the magnitude of this downward shift scales quadratically with the current. That quadratic scaling is the fingerprint of Joule heating, where power dissipated as heat grows as the square of the current. In plain terms, the sample gets hotter, and when it crosses its equilibrium Curie temperature, the magnetic order liquifies. At that moment, the 2 mT field—a field you could nearly cancel with the Earth’s own magnetism—quietly dictates the final spin orientation.

What makes this all‑electronic scheme particularly intriguing is its apparent indifference to the current’s direction. Reversing the pulse polarity does not alter the switching, a behavior strongly at odds with spin‑orbit torque mechanisms, which would flip the magnetization for one polarity but not the other. The team explicitly checked: the Hall resistivity after a pulse of opposite sign follows the same field‑driven pattern. This simplicity hints at a fundamentally thermal, rather than torque‑driven, switching process.

Yet an important question lingers, one that earlier work on all‑electrical magnetization control has sharpened. A separate team recently demonstrated self‑switching in a van der Waals chiral antiferromagnet, using electrical pulses that generated internal spin‑orbit torques strong enough to reverse the order without heating above the transition. That demonstration shows that current‑induced torques can, in carefully chosen materials, perform magnetization reversal without the crutch of a thermal cycle. Against that backdrop, the Berkeley group’s experiment, while clean and reproducible, cannot fully exclude a torque contribution because they did not perform the most decisive control: applying a current pulse in zero external field and comparing switches for opposite polarities. In their measurements, a small field was always present, so a residual torque aligned with that field could conceivably play a role.

The authors are aware of this ambiguity. They point to the polarity independence and the absence of an asymmetric loop shift—hallmarks of spin‑orbit torque—as suggestive evidence. But the dialogue between these two recent preprints highlights a fascinating tension: when a current runs through a magnetic 2D crystal, it can heat, it can torque, and often it does both. Disentangling these effects will require direct nanoscale thermometry and systematic torque‑exclusion experiments, work that is now a natural next step.

Still, the promise of an all‑electronic HAMR scheme is too compelling to dismiss. The intercalated 2D magnet used here is not a one‑off curiosity; it belongs to a huge family of layered materials whose magnetic properties can be tuned by intercalating different atoms. The writing energy, delivered through simple current pulses, is modest, and the readout uses the anomalous Hall effect—an electronic signal that fits naturally into integrated circuits. In a world where data centres consume an ever‑growing fraction of global electricity, any memory technology that can be written with less power and packaged into dense, on‑chip arrays deserves attention.

What this research ultimately shows us is that the trilemma of magnetic recording is not a hard wall but a negotiable frontier. Heat, when applied with enough precision, can translate the stubbornness of a magnet into its greatest strength: a high coercivity that guards the written bit during storage, yet collapses into docility for the fleeting instant when a new bit must be burned in. The magnet, in that brief thermal window, forgets its past—and that forgetfulness is precisely how it learns a new memory. The road ahead is not about proving that this works in a single 2D flake; it is about understanding what else, besides pure heat, the current may be whispering into the spin system. For now, the Berkeley experiment leaves us with an elegant possibility and a sharper set of questions—and in science, that is often the most valuable gift a paper can give.
