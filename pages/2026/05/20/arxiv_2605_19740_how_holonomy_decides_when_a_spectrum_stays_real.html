How Holonomy Decides When a Spectrum Stays Real

May 20, 2026 Matter Science Source: arXiv:2605.19740

Imagine a city built of interconnected plazas linked by one-way gates. Standing in any plaza, you can move to a neighbour, but the direction you can travel is fixed, like a turnstile that only swings in. After wandering through the city, you return to where you started—but now you’ve accumulated something invisible: a twist, a subtle shift that doesn’t register as a distance but as a remnant of the route you took. If that twist vanishes after a full loop, the city is flat, geometrically unburdened. If it doesn’t, the city has a hidden wind—a curvature that refuses to unwind.

In a preprint (arXiv:2605.19740) released this month, a team led by Emil J. Bergholtz at Stockholm University, with Jiong-Hao Wang, Maria Zelenayova, and Christopher Ekman, shows that exactly this kind of twist—a holonomy in Hilbert space—acts as a gatekeeper for whether the energy spectrum of a quantum many-body system stays real or spills into the complex plane. Their discovery is both geometric and unexpectedly simple: in certain non-Hermitian lattice models, the reality of the energies depends not on symmetry alone, but on a global winding of the state space, and that winding flips dramatically with a single change in the number of particles or the orientation of a single spin.

This is a story about when a many-body system “decides” to show its non-Hermitian nature. Non-Hermitian physics, where a system can exchange energy with its environment, has surged in recent years because it describes open systems, from driven atomic ensembles to classical networks of springs and friction. In such frameworks, the energy eigenvalues need not be real; imaginary parts encode gain or loss. Yet a remarkable finding has been that many non-Hermitian models, under the right conditions, can still have entirely real spectra, mimicking a closed, Hermitian world. The question is: what selects reality?

The Stockholm team answers this with a geometric criterion rooted in the Krylov graph. To build intuition, they work with two minimal many-body models: a fermion chain with non-reciprocal hopping, and a spin chain with analogous interactions. Both are fragmented—meaning the Hilbert space breaks into dynamically disconnected sectors, a form of ergodicity breaking known as Hilbert-space fragmentation. Within this fractured landscape, they focus on sectors defined by total particle number (for fermions) or total magnetization (for spins).

What they find is stark. For the fermion model, when the number of electrons precisely matches the number of lattice sites—what is conventionally called half-filling—the spectrum becomes complex as soon as the non-Hermitian parameter g is turned on. The eigenvalues wander off the real axis, signalling amplification and damping. But remove a single electron, or add one, and the entire spectrum snaps back to real, even though the periodic boundary conditions and the hopping asymmetry remain unchanged. The same holds for the spin chain: at zero net magnetization, the spectrum is complex; flip one spin away from this balanced configuration, and the spectrum becomes entirely real. The sensitivity is extreme: a single-particle or single-spin shift toggles reality on and off.

Adding or removing a single particle dramatically reshapes the energy spectrum, while some states stay unchanged. This sensitivity reveals a geometric structure in the system’s quantum states, key to understanding its exotic behavior. (Source: arXiv:2605.19740)

This behaviour immediately echoes the non-Hermitian skin effect, where a system’s eigenstates pile up at a boundary under open boundaries yet exhibit real spectra under periodic ones. But here, the boundary condition is unchanged; only the filling changes. So the team looks deeper into the geometry of the Hilbert space.

The Graphs That Know

The key is to view the non-Hermitian hopping as a discrete gauge field on the Krylov graph—the graph whose vertices are Fock states (configurations of particles) and whose directed edges represent the action of hopping operators. Each hopping term carries a complex phase; after completing a closed loop in this graph, a state picks up a net phase. This is the holonomy: a cumulative winding that acts like an Aharonov-Bohm flux threading a ring of basis states.

Closed loops of state-changing operations can accumulate a non-zero "twist," revealing a hidden geometric phase. This twist signals spectral complexity in systems with gain and loss, linking topology to observable quantum behavior. (Source: arXiv:2605.19740)

If the net accumulated integer around every relevant loop vanishes—trivial holonomy—then a similarity transformation can map the non-Hermitian Hamiltonian to a Hermitian one, guaranteeing real energies. If the holonomy is nontrivial, however, the obstruction prevents such a mapping, and complex spectra become possible. The team demonstrates that at the balanced filling (fermions with equal numbers of electrons and sites, or spins with zero magnetization), the loops in the Krylov graph acquire a nontrivial phase. Remove a single electron, and the connectivity changes: the loops that previously carried a phase now cancel out, rendering the holonomy trivial. The reality of the spectrum is thus a global geometric verdict, not a local property of individual terms.

An important question, sharpened by recent work on operator-space fragmentation by Paszko and collaborators, is whether this holonomy criterion can be extended beyond the minimal models studied here. The Stockholm authors are careful to note that their analysis is built on two specific fragmented Hamiltonians, and that establishing the full generality of the principle remains an open task. Nevertheless, the geometric language they introduce—the Krylov graph as a manifold with a gauge field—offers a conceptual bridge that may prove adaptable to a wider class of non-Hermitian many-body systems.

Loops That Lock Reality

The holonomy criterion is not, as the team explicitly states, a guarantee of spectral complexity. Even when the holonomy is nontrivial, other mechanisms—such as additional symmetries—can still enforce real eigenvalues. This honesty is refreshing and prevents overinterpretation. What the criterion does provide is a necessary condition for a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian to possess a diagonal similarity transformation to a Hermitian counterpart. In the models studied, where the fragmentation is strong and the Krylov sectors are small enough to analyze exactly, the condition is also sufficient to explain the observed filling-sensitivity.

To see the mechanism in action, picture each Fock state as a peg on a board, and each directed hopping as a string that passes around the pegs, collecting a small twist at each step. At the balanced filling, the strings form closed loops that go around the entire board, picking up a net twist—like a ribbon given a half-turn before its ends are glued. Away from this filling, the strings can be rearranged so that every loop is untwisted: the system becomes topologically trivial. The transformation that removes the non-Hermitian character is then simply a relabelling of the basis states with appropriate phase factors, which exists if and only if the twists cancel globally. This is the discrete gauge theory on the Krylov graph.

The team also explores longer-range hopping models and constructs a phase diagram. Here, the filling sensitivity persists but becomes richer: certain fillings, such as one particle for every five sites, mark the boundaries between real and complex spectral regions. The holonomy criterion again governs the location of these boundaries, revealing that even in more complex fragmented settings, the same geometric principle organizes the spectral reality.

A Geometric Organizing Principle

The broader significance of this work lies in its shift of perspective. Instead of relying on the usual suspects—parity-time symmetry, pseudo-Hermiticity, or the non-Hermitian skin effect—the team proposes that holonomy in Hilbert space is a fundamental and complementary organizing principle. It provides a geometric reason why certain many-body sectors “feel” the non-Hermiticity while others do not. This matters not only for understanding toy models but also for the experimental effort to realize and control non-Hermitian many-body systems with cold atoms or Rydberg arrays. If a filling-sensitive spectral shift can be detected, it would be a signature of the underlying graph holonomy.

An open challenge, highlighted by the team, is that the holonomy analysis currently relies on the exact solvability of minimal models. Extending the idea to generic non-integrable systems, or to systems with a genuine non-Hermitian skin effect in their many-body sectors, is a nontrivial step. Yet the geometric formulation itself is suggestive: perhaps there is a topological invariant that classifies fragmentation patterns not just by symmetry but by the winding of the Krylov graph.

Another tension, noted by researchers working on fragmentation and spectral reality, is the subtle interplay between holonomy and the specific form of the non-Hermitian perturbation. In certain settings, nontrivial holonomy permits a complex spectrum, but whether one actually appears can depend on the specific structure of the Krylov sector and the strength of the non-Hermitian term. The Stockholm team's work clarifies that while holonomy sets the stage, the actual appearance of complex eigenvalues depends on whether other protective mechanisms—such as additional symmetries or more general similarity transformations—are also present. It is an invitation to explore the parameter space more thoroughly, in both theory and experiment.

Perhaps the most philosophically resonant point is that the reality of a quantum system’s energies—a property we usually take as a given of closed dynamics—can be traced to a global geometric property of the state space. When a many-body system is opened to its environment, the very fabric of Hilbert space acquires a curvature, and that curvature, like the curvature of spacetime in general relativity, dictates what is physically possible. The holonomy is not a metaphor; it is a precise mathematical statement about the connectivity of possible configurations.

The road ahead is clear, even if the exact path remains to be charted. The Stockholm team has given us a new lens: to look at non-Hermitian many-body systems and ask, not “Is there a symmetry protecting reality?” but “What is the holonomy of the Krylov graph?” That question leads us to consider the deep geometry of quantum states themselves. The answer may one day guide the design of open quantum systems that can switch between real and complex spectral regimes by changing a single particle or a single spin—a control knob that might find a home in quantum simulation or sensing.

For now, it is a reminder that the mathematics of one-way streets and invisible twists is not confined to the abstraction of gauge theory; it lives inside the quantum spaces we are only beginning to explore. The map is not the territory, but in Hilbert space, the map is precisely what determines the terrain.
