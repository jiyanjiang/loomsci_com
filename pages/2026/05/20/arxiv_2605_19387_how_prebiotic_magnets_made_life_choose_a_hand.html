How Prebiotic Magnets Made Life Choose a Hand

May 20, 2026 Matter Science Source: arXiv:2605.19387

Imagine a shallow lake on the early Earth, brownish with dissolved iron, shimmering under a young, ultraviolet-rich sun. There are no living organisms yet—just water, rock, and the steady beat of sunlight. But within that tepid brew, invisible crystals are forming. They are magnetite, the same magnetic mineral that guides compass needles today. And according to new research, these tiny magnets may have nudged life toward one of its most stubborn asymmetries: why every living thing uses only left-handed amino acids and right-handed sugars.

Cast your mind back to a chemistry class and you’ll recall that many molecules come in mirror-image versions, like left and right hands. In the laboratory, when chemists cook up amino acids, they get a 50–50 mix of both forms. But life, from bacteria to blue whales, opts for one hand only. This phenomenon—homochirality—has puzzled researchers for decades. How did a blind geochemical process select one handedness and then lock it in forever?

A team led by S. Furkan Ozturk at the California Institute of Technology, working with researchers at the University of Cambridge and Harvard University, now proposes that magnetite itself could have been the agent of choice. In a preprint (arXiv:2605.19387), they argue that magnetite formed under realistic prebiotic conditions can act as a chiral amplifier through the chiral-induced spin selectivity (CISS) effect—a quantum phenomenon where the handedness of a molecule influences the spin of electrons, and thereby the magnetization of a surface. The twist, they show, is that the magnetite grains formed in such environments are structurally different from the thin films used in previous experiments, and these differences make them far more effective at preserving a chiral bias.

The researchers synthesized magnetite via two geochemically plausible routes: one using ultraviolet light to drive the oxidation of Fe(II)—simulating sunlit surface waters—and another using nitrite (NO₂⁻), a product of abiotic nitrogen chemistry. The resulting particles fell into what the team calls “nonstandard” magnetic domain states. Rather than the uniform, single-domain grains often used in CISS studies, transmission electron microscopy revealed particles with sizes around 250 nm (about one-third the wavelength of red light) that were dominated by single-vortex and multi-vortex configurations, where the magnetization swirls in closed loops or multiple vortices rather than pointing uniformly. Magnetic measurements using first-order reversal curves and standard magnetometry placed the grains squarely in the vortex region of a standard rock-magnetism diagram known as the Day plot, far from the single-domain regime typical of thin films. This is the crucial material difference: the prebiotic magnetite has a swirling, easily reconfigurable internal magnetization that no thin film can provide.

Sunlight or nitrite reactions create magnetite particles that become naturally magnetized. This magnetic surface may have selectively shaped early organic molecules, linking geology to the origin of life. (Source: arXiv:2605.19387)

This is where the CISS effect enters. In the CISS framework, chiral molecules act as spin filters: a layer of left-handed molecules preferentially passes electrons with one spin orientation, while right-handed molecules pass the opposite. When such molecules adsorb onto a magnetic surface, they inject spin-polarized electrons that exchange-torque the local magnetization. However, the team notes: “magnetite synthesized through two geochemically plausible pathways—UV-driven photo-oxidation and nitrite-mediated oxidation of Fe(II)—produces particles dominated by single-vortex and multi-vortex magnetic domain states.” So the substrate is not a simple ferromagnetic slab but a complex, swirling magnetic texture.

The key insight is that these vortex states can be irreversibly remagnetized by chiral molecules. The team performed 3D micromagnetic simulations, varying the strength of the exchange interaction between a spin-polarized molecular layer and magnetite grains of different sizes. They found that for grains below about 130 nm (about 1000 atoms lined up, or the wavelength of ultraviolet light)—the size range that actually forms in their prebiotic syntheses—the magnetization undergoes a permanent change after the interaction is cycled on and off. “Magnetic irreversibility provides a robust mechanism for storing and reinforcing weak chiral bias,” the researchers write, “suggesting that prebiotic magnetite could have contributed to the emergence and stabilization of persistent chiral bias on the early Earth.”

Chiral molecules permanently flip the magnetic structure of small magnetite particles, locking in a new orientation. This feedback between molecular handedness and magnetism could explain how life’s single-handed molecules first emerged. (Source: arXiv:2605.19387)

Think of it like a magnetic tape recorder. A slight initial excess of, say, left-handed molecules would align the magnetization of nearby magnetite grains to a particular orientation. That magnetized surface, in turn, would facilitate the further adsorption of left-handed molecules over right-handed ones, reinforcing the bias. Over time, the imbalance could propagate and become locked in, even as the original chemical environment changed. The simulations showed that for exchange energies above roughly 50 meV, the final magnetization would be substantially different from the starting state, and because the process is irreversible, the information is stored.

The idea is elegant, but it leans on an important assumption: the strength of the exchange coupling between the molecular layer and magnetite. An earlier ab initio study (Safari et al.) suggested that such interactions might be too weak to produce significant remagnetization. Ozturk and colleagues acknowledge the uncertainty, yet argue that the broader body of CISS experiments—including those on various magnetic surfaces—supports a higher coupling strength, and that their parameter range captures the essential physics. This is a typical tension in a young field: theory and ab initio calculations are still converging on what the actual molecule‑surface exchange energies are, but the new simulations show that even a moderate coupling, well within the range discussed in the literature, suffices to drive irreversible changes. If validated by direct measurement, the feedback mechanism would be robust.

The team is careful not to claim they have solved the homochirality puzzle. Other amplification mechanisms, such as autocatalytic reactions, have also been proposed. But the magnetic feedback loop offers a unique advantage: it can operate in a purely geological setting, without the need for living cells or complex catalysts. It ties together geochemistry, mineralogy, and the quantum behavior of electrons. The origin of homochirality is not just a chemical curiosity; it is a window into the conditions that make life possible, and it may guide the search for biosignatures on Mars and exoplanets.

The next step is clear: to test the proposed mechanism experimentally. Researchers could grow magnetite particles under prebiotic conditions, expose them to a known excess of one chiral molecule, and then measure whether the magnetization shifts accordingly and stays shifted after the molecules are removed. Such experiments are becoming feasible with today’s nanoscale magnetic probes, and they will determine whether these ancient magnetic memories are more than a theoretical possibility. If the effect holds up in the lab, we may have found one of the hidden keys to the handedness of life.
