When AI Learns to Second-Guess the Doctor

Drafted May 14, 2026 · Published May 16, 2026 Artificial Intelligence Source: arXiv:2605.13542

A new benchmark reveals that AI trained to mimic ICU doctors repeats their mistakes, but structured memory can improve clinical reasoning.

Imagine being taught to sail not by learning how wind and current work, but by watching a thousand skippers navigate a treacherous strait. You copy their turns, their speed adjustments, their moments of hesitation. You become a mirror. Then one day you are put at the helm alone — and the sea you face is not the one your teachers sailed. The waves are different, the fog is thicker, and you have no idea why they once took this heading rather than that. You can only mimic, not understand.

This is, in essence, how most artificial intelligence models are trained for clinical decision support in intensive care units. The ICUs generate dense, streaming data: heart rhythms, drug drips, ventilator settings, lab values arriving at irregular intervals — a torrent so relentless that even seasoned physicians struggle to keep the patient’s story straight. Existing benchmarks for ICU AI treat the historical actions of these exhausted, time-pressed clinicians as gold-standard truth. The AI learns to predict what the doctor did. But a preprint (arXiv:2605.13542) from Shen and colleagues argues, with painstaking empirical evidence, that this is a dangerous shortcut. What if some of the doctor’s decisions were wrong? What if the AI, by learning to imitate, learns to repeat mistakes it cannot recognise?

It is a question sharpened by earlier work on clinical evaluation. Previous studies, including the CliBench framework developed by Ma and collaborators, have shown that even expert physicians are prey to systematic biases — anchoring too heavily on an early diagnosis, for instance, or letting the eventual outcome colour their memory of what they knew at the time. The new benchmark, RealICU, proposes a simple yet radical corrective: instead of treating clinicians’ actions as ground truth, the team asked senior physicians to review the full patient trajectory in hindsight and then annotate what the correct clinical actions should have been. This shift from “what was done” to “what ought to have been done” transforms the evaluation from a game of mimicry into a test of clinical reasoning.

RealICU crystallises this into four tasks that any AI co-pilot ought to perform: assess Patient Status (is the patient deteriorating, stable, or improving?), identify Acute Problems, propose Recommended Actions, and — perhaps most critically — flag Red Flag actions that would risk unsafe outcomes. The team built two datasets. RealICU-Gold comprises 930 evaluation windows manually annotated by physicians across 94 patient records from the MIMIC‑IV database. RealICU-Scale extends this to 11,862 windows using an LLM-based “Oracle” labeler, itself validated against the human annotations.

The Two Failure Modes That Haunt Every AI Clinician

The results are sobering. Even the frontier models — Gemini-3.1-pro, GPT-5.4, Qwen3–235B — struggled, often failing to match the senior physicians’ judgment. But the paper’s deepest insight lies not in the poor overall scores, but in the specific ways the models failed. Shen and colleagues describe two distinct failure modes, each with its own uncomfortable implications.

The first is a recall-safety tradeoff. Think of a junior doctor who, terrified of missing a diagnosis, orders every conceivable test. Such a doctor will flag many real dangers — but also generate a blizzard of false alarms that can paralyse a ward and waste precious resources. The models display the same tension: configurations that remember more of the patient's history and thus catch more real dangers also recommend unsafe actions that the senior physicians would flag. A model can be vigilant or precise, but not both. In the high-stakes environment of an ICU, this tradeoff is excruciating. A false negative kills; a false positive sows chaos.

The second failure mode is an anchoring bias that feels eerily human. When a model reads a patient’s early admission notes, it forms an initial impression — say, “sepsis” or “heart failure” — and then clings to it, interpreting all subsequent data through that lens. Lab values that contradict the early picture are underweighted; notes that confirm it are overemphasised. It is precisely the cognitive trap that CliBench’s authors warned about, now reproduced inside a silicon brain. And it grows worse as the patient’s stay lengthens: across 1,800-hour trajectories, the models become less and less able to revise their initial diagnosis, even when the ground truth — the patient’s actual outcome — tells a different story.

Can a Structured Memory Break the Grip of the Past?

To counter these biases, the team introduced ICU-Evo, an agent architecture that gives the AI a structured, external memory — a kind of clinical diary that grows and revises itself over time. Rather than forcing the model to hold the entire epic of a patient’s stay in a single, fixed context window, ICU-Evo lets it selectively read, update, and forget. The approach echoes principles from recent work by Xu and colleagues on agentic memory, where an AI system dynamically curates what it remembers to improve long-horizon reasoning.

The results, measured on RealICU-Scale, are promising but incomplete. ICU-Evo improves performance on Patient Status and Acute Problem assessments even for the longest 1,800-hour trajectories. Yet the recall-safety tradeoff persists. The memory helps the model stay oriented — it remembers that the patient was once considered stable, that a specific antibiotic was trialled, that a particular lab trend is worrying — but it does not confer clinical wisdom. The model still cannot reliably know when a memory should override a new observation, or when a longstanding assumption has become dangerous.

Perhaps the most unsettling finding concerns the very act of looking backward. In RealICU, the “correct” answers are generated by physicians who already know how the patient’s story ends. This is a powerful lens: it filters out the fog of war. But it is also a bias. Work on clinical reasoning has long shown that outcome knowledge can retroactively colour judgments of what was knowable at the time. A patient who deteriorates can make earlier benign signs look ominous; a patient who recovers can make risky decisions appear wise. The RealICU oracle evaluator, an LLM itself, achieves only moderate agreement with human experts on some tasks, especially the subtle Red Flag identifications. The mirror is not perfectly flat — and perhaps a clinical benchmark should not aspire to be.

This does not make RealICU invalid; it makes it human. Any benchmark that aspires to teach machines clinical reasoning must itself confront the same epistemological quicksand that physicians wade through every day: that medical truth is rarely a single point, but a distribution of plausible interpretations, shifting with time and perspective.

What This Challenges

The deeper provocation of RealICU is not simply that today’s LLMs are not ready for the ICU — that much was already clear. It is that the very idea of a “correct” clinical action, enshrined in a static benchmark, may be a convenient fiction. Medicine, in its most honest moments, is a probabilistic enterprise. Two excellent physicians, given the same data at the same moment, can reasonably disagree. The benchmark therefore becomes not a referee but a conversation: a tool for exploring where models and humans diverge, and why.

The implication for the field is uncomfortable. If hindsight labels are contaminated by outcome bias, then no amount of scaling or architectural tinkering will produce an AI that meets the benchmark’s stated ideal. The gold standard would itself need constant recalibration — perhaps through prospective studies, or through a panel of physicians who must justify their annotations blind to the outcome, or through adversarial frameworks that probe the limits of clinical certainty. The RealICU team, to their credit, does not hide these difficulties. Their code and data are open, and their paper reads as much as an invitation to critique as a claim to finality.

So where does this leave the dream of an ICU AI co-pilot? Not in despair, but in a more mature, more honest place. The road ahead demands models that can not only remember, but also doubt; that can hold conflicting interpretations without collapsing into the first one they encounter; that can recognise when their own training data may have been shaped by human fallibility. It is a road that will require not one solution, but many — because, as the complexity of the human body teaches again and again, there is no single algorithm for living.
