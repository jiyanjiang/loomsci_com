A Symplectic Operator Learns the Dance of Optimal Control

Drafted May 15, 2026 · Published May 16, 2026 Other Sciences Source: arXiv:2605.14332

A neural network that enforces symplectic geometry learns the entire family of optimal control solutions for swarms, achieving real-time coordination with 10,000-fold speedup.

Picture a murmuration of starlings at dusk — thousands of birds wheeling in synchrony, never colliding, as if guided by a single intelligence. Or a school of fish darting in unison to elude a predator. For decades, engineers have dreamed of commanding swarms of autonomous agents — drones, robots, satellites — with the same effortless grace. The obstacle, however, has been computational: calculating the optimal path for each agent, given the complex interplay of dynamics and constraints, requires solving a high-dimensional optimization problem that can stretch from minutes to hours. Every time the scenario changes — new starting positions, a different obstacle — the entire calculation must be rerun from scratch.

Now, a team at Brown University led by George Em Karniadakis, with Alan John Varghese and colleagues — including Paula Chen at the Naval Air Warfare Center Weapons Division in China Lake, California — has shown that a single neural network can learn to do all of this in a fraction of a second. Their work, described in a preprint (arXiv:2605.14332), introduces PI‑SONet — a Physics‑Informed Symplectic Operator Network — that does not solve each control problem anew but instead learns the entire family of solutions, then produces answers with speedups of up to 10,000 times over conventional solvers.

Optimal control is the mathematical discipline of steering a system from one state to another while minimizing a cost — think fuel, time, or deviation from a desired path. For a single agent, like a robot arm, the problem is manageable. But for a swarm of dozens of agents, each with its own position, velocity, and control input, the dimensionality explodes. The standard approach relies on the Pontryagin Maximum Principle (PMP), which converts the control problem into a set of coupled differential equations that evolve along Hamiltonian trajectories — the same geometric language that governs planetary motion and quantum mechanics. Solving these equations for every new initial condition is brutally expensive, a phenomenon known as the curse of dimensionality.

This is where the “operator” in PI‑SONet earns its name. An operator is a mathematical object that maps entire functions to other functions — in this case, it maps the problem’s specifications (initial conditions, obstacle geometry, agent sizes) directly to the optimal trajectories, without solving the underlying equations each time. Think of it as the difference between recalculating a symphony note by note for each new performance and learning the conductor’s baton movements that generate the music. PI‑SONet is trained on a family of problems, so that when a new member of the family arrives, it instantly infers the correct choreography.

What makes this possible is a deeper structural insight. The Hamiltonian dynamics that underlie optimal control possess a special geometric property: they are symplectic — meaning they preserve the phase‑space volume, much like an incompressible fluid whose elements never clump or spread arbitrarily. In physical terms, energy is conserved, and the dance of the system remains physically plausible. If a neural network ignores this structure, its predictions can drift into unphysical territory, accumulating errors that make the trajectories useless. PI‑SONet enforces symplecticity through a conditional symplectic decoder, a module whose architecture is mathematically guaranteed to produce symplectic maps, conditioned on the problem parameters. This is not a metaphor; the decoder literally composes a series of symplectic transformations, each described by equations that preserve the appropriate geometric quantity.

The network’s design is a two‑stage affair. First, a latent right‑space solver compresses the high‑dimensional problem into a low‑dimensional auxiliary space where the dynamics are computationally cheap to evolve. This is akin to a cartographer projecting a globe onto a flat map — the essential relationships are preserved, but the calculation becomes far simpler. Second, the conditional symplectic decoder lifts this simplified trajectory back into the full physical phase space, reconstructing the detailed positions and momenta of every agent while strictly obeying the symplectic rules. The entire network is trained by minimizing the PMP residuals — the discrepancy between the network’s output and the true optimality conditions — so that it becomes a physics‑informed surrogate for the exact solution operator.

This speedup makes real-time coordination of multiple drones or vehicles practical for dynamic, unpredictable environments. (Source: arXiv:2605.14332)

The results are striking. The team tested PI‑SONet on a broad suite of benchmarks: up to 64 agents navigating free space and weaving around a circular obstacle, and 8 agents threading through a maze‑like environment. In all cases, the network produced collision‑free, coordinated maneuvers in under a second, with near‑optimal costs. When the obstacle radius was changed, the network smoothly expanded the trajectories to maintain safe clearance while preserving the same global maneuver — evidence that it had learned a continuous family of solutions, not just a memorized response to a single geometry. Even more, the same trained network handled agents of different sizes, automatically adjusting clearance margins, and extended to three‑dimensional cluttered scenes, demonstrating robust generalisation across agent heterogeneity and spatial dimension.

These capabilities matter because real-world swarms rarely resemble textbook problems. Drone deliveries encounter shifting no‑fly zones; autonomous vehicles must merge into traffic with vehicles of varying sizes and behaviours. A controller that must be re‑tuned for every new scenario is of limited practical use. PI‑SONet offers a glimpse of a different paradigm: a single pretrained surrogate that can be queried in real time, adapting on the fly.

Yet the deeper significance is not the speedup, impressive though it is. The work is a case study in how physics‑informed machine learning can encode not just a mapping from inputs to outputs, but the underlying laws that generate those outputs. By baking in symplectic structure, the network learns to respect the geometric grammar of motion — the same grammar that governs everything from the orbit of a planet to the flutter of a quantum wave function. It is not guessing; it is discovering the permanent choreography behind the ephemeral steps.

That insight points forward. The same framework could be adapted to any domain where a family of optimal control problems shares a Hamiltonian skeleton — quantum circuit design, spacecraft rendezvous, or even the assembly of synthetic molecular machines. The method is not a universal panacea; the training still requires generating a representative family of solved problems, and the symplectic decoder introduces its own architectural assumptions. But the direction is clear: we are moving from solvers that start from zero to surrogates that already know the dance.

The murmuration of starlings, it turns out, is not planned on the spot. It emerges from simple behavioural rules encoded in each bird’s nervous system — a distributed computation that has been honed by evolution over millions of years. PI‑SONet captures a similar logic: implicit knowledge of the governing structure, condensed into a network that can respond in an instant. Perhaps one day our swarms will move with that same genetic fluency, guided not by brute‑force number‑crunching but by an ingrained physical intelligence, learned once and deployed everywhere.
