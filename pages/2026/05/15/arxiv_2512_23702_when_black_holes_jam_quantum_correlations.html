When Black Holes Jam Quantum Correlations

Drafted May 12, 2026 · Published May 15, 2026 Quantum Physics Source: arXiv:2512.23702

Black holes can jam quantum correlations without violating causality, using the event horizon as a natural jamming filter.

Special relativity’s prohibition on faster-than-light signalling seems ironclad. A message sent between two events that lie outside each other’s light cones is, according to every textbook, impossible. But what if that prohibition is not a law of nature, but a rule of logical consistency—something that emerges from the very act of trying to define a coherent universe? A preprint (arXiv:2512.23702) from Michal Eckstein at Jagiellonian University and a team of collaborators spanning Gdańsk, Hong Kong, and the Copernicus Center for Interdisciplinary Studies proposes a unified framework that turns this idea into a precise mathematical statement. Their work does more than tidy up existing theory; it challenges two recent headline-grabbing claims about causality in quantum spacetime and unearths a strange new role for black hole event horizons.

The core innovation is deceptively simple—in retrospect, like all elegant ideas. Instead of starting with the usual assumption that spacetime points have a fixed causal order dictated by the metric, Eckstein and colleagues define something they call an operational separation relation. Think of it this way: imagine a network of laboratory outposts scattered across the cosmos, each capable of performing a measurement at a specific instant. If two outposts are so far apart that not even light can travel between them, intuition says they cannot signal one another. But the real constraint is more subtle. What if a group of outposts pools their data? A clever combination of individually innocent statistics might, in principle, encode a superluminal message. The new framework makes this precise: a collection of measurement points is operationally separated from a later point if no physical pooling of their results can influence what happens there. It is a condition on correlations, not on geometry, and it generalizes the everyday notion of causality into a robust operational no-signalling constraint.

This is not will, but how quantum mechanics weaves reality—when we ask what it means to not be able to signal, the answer is written in the algebra of measurement statistics, not in postulates.

The first consequence is a quiet bombshell. In 2022, a study in Physical Review Letters claimed that certain exotic quantum processes—mathematical objects called process matrices—could give rise to operationally detectable causal loops in ordinary Minkowski spacetime. In plainer language: an effect could precede its cause, not just as a mathematical curiosity but as something an experimenter could directly witness. The claim set off a small storm of interest. If true, it would undermine the very notion of forward causation in flat spacetime. Eckstein and colleagues show that such claims are not viable. They prove that any process matrix purporting to create a causal loop in Minkowski spacetime would inevitably violate the operational no-signalling constraints. And violating those constraints, they further demonstrate, leads directly to a logical paradox: you could send a signal backward in time to prevent its own transmission. The universe, it seems, protects its consistency with an iron hand.

An important question raised by earlier work on process matrices is whether this new framework can accommodate genuinely indefinite causal structures at all. The framework’s relationship to formalisms such as Chiribella et al.'s theory of quantum computations without definite causal structure has not yet been fully explored. Whether this framework can accommodate processes like the quantum switch remains an open question, since the scheme relies on assigning each operation to a specific spacetime point. However, there is a limitation. The scheme relies on assigning each operation to a specific spacetime point. When the causal order is so indefinite that no point can be assigned, the description breaks down. This tension with the process-matrix formalism of Oreshkov and colleagues remains an open question—not a contradiction, but a frontier where the operational and formal definitions of causality may need to co-evolve.

An even more striking result concerns a phenomenon known as the jamming of nonlocal correlations. Imagine Alice and Bob share a pair of entangled particles; their measurement outcomes are correlated but, famously, cannot transmit a signal. Now introduce a third party, Charlie, who can manipulate the entangled state in a specific way. Under certain conditions, Charlie can jam the correlation—alter it so that Alice and Bob’s joint statistics carry a message, yet without any single party’s data revealing anything. Intuition has long held that such jamming, while conceptually tantalizing, must open a back door to superluminal signalling. A 2025 Nature Communications paper formalized this suspicion: any physical mechanism for jamming, the authors argued, would inevitably permit information to travel faster than light. Eckstein and colleagues controvert that claim. Using their operational constraints, they demonstrate that jamming can be compatible with a strict no-signalling envelope—provided the architecture of spacetime is right. The trick is that the jamming operation itself must be placed so that its influence falls entirely within the future light cones of all relevant measurement points, a configuration impossible in flat spacetime but achievable near a black hole.

Here the narrative takes a turn toward the eerie. The team shows that certain nonlocal correlations in a Schwarzschild black hole spacetime—correlations that straddle the event horizon—can be jammed by an agent falling across the horizon, without ever violating operational no-signalling. The event horizon, in this picture, is not a one-way membrane that simply swallows information; it becomes a kind of cosmic jamming filter, a place where the fabric of causality bends enough to permit an effect that would be unthinkable in flat space, yet still forbids paradoxes. Picture a tightrope walker over a chasm: the rope sags under the pull of gravity, allowing a new kind of step that would break the rope on level ground. The horizon’s curvature thus reveals a hidden flexibility in nature’s rules.

The result also sits in a productive tension with earlier philosophical analyses of superluminal signals. Robert Geroch, in a prescient 2010 essay, argued that special relativity can remain logically consistent even if superluminal signals exist, provided those signals cannot be used to construct a closed causal loop—a grandfather paradox. Eckstein’s framework sharpens that insight: the operational no-signalling constraints are precisely what prevent such loops, making the consistency argument precise and quantitative. The operational constraints are not a wall imposed from outside; they are the logical skeleton of a self-consistent world.

The philosophical implications are profound. For decades, physicists have treated the impossibility of faster-than-light signalling as a boundary condition on theories—a “thou shalt not.” Eckstein and colleagues reframe it as a derived constraint, a theorem that follows from the requirement that any physical process, in any spacetime, must avoid logical contradiction. Causality, in this view, is not a postulate but a consequence. If that idea holds, then the causal structure of spacetime itself may be emergent—not a fundamental fixture, but a pattern woven by the statistical rules governing information exchange. The black hole result lends weight to that speculation, because it shows that the horizon alters what can be jammed, suggesting a deep link between geometry and the algebra of no-signalling.

Not every question is settled. The framework’s reliance on fixed spacetime points for each measurement still restricts its applicability to the most radically indefinite causal structures. And the practical empirical testability of jamming near black holes remains, for now, a thought experiment. But the direction is clear. Eckstein and colleagues have given us a new lens through which to view the dance between quantum nonlocality and relativistic causality—a lens that, rather than resolving every paradox, sharpens the remaining questions into a more interesting form. What this work ultimately shows us is that the universe’s most fundamental speed limit is not a policeman enforcing a code, but a logician ensuring the play is consistent. And that may be a deeper truth than we were prepared to admit.
