Drawing Algebraic Geometry: A Diagrammatic Language Bridges Polynomials and Quantum Circuits

May 15, 2026 Quantum Physics Source: arXiv:2605.13993

New diagrammatic languages like GAG let mathematicians and quantum computer scientists draw polynomial equations and quantum circuits as pictures, revealing deep structural connections.

Modern algebra is built on symbols: variables, polynomials, and ideals — abstractions that live on the page, manipulated by rules learned in school. But what if you could do algebra by drawing pictures, and those pictures were not merely illustrations, but the mathematics itself? That is the central claim of Graphical Algebraic Geometry (GAG), a new family of diagrammatic languages introduced in a preprint (arXiv:2605.13993) by Dichuan Gao, Razin A. Shaikh, and Aleks Kissinger.

At its heart, GAG extends the tradition of "graphical calculus" — a way of representing mathematical structures as networks of wires and boxes, where each diagram stands for a composition of operations. The best-known example is Graphical Linear Algebra (GLA), which represents linear maps between vector spaces as diagrams of spiders and cups. GLA has proven remarkably powerful: it is a complete language for linear algebra, meaning every true equation corresponds to a diagram that can be transformed into an equivalent one using a small set of rewrite rules. Now, Gao and colleagues ask: can we do the same for algebraic geometry — the study of solutions to polynomial equations?

The answer, it turns out, is yes. The team constructs two symmetric monoidal categories: GCA, built from cospans of finitely generated commutative algebras, and GAG, built from spans of affine varieties. The key result is that these two categories are equivalent, providing a formal bridge between the algebraic perspective (polynomial rings, ideals) and the geometric one (solution sets, varieties). More strikingly, the authors prove a completeness theorem: every true statement in the category can be derived from a finite set of diagrammatic rewrite rules. This is no small feat — it means that algebraic geometry, a field often seen as the pinnacle of abstraction, can be studied by drawing and rewriting pictures.

Think of it this way: if GLA is a universal diagrammatic language for linear algebra, GAG is its cousin for algebraic geometry. But the analogy runs deeper. Just as GLA is closely related to the ZX calculus — a graphical language for quantum computing over qubits — GAG turns out to be intimately connected to the ZH calculus, a language designed for qudit-based quantum circuits over finite fields. The team shows that the qudit ZH calculus is precisely an extension of GAG, in the same sense that the ZX calculus is an extension of GLA. This is not a superficial correspondence; the authors provide a rigorous construction showing that computing amplitudes in qudit ZH requires only a constant number of queries to a GAG oracle. The connection is structural and complexity-theoretic, not merely metaphorical.

From Polynomials to #P-Hard Problems

The paper also reveals a surprising computational side. The authors show that instances of the counting constraint satisfaction problem (#CSP) — a classic #P-hard problem that asks how many assignments satisfy a set of constraints — can be recast as rewrite problems of closed diagrams in GAG. This means that deciding whether two diagrams can be rewritten into each other is itself #P-hard. In other words, GAG is not just a toy language; it captures the full computational complexity of counting solutions to networks of polynomial equations.

This result gives GAG a dual identity. On one hand, it is a complete and compositional diagrammatic calculus for algebraic geometry, elegant and conceptually clarifying. On the other hand, it is a computational model whose decision problems are provably hard — which, ironically, makes it a natural framework for reasoning about quantum complexity. The authors do not claim to have built a quantum computer out of diagrams; rather, they have shown that the mathematical structures underlying one language for quantum circuits (ZH) are exactly those of GAG, and that this relationship is computationally meaningful.

The Completeness Conversation

An important question raised by earlier work on graphical calculi is whether such completeness theorems are truly syntactic. The authors of the 2020 paper "Graphical Algebraic Geometry" (arXiv:2004.05287) — which shares the same name and was a direct predecessor — introduced similar diagrammatic ideas but stopped short of proving full completeness. The current paper goes further, but the proof relies on semantic equivalence of categories rather than explicit syntactic rewrite rules for the Nullstellensatz, a central theorem of algebraic geometry. This leaves a subtle gap: the completeness is demonstrated via a translation to the semantics and back, rather than by deriving every geometric fact through pure diagram chasing. Whether a purely syntactic completeness — one that stays entirely within the diagram language — is possible remains an open question.

This tension is not unique to GAG. In the ZX calculus, completeness proofs also typically rely on semantics, though specialized syntactic proofs exist for certain fragments. The real test, as the authors acknowledge, is whether the GAG rewrite system can be made to directly manipulate geometric objects like varieties without passing through algebra. The current work provides the foundation; the tools for full syntactic mastery may come in the future.

The Reality Check

A separate thread from foundational quantum mechanics raises a different kind of question. In "Impossible measurements require impossible apparatus" (arXiv:2003.04660), Bostelmann and colleagues argued that certain operations described by mathematical structures may not be physically realizable. The GAG framework, while mathematically rigorous, does not address whether the quantum circuits described by the ZH calculus (via GAG) correspond to physically feasible operations. The connection between GAG and ZH is structural and algebraic; it does not guarantee that every diagram in GAG corresponds to a physically implementable quantum process. This distinction is important for anyone hoping to use GAG as a direct guide for experimental design. The authors are honest about this: their construction is about the mathematics of computation, not the physics of implementation.

Nevertheless, the value of a unified diagrammatic language should not be underestimated. Just as the ZX calculus has become a standard tool for reasoning about quantum circuits — used in optimization, compilation, and verification — the ZH calculus and its GAG foundation could provide a similar service for quantum computing over qudits, especially in fault-tolerant models based on finite fields. The ability to rewrite diagrams is not just an intellectual exercise; it underpins many of the automated tools that quantum software engineers rely on.

A Language Still Learning to Speak

Where does this leave us? The team has built a language that can talk about algebraic geometry, counting problems, and quantum circuits using the same visual grammar. It is a remarkable feat of unification — the kind that mathematicians and computer scientists often dream of but rarely achieve so cleanly. Yet the language is still learning to speak fluently. The completeness proof, while impressive, leaves room for more syntactic control. The connection to physics remains indirect. And the #P-hardness result, while a demonstration of power, is also a warning: complete reasoning in GAG is computationally expensive.

But perhaps that is exactly the point. The cathedral of mathematical knowledge is not built in a day, and neither are the tools that let us see it clearly. GAG offers a new window into a world where polynomials, varieties, and quantum circuits are all patterns in the same tapestry. The thread that weaves them together is drawn not in symbols alone, but in diagrams — and that is a kind of beauty that speaks directly to the eye.
