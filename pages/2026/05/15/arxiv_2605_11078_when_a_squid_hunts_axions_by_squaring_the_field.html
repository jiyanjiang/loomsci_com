When a SQUID Hunts Axions by Squaring the Field

13 May 2026, Yanjiang

A dc SQUID operated at its flux sweet spot detects the zero-frequency component of the squared axion field, enabling a broadband search for dark matter across a vast mass range.

Deep inside a cryostat, cooled to within a whisker of absolute zero, a tiny superconducting circuit — a dc SQUID — sits in utter stillness, shielded from every magnetic whisper of the world outside. It is listening, not for a note at a single pitch, but for a constant, quiet hum that would betray the presence of the dark matter that holds our galaxy together. A preprint (arXiv:2605.11078) from a team spanning Sapienza, Northeastern, and Columbia University now proposes a radically different way to listen, one that could turn a tabletop sensor into an all-frequency radio for the axion — the most compelling and elusive dark matter candidate.

Axions are hypothetical particles whose existence would solve a deep puzzle in the strong nuclear force, yet their mass is unknown, stretching across a staggering range. A traditional axion haloscope, like the celebrated ADMX experiment, works like a radio receiver that must be tuned to exactly one station at a time: it searches for the faint conversion of an axion into a microwave photon at a frequency set by the axion mass. That narrowband approach is exquisitely sensitive, but it can only probe one mass at a time. The new idea, put forward by Angelo Esposito, Kin Chung Fong, and Lam Hui, is to forget the radio station’s frequency and instead measure a signal that is always there — a sort of constant background hum — produced by the square of the axion field.

The authors open their preprint with a disarmingly simple statement: they “propose a novel broadband strategy to search for axions by leveraging observables controlled by the axion field squared.” If an axion field oscillates at a frequency matching its mass, then its square — the field multiplied by itself — inevitably contains a component that does not oscillate at all. It is a steady, zero-frequency contribution, like the average brightness of a flickering candle. The idea is to detect that unvarying DC component, which is present regardless of the axion’s mass, and so open a window onto axions spanning more than 15 orders of magnitude in mass, all in a single experiment.

The practical implementation is both elegant and demanding. The team proposes to use a dc SQUID — a superconducting quantum interference device — biased not where its voltage responds linearly to magnetic flux, but at a special “flux sweet spot” where the linear response vanishes and the voltage depends quadratically on the flux. Think of a weighing scale that, instead of reporting your weight directly, responds to the square of your weight: a tiny change in the weight is then magnified by the square, provided you know how to read it. In this quadratic regime, a minuscule axion-induced magnetic flux, squared by the SQUID’s own nonlinearity, generates a measurable voltage signal. The scheme also uses a lock-in modulation — a well‑known technique for pulling faint signals out of noisy backgrounds — by deliberately shaking the SQUID with a known magnetic modulation and then demodulating the output, which effectively removes the low‑frequency electronic noise that normally plagues DC measurements.

Running a superconducting detector at its flux sweet spot makes its voltage respond quadratically to the axion field. This lets the experiment measure axion dark matter by detecting the square of its effect, covering a wider range of possible masses. (Source: arXiv:2605.11078)

The projected sensitivity is startling. The team estimates that, with a one-year integration, a modest 1-tesla magnet, and careful noise management, they could probe axion‑photon couplings roughly a thousand times smaller than current laboratory limits, and largely independent of the axion’s mass from the feebly light end up to masses around a micro‑electronvolt. This would carve deep into the parameter space favoured by many theoretical models, including the classic QCD axion that could simultaneously solve the strong-CP problem and supply the observed cosmic dark matter.

Axion dark matter hides in a narrow yellow band untouched by past experiments, while red and blue zones are already ruled out. This map guides the new experiment to the most promising spot for discovery. (Source: arXiv:2605.11078)

Yet an important question, sharpened by earlier broadband searches, is how to tame the real-world backgrounds that threaten to drown such a delicate signal. The ABRACADABRA experiments (Ouellet et al., 2019; Salemi et al., 2021) demonstrated that even with exquisite magnetic shielding and cryogenic cleanliness, stray magnetic flux leaking into the SQUID, and the sensor’s own residual linear response, can produce spurious signals that look exactly like an axion. The present proposal tackles this with a nulling technique — essentially, a feedback loop that cancels the ambient flux — but the required precision is daunting: to reach the projected sensitivity, the nulling must suppress stray flux to better than one part in a hundred million, a level not yet demonstrated in a long‑duration experiment. Whether that stability can be maintained over a year of continuous running is an open question that the authors acknowledge as central.

A second point, raised in earlier theoretical work on broadband axion detection (Kahn et al., 2016), concerns the magneto‑quasistatic approximation used to treat the axion‑induced current. The approximation holds only for axion masses below a scale set by the inverse size of the instrument, roughly 1/l, which naturally truncates the mass reach. The authors discuss ways to extend the range, but the practical ceiling remains a challenge for future design refinements. The paper is commendably honest about these hurdles, admitting that a full quantitative noise budget and a detailed nulling tolerance analysis are still needed before the scheme can move from concept to a working experiment.

Despite the open questions, the proposal’s versatility is striking. The same quadratic‑response approach, the team notes, could be adapted to search for axion‑fermion couplings — how axions interact with ordinary matter — or even for dark photons, another serious dark matter candidate. A single cryogenic platform could thus become a multipurpose dark matter observatory, probing interactions that current experiments cannot reach.

A generation ago, the notion of detecting an axion through its gossamer coupling seemed almost absurd, yet today’s experiments have pushed sensitivity by orders of magnitude. This proposal asks us to take another conceptual leap, shifting from the familiar hunt for a narrow resonance to a broadband search that feels, in a sense, like listening to the gravitational hum of an entire orchestra without knowing which instruments are playing. The sceptic in us might quibble that the practical challenges are formidable, and that no cryostat yet built has demonstrated the required magnetic stability. But the idea is undeniably clever. The axion hunt needs fresh thinking, and this design delivers it in spades.

If experimentalists can tame the noise and perfect the nulling, the road ahead could lead to a tabletop SQUID that functions as a universal axion radio — one that, for the first time, could scan the dark matter spectrum in a single, patient listen.
