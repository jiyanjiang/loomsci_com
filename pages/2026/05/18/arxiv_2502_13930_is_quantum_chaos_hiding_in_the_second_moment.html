Is Quantum Chaos Hiding in the Second Moment?

May 18, 2026 Quantum Physics Source: arXiv:2502.13930

In a many-body spin chain, the variance of entanglement across measurement histories separates chaotic dynamics from integrable ones, unmasking quantum chaos where averages fail.

What does it mean for a quantum many-body system to be chaotic? In classical mechanics, the answer is visceral: a butterfly’s wing, a hurricane, a trajectory that writhes away from a neighbour faster than any finite precision can track. But quantum evolution is linear—Schrödinger’s equation never amplifies small differences into anything dramatic. So theorists have built an arsenal of tools that look for chaos not in the divergence of trajectories, but in the way information scatters across space and time. Entropies grow, correlations spread, and the effective number of degrees of freedom explodes. Yet every diagnostic has a blind spot. An integrable system, when prodded just so, can mimic the entropy production of a chaotic one, muddying the very distinction the tools are meant to settle.

A preprint (arXiv:2502.13930) from a team led by Peter O’Donovan at Trinity College Dublin, with collaborators in Cologne and Singapore, now argues that the key to unmasking quantum chaos has been hiding not in the average behaviour of these entropies, but in their variance—the scatter of answers the system gives when you ask it slightly different questions. They introduce the projected process ensemble (PPE) and use its higher moments to build a probe that, in extensive numerical simulations, cleaves chaotic from integrable dynamics with a sharpness earlier measures lacked.

To understand why this matters, we need to step into the mind of an interrogator. Picture a quantum many-body system—a chain of spins, say—as a suspect under repeated questioning. The experimentalist’s interventions are the questions: at regular intervals, you reach in and measure a particular spin along a chosen axis, or perhaps you apply a unitary kick. The system, in between your pokes, evolves according to its own internal logic, the Hamiltonian. The process tensor, a concept that has become central to open quantum dynamics, is the mathematical object that stores all the correlations this sequence of questions and evolutions can generate. It is a memory of cause and effect, written in quantum spatiotemporal form.

O’Donovan and colleagues now take a crucial step. They define the projected process ensemble: fix a basis of local interventions—say, measuring the first spin in the computational basis at every time step—and record the pure quantum state that emerges at the end, after all interventions, for each possible measurement outcome. The result is an ensemble of output states, each corresponding to a different history of measurement results. Think of it as running the same interrogation protocol thousands of times, and collecting a different final wavefunction every time the suspect blinks left instead of right.

Chaotic systems show higher average entanglement with less variation than integrable ones across both intervention types. This distinction offers a reliable way to diagnose chaos in quantum systems. (Source: arXiv:2502.13930)

From this ensemble, you can compute entanglement properties. The bipartite entanglement entropy of each output state tells you how scrambled the information has become between two halves of the spin chain. The first moment—the average of the entanglement over all histories—recovers the quantum dynamical entropy and spatiotemporal entanglement, concepts that had been studied separately. Indeed, the PPE subsumes the Alicki-Fannes dynamical entropy, butterfly flutter fidelity, and other chaos quantifiers under a single mathematical umbrella. But the average alone, as the team shows, can be a crude judge. In interacting integrable systems, the mean entropy can climb almost as high as in chaotic ones, especially at finite sizes.

Entanglement patterns scale predictably with system size only for chaotic dynamics. This scaling difference provides a clear fingerprint to distinguish chaos from regular motion in complex systems. (Source: arXiv:2502.13930)

Here is where the PPE’s hidden power emerges. The second moment—the variance of the entanglement distribution—acts like a scalpel. In the team's simulations of the XXZ spin chain, a workhorse of many-body physics, the chaotic regime—with next-to-nearest-neighbour interactions that break integrability—produced a tightly clustered distribution: every measurement history ended with similarly high entanglement, and the variance was small. The integrable regime, by contrast, produced the same average entanglement but a much broader distribution, with some histories yielding high entanglement and others low. The variance was large. An analogous pattern held when the team turned to a model of many-body localization (the IAA model) and to free fermions: chaotic dynamics compress the entanglement spread; non-chaotic dynamics let it run riot.

Someone familiar with random matrix theory might find this hauntingly familiar. Chaotic systems, after all, are those whose energy-level statistics mimic the universal patterns of random matrices; integrable systems cluster around Poissonian rules. The PPE’s distribution of entanglement seems to be a many-body analogue of this spectral diagnostic—a window into universality, not of eigenvalues, but of intervention-conditioned quantum states. The team checked this by comparing their PPE distributions to those generated by Haar-random unitaries. In the chaotic XXZ model, the distribution hugged the Haar prediction; for integrable, localized, and free-fermion cases, it drifted far away.

The unification is elegant. The PPE framework shows that what earlier work called quantum dynamical entropy—the entanglement between past interventions and the output state—is just one slice of a richer statistical structure. Spatiotemporal entanglement, which looked at entanglement across the process tensor itself, is another slice. The PPE’s hierarchy of moments offers a systematic way to go beyond the mean. The variance is only the beginning; higher cumulants could capture even subtler prints of chaos. But the story is not without its tensions.

A natural question, sharpened by prior work from White and colleagues (arXiv:2107.13934) on what unitary sequences can teach us about multi-time physics, is whether these higher moments can actually be accessed in an experiment. Measuring the full PPE requires projective interventions—sharp measurements—at multiple times, each collapsing the wavefunction. An experimenter who can only apply unitary pulses, without mid-evolution measurement and re-preparation, may not be able to assemble the full ensemble. O’Donovan’s team acknowledges this challenge and suggests that randomized measurement schemes, which can statistically reconstruct entanglement properties from simpler data, might serve as a bridge. But no experimental demonstration yet exists.

There is a second, deeper, question that comes from the theoretical side. Bou-Comas and collaborators (arXiv:2409.05517) recently investigated how generalized temporal entropies can be measured in experiments, and their framework raises a subtle point: the moments of the PPE are defined on output states after repeated interventions, which is not identical to the temporal entanglement studied in other works. The PPE’s variance is sensitive to the particular basis of interventions chosen. In the Trinity College Dublin paper, deterministic interventions (fixed Pauli-Z measurements at each step) produced one pattern, while non-deterministic interventions (measuring along different axes) yielded another. The distinction between chaos and integrability persisted, but the quantitative behaviour changed. This is not a flaw; it is a feature. The PPE is a family of diagnostic tools, and the choice of basis gives the experimentalist a dial to tune sensitivity. What remains to be proven, however, is whether the sharp separation seen at modest system sizes survives the thermodynamic limit. Does the variance of entanglement display a genuine exponential sensitivity in chaotic systems, or merely a weaker power-law suppression? A rigorous finite-size scaling analysis—the kind that separates a phase transition from a crossover—has not yet been performed.

Nevertheless, this work reframes the search for quantum chaos. It tells us that we should not be content to ask “how entangled does the system become on average?” but “how predictably does it become entangled?” A chaotic system is an honest informant: no matter the specific measurement outcome sequence, the result is maximal scrambling, almost every time. An integrable system is a poet who sometimes delivers a sonnet and sometimes a grocery list, depending on the historical accidents of the measurement record. The variance captures this reliability, or lack thereof.

For decades, physicists have used chaos as a synonym for disorder. But the language itself betrays us: chaos, from the Greek khaos, originally meant a gaping void, the primordial formlessness before order. The PPE suggests that what we call quantum chaos is, in a precise statistical sense, a kind of order—a narrowing of possibilities, a concentration of outcome distributions onto a universal form. Order is not the absence of scatter; order is when the scatter itself follows a law.
