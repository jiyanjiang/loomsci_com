Fluid Dynamics Learns to Intersect: A Geometric Reformulation

May 18, 2026 Cosmos Source: arXiv:2512.25053

Fluid dynamics is revealed as the intersection of a coisotropic submanifold (spacetime constraints) and a Lagrangian submanifold (fluid equation of state) in an infinite-dimensional symplectic geometry.

Picture two immense, invisible surfaces floating in a space of infinite dimensions. One surface is shaped by the internal nature of a fluid — its equation of state, its temperature, its capacity to swirl. The other is carved by the rigid demands of spacetime itself, the conservation laws that no flow can violate. Where these two surfaces touch, motion is born. The point of contact is not a random event; it is the entire fluid dynamics of a star’s interior, a superconductor’s strange viscosity, or the quark-gluon plasma that filled the early universe. This is the startling vision that Nikita Nekrasov and Paul Wiegmann, both of the Simons Center for Geometry and Physics at Stony Brook and the Kadanoff Center at the University of Chicago, have laid out in a preprint (arXiv:2512.25053). They propose that fluid dynamics, in all its incarnations, is at heart an intersection problem in an infinite-dimensional symplectic geometry. The equations we write down — Euler, Navier‑Stokes, magnetohydrodynamics, even the chiral anomalies that twist currents in a magnetic field — are nothing more than the mathematical expression of where two geometric shapes, a coisotropic submanifold and a Lagrangian submanifold, meet.

The intersection of two geometric surfaces inside a larger space pinpoints the exact fluid flows allowed by physics. This geometric picture uncovers the hidden rules that govern how fluids move and mix. (Source: arXiv:2512.25053)

This is not merely a decorative analogy. The paper demonstrates that once the correct symplectic manifold is identified — one naturally associated with the four‑dimensional spacetime in which any fluid lives — the entire machinery of covariant hydrodynamics, first worked out by Lichnerowicz and later Carter, drops out as a geometric consequence. The approach separates, as the authors write, “the structures determined by the equation of state and the spacetime geometry from the differential‑topological data of spacetime.” What that means, distilled, is that the physics of a fluid (its pressure, its composition) and the physics of the world it inhabits (relativity, curvature) no longer need to be glued together by ad‑hoc assumptions. They instead sit at two ends of a single intersection framework, speaking a common geometric language.

To understand why this matters, one must first appreciate how remarkably splintered fluid dynamics has become. The same underlying substance — a collection of particles that flow — is described by different equations in different contexts: the Euler equations for a perfect fluid, the Navier‑Stokes equations when viscosity matters, relativistic magnetohydrodynamics for plasmas around black holes, and exotic versions for superfluids or fluids carrying topologically protected surface waves. Attempts to give a unified geometric formulation go back decades. The most successful, the Lichnerowicz‑Carter formulation, gives a covariant description that cleanly separates thermodynamics from flow, but it still requires that one already know the right equations before one writes them down. A deeper, generative principle — a geometry that gives you the equations — has been missing.

A 2024 preprint by Davide Gaiotto and collaborators (arXiv:2402.00494) made a first move toward such a principle by recasting certain non‑relativistic fluid flows as Lagrangian intersection problems. But that work was, by design, limited to non‑covariant, non‑dissipative settings. What Nekrasov and Wiegmann have done is to lift the entire enterprise into the fully relativistic, infinite‑dimensional arena that modern physics demands. Their symplectic manifold (\mathscr{P}_{M^4}) is a space of fields — velocity potentials, densities, and internal variables — living on spacetime. Within this space they define a coisotropic submanifold (\mathcal{C}), given by the zero-locus of a moment map for the diffeomorphism group and thus purely differential-topological, and a Lagrangian submanifold (\mathcal{L}), determined by the fluid's equation of state and the spacetime metric. The condition that these two submanifolds intersect — that there exists at least one point in field space satisfying both sets of constraints — is the fluid equation of motion.

Flowing lines cross and merge at precise points, revealing hidden patterns in fluid motion. This insight helps predict how liquids and gases behave in everyday situations like weather or pipe flow. (Source: arXiv:2512.25053)

This is where the classical invariants of fluid dynamics acquire a new geometric clarity. The canonical velocity, a kind of gauge potential that encodes all the kinetic information of the flow, appears naturally as the pullback of a certain one‑form from the ambient symplectic manifold. The Ertel invariant, a conserved quantity that for an ideal fluid combines vorticity with the entropy gradient, becomes a topological index that counts a kind of winding number of the intersection. And the Kelvin circulation theorem — the statement that the circulation of velocity around a closed loop dragged along by the fluid is constant — is re‑derived as a consequence of the symplectic structure, a property of how the Lagrangian submanifold evolves. Even the celebrated chiral anomaly, the effect by which a classical symmetry can be broken by quantum fluctuations and generate a current along magnetic field lines, is captured: the framework naturally incorporates the Onsager quantization and the chiral separation effect when one includes the fluid’s anomalous degrees of freedom. In the words of the authors, “the same intersection‑theoretic viewpoint applies to self‑dual fields, including chiral bosons… the (2,0) theory… and the self‑dual four‑form field of type‑IIB supergravity.”

Yet the paper is not a final theorem. It is a proposal, and a provocative one. The elegance of the construction rests on a moment map — a function that translates symmetries of spacetime into geometric constraints. For the intersection to be well‑defined, that moment map must satisfy a property called equivariance: it must preserve the Lie algebra of the diffeomorphism group. As the paper stands, this equivariance is not proved in full infinite‑dimensional generality, and the explicit Poisson bracket relations that would seal the consistency of the whole scheme are not written down. The authors acknowledge the gap, and in a later exchange with experts they outlined a path using known results from geometric quantization — but the details remain to be filled. Similarly, the connection to the multivalued Wess‑Zumino‑Novikov functional, which Paul Wiegmann himself had developed in a separate 2024 preprint (arXiv:2403.19909) as the proper home of the chiral anomaly in fluids, is here only sketched. The intersection approach promises to reproduce that functional from first principles, but it does not yet show exactly how the topological obstruction that gives rise to the anomaly is encoded in the geometry of (\mathcal{C}) and (\mathscr{L}). These are not refutations; they are the ordinary — and healthy — signs of an idea in its early, generative phase.

What, then, should one make of this work? The independent value lies not in having solved hydrodynamics, but in having given it a new grammar. For the first time, the diverse equations of fluid motion — ideal, dissipative, anomalous, and superfluid — are seen as different choices of submanifolds within the same infinite‑dimensional symplectic space. The framework acts as a classification scheme: a fluid is a fluid is a fluid, and what distinguishes one from another is merely which geometric condition one imposes. The practical consequences, while distant, are intriguing. A geometric formulation naturally suggests numerical schemes, such as symplectic integrators, that preserve conservation laws to machine precision. It might also guide the search for new topological phases of fluid matter, where the intersection condition acquires a non‑trivial homotopy class — a fluid that cannot be continuously deformed into a trivial flow without crossing a singularity.

The broader implication is that the old dream, going back at least to Arnold’s 1966 insight that Euler’s equation is a geodesic flow on the diffeomorphism group, is still alive. Nekrasov and Wiegmann have pulled that geometric thread all the way to the relativistic, quantum‑anomalous limit. The road ahead will require hard mathematical work: proving equivariance, closing the Poisson algebra, and linking the intersection geometry to concrete, solvable equations like that of a realistic neutron‑star ocean. But the map has been drawn, and the territory looks remarkably coherent. In the coming years, when physicists speak of a fluid, they may find themselves describing not a substance but a meeting of shapes — a quiet, geometric convergence that, once understood, may finally tell us why blood flows, stars shine, and the infant universe swirled in just the way it did.
