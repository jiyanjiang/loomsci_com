Seeing Without Sight: The Emergent Imagery of Language Models

Drafted May 20, 2026 · Published May 27, 2026 Artificial Intelligence Source: arXiv:2509.23108
Large language models trained only on text can outperform humans at spatial mental imagery tasks, suggesting language alone may enable a form of artificial phantasia.

Close your eyes. Take a capital letter L, lie it on its side, rotate it ninety degrees, and attach a small rectangle to what is now the top. What shape appears? If you've formed a mental image of an umbrella — curved handle, canopy — you've just performed a task that cognitive scientists have long called the gold standard for testing visual mental imagery. The reasoning itself is linguistic: you hear a sequence of words, parse instructions, execute spatial transformations in your mind. But the format of the final product — the way the answer arises — has been debated for decades. Is it a picture in the head, with spatial properties analogous to a visual percept? Or is it a purely propositional code, like a set of logical relationships expressed in language?

A new preprint (arXiv:2509.23108) by Morgan McCarty and Jorge Morales at Northeastern University makes a provocative claim: large language models, trained only on text and utterly devoid of anything resembling a visual cortex, can outperform humans at exactly this kind of task. The researchers call the phenomenon "artificial phantasia" — an emergent capacity to reason about spatial configurations with a fluency that, at least functionally, rivals the human mind's eye.

The Imagery Debate, Reframed

For half a century, the quarrel between pictorialists and propositionalists has shaped how we understand mental life. Pictorialists, led by Stephen Kosslyn, argue that visual mental imagery relies on depictive representations — analogs of real visual experiences that preserve spatial layout, can be scanned, and are subject to limitations like resolution and rotation speed. Propositionalists, following Zenon Pylyshyn, insist that what we experience as "pictures" are epiphenomena; the underlying representations are amodal, symbolic, language-like. The task McCarty and Morales employ — having subjects mentally compose letter and shape transformations until a recognizable pattern emerges — was specifically designed to force pictorial processing. Or so we thought.

Think of it like this. I can describe a face to you using a list of coordinates for eyes, nose, mouth. You might "see" the face. But if I ask you to superimpose that face onto a rotated mask, suddenly the coordinate-list format feels inadequate — you want a picture you can mentally twist. The compositional letter task pushes exactly that boundary. Subjects must hold multiple spatial steps in mind, each building on the last, until they "see" the final gestalt. This is supposed to be the pictorialist's home turf.

When Language Outperforms the Mind's Eye

McCarty and Morales created dozens of novel items for an extended version of the classic task and presented them to both human participants and a suite of large language models. The best models — Gemini 3 Pro, GPT-5, and the o3 family — didn't just keep up; they significantly outperformed the baseline of one hundred human participants (p <.0001). The result is not a subtle statistical nudge; it is a gap wide enough to feel like a fracture in the orthodoxy.

The secret, they suggest, lies in the nature of language itself. In a set of experiments where they varied the number of reasoning tokens the model was allowed to generate — essentially, how many words it could "think" before answering — performance improved with longer chains. This is what you'd expect if the model were solving the task through a sequence of propositional steps, refining its implicit spatial algebra with each new token. Language, the data imply, may be sufficient. The mind's eye might not need an eye at all.

This is not a claim of conscious experience. The word "phantasia" is borrowed from the Greek for appearance or imagination — the model is not claiming to feel anything. It is, however, producing outputs that match or exceed human performance on a task specifically designed to demand visual-like processing. That functional equivalence is the puzzle.

The Contamination Question

Before we hand the model a beret and call it an artist, there is a shadow over this result that the authors themselves do not hide. Hyperphantasia, a benchmark introduced by Sepehri and colleagues, tests multimodal language models on tasks of visual composition and transformation — and early findings suggest that when stimuli are genuinely unfamiliar, when the compositional path cannot be reverse-engineered from training data, the models' apparent visual prowess can evaporate. Even more pointedly, the MentisOculi work by Zeller et al. reveals that as soon as you push beyond familiar, canonical shapes — an umbrella, a house — into configurations that resist verbal shorthand, large language models start to stumble in ways that humans do not.

This sits in an uncomfortable but productive tension with the Northeastern team's claims. Are these models truly constructing an internal "image" — even a propositional one — or are they exploiting memorized schemas and statistical shortcuts absorbed during training? The paper's designs are all novel, yes, but "novel" is a precarious term when the training corpora of these systems are inconceivably vast and largely opaque. An instruction like "take the letter L, rotate it, add a shape" might map onto hundreds of similar puzzle descriptions in the scraping, allowing the model to pattern-match rather than simulate.

The diagnostic bottleneck identified by Tartaglini and colleagues — that performance on data visualization tasks often hinges on shallow heuristics rather than genuine spatial understanding — applies here as well. The task, for all its elegance, may be too close to the models' comfort zone.

The Propositional Route, and Its Limits

What makes the paper genuinely valuable, despite these concerns, is not the headline performance but the structural hypothesis it plants. The authors propose three ways a language model could succeed: pure propositional imagery, where all spatial relationships are encoded as symbolic relations; propositional imagery enriched by visio-linguistic priors — statistical co-occurrences between visual concepts and spatial words absorbed from image captions and descriptive text; or full-blown pictorial imagery, an emergence of depictive representations from text alone.

The data favour the first two. Longer reasoning chains help, but the models still make characteristic errors that betray a reliance on relational logic rather than visual simulation. When asked to combine an amorphous "curved shape" with a letter rotated in a non-standard orientation, they sometimes output descriptions that are logically consistent but visually impossible. A pictorial system wouldn't make that mistake; a propositional algebraic system would. McCarty and Morales interpret this as evidence for an emergent propositional "phantom-like" imagery — a capacity to simulate the functional output of a visual system without ever generating a depictive format.

Think of the difference between arranging furniture in a room by looking at a floor plan, versus doing it from a list of dimensions and placement rules. The former is depictive; the latter is propositional. The language model, in this analogy, is consulting the rulebook — not the picture — and astonishingly, it works.

A Blueprint, Not a Proof

The paper does not claim to have settled the imagery debate. It offers something perhaps more valuable: a new testbed. By treating large language models as cognitive simulacra, we can interrogate them in ways that are impossible with human subjects. We can control their "experience," systematically modify their training, probe their internal representations, and ask counterfactual questions about what they would do if equipped with different architectural priors. The authors acknowledge the road ahead: contamination controls must become stricter, representational probing must become standard, and the tasks must evolve beyond the comfort zone of the training set.

What this work ultimately shows us is that the boundary between language and vision is more porous than cognitive science has been willing to admit. If a blind system of text prediction can, without any dedicated visual hardware, learn to navigate a spatial reasoning task designed to require pictures, then perhaps the distinction between depictive and propositional formats is less a dichotomy and more a continuum of informational adequacy. The mind's eye, in both humans and machines, may be less a projector and more a story we tell ourselves — a story composed in words so fluent that it reads as light.

The question now is not whether a machine can hallucinate shapes. It is whether we, looking at a string of output tokens, can tell the difference between a picture and a poem. We may need a sharper lens.
