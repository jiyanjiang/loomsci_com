The Impossibility That Turns Explanations into a Coin Toss

May 22, 2026 Artificial Intelligence Source: arXiv:2605.21492

Imagine a courtroom where two defendants are identical twins. Every piece of evidence — fingerprints, alibis, motive — points equally to both. The judge, determined to declare who is more guilty, studies the files one last time and, finding no difference, flips a coin.

Now you know how it is for feature attribution methods when the variables in a machine‑learning model are tightly correlated. A preprint (arXiv:2605.21492) by a team of independent researchers — Drake Caraker, Bryan Arnold, and David Rhoads — proves something that many data scientists have suspected but nobody had pinned down with mathematical iron: no feature‑ranking method can simultaneously be faithful, stable, and complete when collinearity is present. The three virtues that we ask from an explanation — that it truthfully reflects the model, that it gives the same answer every time, and that it produces a strict order of importance — cannot all be had at once. If you want faithfulness and a definite ranking, the ranking will wobble like a weather vane. If you demand stability, you must accept ties, a ranking that politely refuses to distinguish the undistinguishable.

This is not a soft, “it’s complicated” sort of limitation. It is an impossibility theorem of the same logical breed as the Halting Problem or Arrow’s voting paradox, and the authors have mechanised it in the Lean 4 proof assistant with 305 theorems built from first principles. For the first time in explainable AI, an impossibility has been verified down to the machine’s own code, leaving no room for a clever escape hatch.

The central villain is collinearity — the familiar situation where two or more features move in lockstep. Think of a model that uses both a person’s income and their tax bracket; making one a bit larger automatically makes the other a bit larger too. When the model is a black box, any method that tries to apportion credit to individual features (SHAP, LIME, integrated gradients, you name it) faces a mathematical mirror. There is simply not enough information to decide which of the twins was the real culprit. The team shows that this uncertainty takes a precise quantitative form: for gradient‑boosted trees, the attribution ratio diverges as the correlation coefficient climbs, while for the Lasso it is infinite — no finite sample can ever pin down unique importances.

This would be an elegant but academic observation if it were not so common. In a survey of 77 real‑world datasets, roughly two‑thirds showed attribution instability to a significant degree. Even in the canonical Wisconsin Breast Cancer dataset, the two most correlated features — worst perimeter and worst area — produce importance rankings that flip nearly half the time when the model is retrained. If a diagnostic tool tells you on Tuesday that tumour size is the key risk factor and on Wednesday that it is cell shape, which do you trust? The mathematical coin is landing heads as often as tails.

Yet an important current in the literature, notably the cohort Shapley framework of Mase and collaborators (2020), has shown that restricting the coalitions considered to those that are physically or logically plausible can sidestep the “mixing and matching” of impossible feature combinations that causes the instability. The impossibility proved in the new paper is narrowly aimed at methods that use the model’s own internal attributions without such cohort restrictions, and the authors are careful to acknowledge that their theorem leaves those other approaches untouched. That gap is fertile ground for a genuinely better class of explanations.

From a different angle, Rao (2025) has argued in an algorithmic information theory framework that the limits of explainability run much deeper than collinearity; they are tied to the Kolmogorov complexity of the model itself — a perspective that the present work cites as complementary, not competing. Whether the attribution design space can ever be completely characterised — even with a Lean‑verified two‑family structure — is therefore a tantalising open question, one that the present work sharpens but does not close.

What, then, does the impossibility force us to give up? The team shows that the design space has exactly two families of methods and no third option. One family, call it Faithful‑Complete, uses a single model, gives a strict ranking, but that ranking will reverse on roughly half of all retrainings for highly correlated features. The other family, which the team calls DASH (Diversified Aggregation of SHAP), builds an ensemble of SHAP scores across many model fits and then reports a tie when two features cannot be reliably distinguished. This ensemble approach is provably Pareto‑optimal: it achieves the Cramér‑Rao variance bound, the tightest possible guarantee that no other unbiased aggregation can be more stable without sacrificing faithfulness. In the courtroom, it would humbly report, “These two defendants are indistinguishable.”

The practical consequences are immediate for fairness auditing. A widespread technique is to check whether a model’s SHAP values rank a protected attribute — race, gender, age — among the top‑few most important features. If that ranking flips on every second retraining, the “audit” itself becomes a coin toss, a tool that can arrive at opposite conclusions about discrimination depending on nothing but the random seed. The paper provides a simple diagnostic, a Z‑test workflow, that practitioners can use to screen which feature pairs are too collinear for any single‑model ranking to be meaningful. Those pairs, the authors urge, should be reported with a tie — effectively DASH’s answer — rather than stoked into a fragile story about which variable “causes” the outcome.

To a physicist, the situation carries echoes of the quantum measurement problem. Just as you cannot simultaneously know a particle’s position and momentum with arbitrary precision, a data scientist cannot simultaneously have a faithful attribution that is stable and complete. The uncertainty is not a failure of instrumentation but a boundary inscribed in the mathematics of collinearity itself. The dream of a single, objective, and final “explanation” of a model’s decision dissolves: we are left instead with an ensemble of plausible stories, and the lucid choice is to report where they agree and to mark where they do not. That kind of humility, enforced by a theorem, may be exactly what the field of explainable AI needs to grow up.
