Transporting Fluctuations: A New Theorem Preserves Hyperuniformity

May 22, 2026 Mathematics Source: arXiv:2605.22803

Just as seismologists once realized that the speed of P and S waves reveals Earth’s hidden structure, physicists use density fluctuations to map the unseen order inside materials. A glass of water looks uniform, but the molecules are jostling at random, creating temporary clumps and voids. Some arrangements, however, are so exquisitely ordered that their density variations vanish as you zoom out—a property called hyperuniformity. It was long thought that moving the particles around would destroy this delicate balance, like stirring tea and watching the swirls dissolve into chaos. A new theorem by Luca Lotz and Michael Klatt at the German Aerospace Center (DLR) challenges that assumption. In a preprint (arXiv:2605.22803) they introduce a quantitative measure, p-uniformity, and prove that, remarkably, transport preserves the very quality that makes a system hyperuniform—so long as the shuffling isn’t too violent.

Think of a flock of starlings wheeling over a winter dusk. At any instant the birds seem scattered haphazardly, but if you look from high above and count how many appear in a large window, you’ll find that the variation from place to place is vanishingly small on the largest scales. That’s hyperuniformity. The flock’s density fluctuations shrink faster with window size than they would in a random gas. The new p-uniformity framework captures exactly how fast: the variance of the number of points in a box of side L behaves like L⁻ᵖ. The exponent p is a signature of order. A completely random scatter has p = 0; ordinary crystals give small p; and so-called stealthy hyperuniform systems, in which fluctuations are suppressed entirely up to a finite wavenumber, correspond to arbitrarily large p. The concept stretches all the way to hyperfluctuation, where the variance grows faster than the box volume—think of a wildly clumpy pattern. By classifying everything from total disorder to near-perfect rigidity, p-uniformity offers a single language for spatial randomness.

What happens if you take a p-uniform point set and independently displace every point a short distance? A geometer might suspect that the fine-tuned order would be ruined, because even a tiny perturbation scrambles the exact relative positions. The central theorem of Lotz and Klatt says that, under surprisingly mild conditions, the perturbed set remains p-uniform. The key requirement is that the transport distances don’t grow too quickly: the average of the displacement raised to the (d+p)-th power must be finite, where d is the spatial dimension. If that moment is bounded, a Taylor expansion of the transport operator becomes controlled, and the asymptotic variance is preserved. The theorem is vastly more general than prior results—it works for any dimension, any source, and even allows the source and the displacements to be statistically dependent.

The algebraic elegance should not obscure a practical payoff. The team uses the theorem to construct an entirely new class of point processes that are isotropic (look the same in all directions) and arbitrarily p-uniform, with p as large as one wishes. They begin with a random fair tiling—a space-filling division into cells of equal volume—then within each cell they condense the uniformly distributed mass into n points while preserving the first p moments. The resulting pattern is isotropic and, by the theorem, is at least (2p − d)-uniform. For two dimensions with n = 12 and p = 6, that guarantees 10-uniformity, but the team’s simulations suggest an even larger effective exponent of about 11.5, pushing the structure toward stealthiness. Numerical estimates of the structure factor drop so steeply at low wavenumbers that the system appears completely free of long-range fluctuations out to intermediate scales. Because the algorithm runs in linear time, it can generate samples with millions of points, orders of magnitude larger than what earlier methods could produce.

Yet the sweeping claim of “beyond p-uniform” should give us pause. The theorem guarantees only (2p − d)-uniformity; the sharper bound observed in simulation is a numerical conjecture, not a proven fact. The distinction matters: stealthy hyperuniformity in the strict mathematical sense requires that the structure factor be exactly zero over a finite interval, and that’s a much stronger statement. The current work does not prove that the fair-tiling construction achieves that property, only that it comes tantalizingly close. This is not a flaw—it is a boundary between what the rigorous mathematics can assure and what the data suggest. An important question, sharpened by recent work on convex geometry, is whether the individual cells in the STIT tiling are regular enough to guarantee the required moment bounds for arbitrarily high p. Earlier results on the inradius of convex bodies hint at geometric limitations; the cells may need to be unusually well-behaved to sustain the Taylor expansion’s remainder terms for large p.

A deeper question, raised by parallel developments in optimal transport, is whether the finite-moment condition is strictly necessary for the persistence of p-uniformity. Lachièze-Rey and Yogeshwaran had previously shown that for certain perturbed lattices, a moment condition is essential; without it, hyperuniformity can be eroded. Lotz and Klatt’s theorem provides sufficient conditions but leaves the necessity open. One can imagine a scenario where a source is p-uniform yet the displacements have just-infinite moments, and the transported process remains p-uniform through some cancellation. The paper does not claim that its conditions are the only ones, and future work might reveal a more refined criterion.

All of this, however, takes nothing away from the conceptual advance. The theorem changes the conversation from “is hyperuniformity fragile?” to “under what precise conditions does transport preserve order?” It solves an open problem that had resisted earlier approaches, and it does so with a language—p-uniformity—that unifies opposite extremes under one scaling law. The practical construction, even if not fully rigorous in its extremal claims, demonstrates that one can engineer hyperuniform configurations with a level of isotropy and computational efficiency that was previously out of reach.

So the result might be best described, with mathematical care, as a passport for p-uniformity under transport. The implications stretch from the design of materials with tailored optical properties to the study of large-scale structure in the universe, where dark matter appears to settle into a pattern that is neither fully random nor fully crystalline. The theorem reminds us that order is not a static arrangement of atoms but a resilient relationship between motion and stillness. Every perturbation, if gentle enough, leaves a fingerprint of the original order. And that, perhaps, is the most thrilling discovery of all: that in a restlessly shifting world, the deepest regularities can survive the journey.
