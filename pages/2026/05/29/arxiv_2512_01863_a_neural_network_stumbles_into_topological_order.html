A Neural Network Stumbles Into Topological Order

May 29, 2026 Matter Science Source: arXiv:2512.01863

Naive intuition would suggest that a neural network, trained only to minimize the energy of a quantum many‑body system, could not possibly discover a deep phase of matter on its own — especially one as exotic as topological order. After all, topological order is a ghost that physicists hunt with elaborate theoretical machinery: it hides in the pattern of many‑particle entanglement, not in any simple local order parameter. But a team led by Liang Fu at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology has just shown the opposite. In a preprint (arXiv:2512.01863), Ahmed Abouelkomsan, Max Geier, and Liang Fu demonstrate that an attention‑based deep neural network, the same kind of architecture that powers large language models, can spontaneously discover a fractional Chern insulator — a strongly correlated topological phase — purely by optimizing a variational energy, with no prior knowledge of what it is looking for. The network not only found the ground state; it revealed the characteristic topological degeneracy that is the unmistakable fingerprint of fractionalised quasiparticles.

This is not the first time neural networks have been used as variational wavefunctions. What makes this work startling is the emergence of something the network was never instructed to capture. The machine was given a Hamiltonian — a minimal model of electrons moving in a periodic magnetic field — and tasked with finding the lowest‑energy many‑body wavefunction. It did not know about topology, about Chern numbers, about anyons. And yet, when the researchers decomposed the optimized wavefunction into different centre‑of‑mass momentum sectors, three nearly degenerate states appeared, exactly as one would expect for a fractional Chern insulator at filling nu = 1/3 hosting quasiparticles with fractional charge e/3.

The Deepest Puzzle in Condensed Matter

To appreciate why this matters, we need to step back and ask what topological order really is. Landau taught us to classify phases of matter by broken symmetries: a crystal breaks translation invariance, a magnet breaks spin‑rotation symmetry. But topological order breaks no symmetry at all. Two topologically ordered states can have identical local properties and yet be fundamentally distinct, distinguished only by a global pattern of entanglement. Their most exotic signature is the existence of quasiparticles that carry fractional quantum numbers — electric charge one‑third of an electron, for instance — and obey fractional statistics: when you braid two of them around each other, the many‑body wavefunction picks up a phase that is neither 0 nor pi.

The theoretical study of such phases is notoriously difficult. Because the interactions are strong, conventional mean‑field theory fails. The fractional quantum Hall effect, the prototype topological phase, was solved by Robert Laughlin using a brilliant ansatz that happened to be exact for a certain model Hamiltonian. For more general systems, however, no such exact solution exists. This is where neural networks enter the picture. The idea is to treat the many‑body wavefunction as a function from particle positions to complex numbers, and to represent that function using a neural network with millions of parameters. The network is then trained, via variational Monte Carlo, to minimize the energy expectation value. If the network is expressive enough, it should be able to represent the true ground state, even if that state is topologically ordered.

The MIT group chose an attention‑based architecture, closely related to the transformers used in large language models. In this setting, the “tokens” are the positions of electrons, and the attention mechanism learns which electrons are most strongly correlated with one another. This is a clever choice, because attention naturally picks out long‑range correlations — the precise kind of structure one needs to encode topological entanglement. The network was then trained on a minimal continuum model of spinless fermions moving in a periodic magnetic field, a system known to host fractional Chern insulator phases for certain parameter values. The energy during training dropped steadily, eventually matching the best results from exact diagonalization on small lattice sizes, and even surpassing the accuracy of more conventional variational ansätze.

The neural network breaks the wavefunction into pieces that correspond to different ways particles move, revealing hidden patterns in the quantum system. This helps researchers identify the exotic quantum states needed for next-generation quantum computers. (Source: arXiv:2512.01863)

The Ghost in the Momentum Sectors

The most striking result, however, is not the energy — it is what happened when the researchers looked inside the wavefunction. After optimization, they projected the wavefunction onto different total momentum sectors. In a topologically ordered phase, the ground state should have a characteristic degeneracy: not just one low‑energy state, but multiple states that become exactly degenerate in the thermodynamic limit. The number of such states is dictated by the topology of the system, not by any microscopic detail. For the fractional Chern insulator at nu = 1/3, one expects three nearly degenerate ground states, each carrying a different centre‑of‑mass momentum.

The optimized wavefunction occupies only three distinct momentum states, each with a different energy. This quantized pattern reveals the system's hidden topological order, a key signature of exotic quantum matter. (Source: arXiv:2512.01863)

The neural network wavefunction, purely from energy minimization, yielded precisely this pattern. The overlap of the optimized wavefunction with its projections showed three dominant momentum sectors, each with substantial weight. Crucially, the energies of the projected states were nearly identical — within statistical error — confirming that they are the expected topological ground states. This is a remarkable finding: the network did not need to be told about momentum sectors, nor about degeneracy. The topology emerged as the minimal‑energy solution.

An important question sharpened by earlier work on self‑attention ansatzes for quantum chemistry is whether the observed momentum structure genuinely reflects topological order or is merely a variational artifact. The architecture used here — a multi‑determinant ansatz combined with attention layers — is highly expressive, and in some cases variational Monte Carlo can get stuck in local minima that happen to have the right degeneracy but are not physically meaningful. The present results, while suggestive, would be strengthened by systematic tests across different random initializations and by quantifying the energy gap between the purported degenerate states. That the network discovered a fractional Chern insulator without any explicit topological bias is impressive; but to fully establish the claim, future work will need to rule out the possibility that the optimization simply converged to a superposition of momentum sectors that mimic degeneracy.

A related concern emerges from the model itself. The Hamiltonian used by the MIT team — a triangular lattice with a periodic magnetic field — was specifically designed to host topological phases. When the band structure is tuned to the right parameter range, the lowest band carries a non‑zero Chern number. In this sense, the network did not discover topology from a blank slate; it discovered topology in a system whose single-particle bands were already known to be topological. The phrase “without prior knowledge” in the abstract should be understood in a narrow sense: the network was not told to look for topological order, but the model it was given was topologically fertile. This is a subtle but crucial distinction. As work on twisted semiconductor bilayers has shown, the competition between fractional Chern insulators and charge density waves is delicate, and small changes in model parameters can tip the balance. The network’s ability to find the topological state in this specific regime is impressive, but its performance in genuinely uncharted territory — where the topology is not already hinted at by the band structure — remains to be demonstrated.

A Tool for the Unknown

These caveats do not diminish the importance of the work; they define the frontier. The broader ambition, shared by many research groups, is to build a universal variational ansatz that can discover new phases of matter without human intervention. The MIT team’s momentum‑decomposition method is a significant step in that direction. By showing that a single optimized real‑space wavefunction can be decomposed into different momentum sectors to reveal topological degeneracy, they have created a diagnostic tool that is more practical than previous approaches, which required explicit construction of degenerate ground states. The method works only for translation‑invariant systems — a limitation that follows from the uniform sum over translations used in the decomposition — but many of the most interesting strongly correlated systems, from cuprate superconductors to twisted moiré materials, possess this symmetry.

Perhaps the deepest lesson, however, is philosophical. A neural network, in its blind search for low energy, can stumble upon a structure that physicists took decades to conceptualize. It did not need to know about the Laughlin wavefunction, Chern‑Simons theory, or braiding statistics. The patterns of entanglement that define topological order are, in some sense, the natural answer to a mathematical optimization problem. This does not mean the machine understood what it found — it has no concepts, no notion of quasiparticles — but it does suggest that the laws of quantum mechanics encode these exotic phases in a way that is amenable to discovery by an architecture that simply tries to find the lowest energy.

What this work ultimately shows us is not that machines can replace physicists, but that the space of quantum many‑body states may be more navigable than we feared. A few years ago, the idea that a neural network could find topological order by itself would have seemed fanciful. Now it is a concrete result, with all the necessary caveats about prior information, optimization artifacts, and limited system sizes. The challenge ahead is to push into truly unknown territory, where no human has ever spotted a topological phase, and to see if the network can find one anyway. If it can, we will have gained a new kind of telescope for exploring the quantum universe — one that does not look through a lens of pre‑existing theory, but that simply asks: what is the lowest possible energy? And in the answer, it might reveal worlds we never imagined.
