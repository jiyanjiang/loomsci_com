Beyond Murray: How Vessel Walls Break a Universal Branching Rule

May 29, 2026 Bioscience Source: arXiv:2603.13687

Nature has a peculiar talent for making mathematics look clumsy. For nearly a hundred years, physiologists have trusted a simple cubic law to describe how blood vessels branch from large arteries down to tiny capillaries: at every fork, the cube of the parent vessel’s radius should equal the sum of the cubes of the two daughters’ radii. The formula, derived by Cecil Murray in 1926, is so clean and so biologically plausible—it minimises the combined cost of pushing blood and maintaining it—that it acquired the status of a universal principle. Yet every careful measurement in real arterial trees has told a different story. Observed branching exponents hover stubbornly between 2.7 and 2.9, not the predicted 3.0. For decades, the gap has been blamed on pulsatile flow, measurement noise, or anatomical irregularity. Now a single‑author preprint from Riccardo Marchesi at the University of Pavia (arXiv:2603.13687) argues that the discrepancy is not a failure of measurement but a signature of biology that Murray’s law was never equipped to see: the living metabolism of the vessel wall itself.

To understand why, we need to look at what Murray’s law actually optimises. Blood must move, and moving it consumes energy. Blood must also be maintained; the larger the volume of blood in the system, the greater the continual cost of renewing cells and transporting nutrients. Murray showed that by choosing the radius of each vessel segment so that the cube of the parent’s radius equals the sum of the cubes of the daughters’ radii, the total cost—pumping work plus blood‑maintenance—reaches a minimum. The exponent three emerges because the pumping cost scales with the inverse fourth power of the radius while the blood‑volume cost scales with the square of the radius. Balance them, and the optimal scaling exponent is exactly three. It is a problem of minimising a function with two terms that share the same mathematical “homogeneity”: both can be expressed as powers of the radius with integer exponents that commute nicely.

Marchesi points out that there is a third player at the table that Murray never invited: the vessel wall. Arteries are not inert pipes; their walls are living tissue, consuming oxygen and carrying out metabolic work. From decades of morphometric data, we know that the thickness of a vessel wall grows with its radius according to a power law: the thickness is roughly proportional to the radius raised to an exponent of about 0.77. The metabolic cost of maintaining that wall therefore scales with the wall’s volume, which itself depends on the radius to the power of about 1.77. This is where the trouble begins. A cost term that grows as the radius to the power 1.77 cannot be written as a simple rescaling of the other terms, which are pure powers of two or four. The cost function has become inhomogeneous—it mixes incommensurate scaling exponents, like trying to balance a budget where some items are priced in dollars per square metre and others in dollars per linear foot. In such a function, no single universal branching exponent can exist; the optimal exponent becomes scale‑dependent, shifting with the flow rate that the vessel carries.

The heart of Marchesi’s proof uses a classic piece of mathematics: Cauchy’s functional equation. A function that is homogeneous in its arguments—where scaling all inputs by a constant factor multiplies the output by a fixed power of that factor—will naturally produce a unique optimal exponent. The loss of homogeneity destroys that guarantee. Marchesi shows that Murray’s law is actually a singular degenerate case of a broader family of cost functions, one where all the metabolic prices align. Add wall‑tissue metabolism, and the family splinters; the optimal branching exponent now depends on the downstream flow, thickening wall material, and the metabolic “price” of wall tissue relative to blood. The exponent is no longer a constant but a function alpha‑star(Q) that rises monotonically from a lower bound of (5+p)/2 to an upper bound of 3 as flow increases. For physiologically measured parameters in coronary arteries, this places the exponent for symmetric bifurcations squarely between about 2.90 and 2.94—an improvement that narrows the long‑standing gap between theory and observation by roughly one‑third.

Think of it like a family of tuning forks. Murray’s law is the fork that rings at exactly middle C, but real arterial trees are a collection of forks that hum somewhere between C and D, depending on the local flow they must carry. The wall‑tissue mechanism sets a floor: no vessel, no matter how weakly it is fed, can have a branching exponent lower than the wall‑dominated limit. For thicker‑walled vessels, the curve shifts downward, and the exponent moves closer to that floor. The result is not a single number but a distribution, exactly as experiment finds.

This is where the story becomes genuinely interesting. Even after accounting for wall metabolism, a gap of about 0.2 still remains between the predicted range (roughly 2.90–2.94) and the cardiovascular average (approximately 2.70). Marchesi’s analysis suggests that this is not a failure of the model but a mathematical necessity: the remaining discrepancy must arise from a physically independent mechanism not captured by the static optimisation. The prime candidate is pulsatile wave dynamics. Arteries do not carry steady flow; they pulse with every heartbeat, and the periodic stretching and recoil of the walls impose additional constraints—elastic energy storage, wave reflections, shear‑stress fluctuations—that a purely metabolic cost function cannot see. The wall‑tissue theory, in other words, acts as a kind of “first‑order symmetry breaking” from Murray’s law, while pulsatility provides the next‑order correction. The two effects are additive, not competing.

An important question sharpened by earlier theoretical work on Murray’s law’s thermodynamic basis is whether the principle can be salvaged as a universal extremum when an extra metabolic dimension enters the problem. Bennett (arXiv:2511.04022) recently reframed Murray's law as an entropy‑per‑information‑cost extremum, endowing it with a deeper physical interpretation. But that elegant thermodynamic reading still assumed a homogeneous cost structure. Marchesi's wall‑tissue mechanism introduces an incommensurate exponent that breaks that homogeneity, and with it the two‑term universality that Bennett's framework captures. The optimal branching network is not minimal in entropy or anything else that can be written in a single scaling language; it is a local negotiation among different metabolic currencies, each with its own price.

The theory leaves open a tantalising puzzle. Marchesi’s derivation treats the cost function as a polynomial truncated at three terms: pumping, blood‑volume, and wall‑tissue maintenance. No rigorous justification is offered for why higher‑order terms—perhaps from immune cell trafficking within the wall, or from active tension regulation in smooth muscle—should not also contribute. A natural question is whether the truncation is safe, or whether additional wall‑cost components would further modify the branching exponent bounds. Marchesi acknowledges the assumption honestly, noting that the identification of the wall‑tissue term is structural rather than derived from first principles. The door is open for a more complete biochemical and mechanical accounting.

Beyond its physiological impact, the work carries a broader philosophical lesson about the nature of physical law. We are used to thinking that the most beautiful laws are universal—that the laws of falling bodies, of electromagnetic radiation, of gravitational attraction, hold with the same constants everywhere and always. Murray’s law seemed to belong to that club. But biological systems are shaped by multiple selection pressures, each with its own intrinsic scaling, and there is no guarantee that they will cooperate to produce a tidy mathematical form. The branching exponent is not a law of nature; it is a signature of compromise, and the compromise shifts with the local demands of the tissue. Marchesi’s analysis elevates that variability from an unexplained nuisance to a predictable consequence of well‑characterised physiology.

The paper also demonstrates something subtle about the role of topology and dissipation. When wall‑tissue cost is included, the optimal number of branches into which a parent vessel should divide becomes bounded—not infinite, not just binary. Marchesi shows that under typical physiological constraints, the optimum settles at small finite integers, and that binary bifurcation, the most common pattern in mammalian vasculature, emerges naturally as the physiological minimum when steric (crowding) constraints are added. The ubiquitous “Y”‑shaped fork is not just an accident of developmental biology; it reflects a deep optimisation principle that can only be seen when the wall’s metabolic bill is factored into the calculation.

What does this mean for the clinician, the bioengineer, or the comparative physiologist? It means that the same framework that explains why mouse arteries branch the way they do can in principle be extended to plant xylem, insect tracheae, or even synthetic microfluidic networks. If a branching structure is lined by living cells that consume energy, its geometry cannot be understood by fluid mechanics alone. One must account for the metabolism of the pipes.

The road ahead is clear, even if the exact timeline remains uncertain. Marchesi’s static wall‑tissue mechanism closes a third of the empirical gap. The next generation of models will need to incorporate pulsatile wave mechanics alongside wall metabolism, possibly through a fully dynamical cost functional. Those models will be less elegant than Murray’s original cubic law, but they will be truer to the living, breathing, vibrating vessels that constitute the arterial tree. In that direction lies a theory of biological transport that is not universal in the physicist’s sense, but richly, locally, biologically optimal. And that, perhaps, is the deeper lesson: in living systems, the most fundamental laws are often the ones that refuse to be written as a single number.
