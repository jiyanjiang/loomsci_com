Conformal Floquet Dynamics Come to Life on a Quantum Processor

May 28, 2026 Quantum Physics Source: arXiv:2605.27530

We think of quantum many‑body systems as rivers of entanglement—always flowing, always rearranging, yet rarely showing us the deep currents that organise their motion. A preprint (arXiv:2605.27530) from a team led by Qiang Miao at the Duke Quantum Center, with Liang‑Hong Mo at Princeton University and Bastien Lapierre at the École Normale Supérieure in Paris, has just managed to glimpse those hidden currents on a real quantum processor. The team prepared a chain of sixteen trapped‑ion qubits in its most delicate possible state—the critical point where order and disorder are perfectly balanced—and then drove it with a periodic pulse that makes the fabric of spacetime itself behave like a cosmic metronome. The physics they observed, conformal Floquet dynamics, had previously existed only in theorists’ equations. Now, for the first time, it exists in hardware.

That leap from the blackboard to the blinking lights of a quantum chip required a double act of engineering. The first act was initialisation. Most quantum simulations start from simple states—all spins up, or an unentangled product of local states—because those are easy to prepare. But the physics the team wanted to explore demanded a starting point that is anything but simple: a many‑body critical state, where correlations stretch across the whole system and the tiniest perturbation can spark a phase transition. To create this near‑magical object they used a logarithmic‑depth quantum circuit inspired by multi‑scale entanglement renormalisation (MERA). Think of a photographer adjusting a zoom lens step by step, from a wide‑angle shot of the whole chain down to the finest detail of a single spin. The MERA circuit does the same with entanglement: each layer of two‑qubit gates entangles spins at increasingly larger scales, building up the critical ground state of the transverse‑field Ising model layer by layer. At the end, what sits inside the processor is not a collection of atoms but a unified, scale‑free object—a snapshot of a system poised exactly on the boundary between ordered and disordered.

The second act was the drive itself. With the critical state in hand, the team applied a Floquet protocol: a repeating sequence of two slightly different spin‑interaction Hamiltonians, (H0) and (H1), played one after the other like the downbeat and upbeat of a drum. Crucially, there are two parameter regimes. In the heating phase, each cycle pumps energy into the chain, causing the memory of the initial state to fade away in a characteristic pattern. In the non‑heating phase, the energy remains bounded and the system experiences periodic revivals, almost as if time were folding back on itself. The boundary between these regimes is not an accident; it is a direct consequence of conformal symmetry—the same mathematical structure that governs magnets at their critical point and even describes the vibrations of strings in high‑energy theory. The team’s quantum chip, running on Quantinuum’s Helios trapped‑ion processor, was able to cross that boundary at will.

Energy either soars exponentially or stays steady depending on how the quantum system is driven. This distinction reveals whether the device heats up uncontrollably or remains stable, a key step toward reliable quantum computers. (Source: arXiv:2605.27530)

To measure the heating, the researchers looked at the Loschmidt echo, a quantity that asks: if you let the system evolve and then attempt to rewind it, how much of the original state do you recover? In the heating phase, the echo decays exponentially with the number of Floquet cycles. What makes this decay special is that its rate is supposed to be universal—it depends on only a single number, the central charge, which labels the “family” of critical behaviour. For the Ising model, the central charge is (c = 1/2). The team measured (c = 0.575 \pm 0.036), a value within about 15% of the theoretical prediction. This is not exact agreement, but it is a genuine milestone: a macroscopic signature of conformal symmetry extracted from a noisy quantum machine through a thousand measurement shots and careful statistical bootstrapping.

A phase diagram reveals two distinct behaviors—heating and non-heating—in the quantum system's dynamics. This distinction helps researchers understand and control thermalization, a key step toward stable quantum computing. (Source: arXiv:2605.27530)

The story gets richer when we look not just at the echo’s decay but at where the energy goes in space. According to the continuum field‑theory description of the heating phase, energy should pile up at specific locations that are determined by the deformation parameters of the Floquet drive. The experimental data, averaged over just 16 qubits and 16 Floquet cycles, show exactly this spatial concentration, matching the CFT prediction within the uncertainty. In contrast, the non‑heating phase shows a flat, bounded energy profile—no hot spots, no runaway heating. The machine, in other words, is following the script written by conformal symmetry.

Yet this script is not yet final‑draft. An important question raised by earlier theoretical work on Floquet CFT concerns the Trotter decomposition used to implement the time‑evolution operators on the quantum chip. Each continuous evolution segment is approximated by a staircase of discrete two‑qubit gates. As Wen and colleagues showed in their foundational Floquet CFT paper, the detailed way the continuum limit is approached matters; the current experiment uses a single second‑order Trotter step per half‑cycle, and we do not yet know how sensitively the CFT predictions depend on that choice. Without a systematic study of step‑size effects, it is plausible that part of the 15% discrepancy in the central charge—or the wiggles in the echo data—arises from this discretisation rather than from genuine finite‑size physics.

A second frontier concerns the size of the system itself. With only sixteen qubits, finite‑size corrections can shift the apparent central charge by amounts that rival the statistical uncertainty. Previous entanglement‑scaling studies by Qiang Miao and collaborators on quantum phase transitions emphasised that finite‑size scaling is essential to distinguish true critical behaviour from lattice‑size artifacts. The present experiment uses a single fixed chain length of sixteen, leaving open the question of how the extracted (c) would evolve if the number of qubits were doubled or quadrupled—would it converge cleanly to (1/2), or drift away? Sun and colleagues, who recently observed CFT spectra in a different quantum simulator, demonstrated that extracting central charge from small systems demands careful accounting of boundary conditions and finite‑size corrections; their work suggests that the 15% offset seen here may not be entirely statistical.

A third question, subtly insistent, comes from the universality claim itself. The team tested their protocol on a non‑integrable model—an interacting Ising chain with an additional transverse coupling—and found that the echo dynamics matched the CFT predictions equally well. That is a strong sign. However, it turns out that the specific model chosen, with parameters (J = 1), (g = 0.6066), and (\Gamma = 0.25), is effectively integrable by the sophisticated standards of modern many‑body physics. If the protocol truly extracts only CFT data, it should work for any model in the Ising universality class, integrable or not. The fact that the “non‑integrable” test model is actually integrable means the most stringent check of universality—against true chaos—remains undone. This is not a failure of the experiment, which by its nature must start somewhere; it is a clear target for the next iteration.

These limitations do not dim the achievement. Building a MERA circuit on a trapped‑ion processor is a tour de force of circuit compilation and gate calibration. Watching a heap of incoherent atoms organise themselves into the ghost of a continuous field theory, then heat up in a pattern that a blackboard calculation predicted eight years ago, is the sort of moment that makes one believe quantum simulation is more than a buzzword. The river of entanglement is no longer invisible; we can see its currents stir.

What the conformal Floquet framework opens, beyond this single experiment, is a window onto non‑equilibrium quantum field theory. The same platform can be tuned to different central charges—the XX model’s (c = 1), for example—and to drives that break conformal symmetry altogether, probing the murky transition between heating and non‑heating phases where chaos and integrability wrestle. And because the protocol is scalable, longer chains and deeper circuits may soon allow finite‑size scaling and Trotter‑step optimisation to be tackled head‑on, transforming the qualitative observation of conformal heating into a precision instrument.

Perhaps one day, when a quantum processor prepared in a critical state is shaken by a carefully shaped Floquet drive, we will not be merely confirming a theoretical prediction. We will be eavesdropping on a conversation between entanglement and the geometry of spacetime, listening for the first whisper of a language we are only beginning to learn. The river is flowing. For the first time, we can hear its rhythm.

— Yanjiang
