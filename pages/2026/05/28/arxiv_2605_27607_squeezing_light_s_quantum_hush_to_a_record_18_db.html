Squeezing Light's Quantum Hush to a Record 18 dB

May 28, 2026 Matter Science Source: arXiv:2605.27607

Imagine you could silence the random fizz that saturates every beam of light—the irreducible quantum noise that sets the ultimate sensitivity of gravitational wave detectors, optical atomic clocks and secure quantum networks. For decades physicists have pursued vacuum squeezing, a subtle trick that redistributes quantum uncertainty: quiet one property of a light field below the vacuum floor, at the price of amplifying another. Now a team led by Mengjie Yu at the University of California, Berkeley, has shrunk that trick onto a single photonic chip, reporting a world‑record 18 decibels of squeezing in a thin‑film lithium niobate waveguide (arXiv:2605.27607). As the authors themselves write, the result is “the highest squeezing reported for any integrated photonic platform.”

The chip at the heart of this achievement is barely longer than a thumbnail. Fabricated in periodically poled lithium niobate—a crystal whose strong second‑order optical nonlinearity has long been a workhorse of quantum optics—the 1.6‑centimeter waveguide was designed by a collaborative team spanning Berkeley, MIT and the University of Southern California. First author Tushar Sanjay Karnik, together with Mengjie Yu and colleagues, pumped the structure with laser light and measured the noise of the outgoing field. In one quadrature of the electromagnetic field—roughly speaking, an amplitude‑like component—the noise was suppressed to 18 decibels below the vacuum state, while the conjugate quadrature was amplified by 20 decibels. That is anti‑squeezing, the mandatory companion of squeezing, a quantum partnership dictated by the uncertainty principle.

Vacuum squeezing is not a filter. It’s a reshaping of the vacuum itself. In empty space the electric field never rests; its amplitude and phase fluctuate randomly. By driving the waveguide’s nonlinearity, the team effectively turned the device into a parametric amplifier that redistributes those fluctuations, making one quadrature quieter and the other louder. The process is the optical analogue of squeezing a balloon: press on one side and the other bulges out, with the total volume—the product of uncertainties—conserved. Unlike a balloon, however, the quantum version doesn’t simply deform; it generates genuine entanglement between the two field components, a non‑classical resource that underpins proposals for continuous‑variable quantum computing and sensing.

Measuring squeezing on a chip is tricky. Light loses its squeezed purity as it exits the waveguide because coupling imperfections inject ordinary vacuum noise, diluting the non‑classical character. To recover the true on‑chip performance, the Berkeley team built a distributed model that traces the evolution of the noise along the device, accounting for propagation loss, phase noise and the parametric gain simultaneously. They then subjected every uncertain input—facet losses, phase jitter, the nonlinear strength—to a Monte Carlo analysis, producing the first assumption‑free 95‑percent confidence interval for an integrated squeezer. The final numbers: an on‑chip squeezing between −18.96 and −17.25 decibels, and anti‑squeezing between +19.96 and +21.35 decibels. It’s a statistical statement of unprecedented rigour, grounding the record figure in a transparent probability.

The inference, however, is not without its subtleties. Earlier chip‑scale squeezers, such as the one demonstrated by Arge and coworkers using modal phase matching on the same material platform, achieved more modest squeezing levels but required fewer calibration steps. The calibration cleverly employed bidirectional second‑harmonic generation measurements—a technique recently advanced by Wu and colleagues to break degeneracies between input and output coupling losses. The extracted facet losses were then fed into the distributed squeezing model, allowing the team to trace the noise evolution along the full waveguide length with high confidence.

Still, what the Berkeley team has delivered is a milestone that shifts the landscape. Integrated squeezers are the missing link for scalable continuous‑variable quantum technologies. They promise to supply the compressed vacuum states needed for error‑corrected quantum computing, for entanglement distribution in quantum communication networks, and for squeezing‑enhanced sensing that could push gravitational wave observatories beyond their current shot‑noise limits. The present device, with waveguide losses hovering at a few tenths of a decibel per centimeter, already operates in a regime where multi‑stage cascading becomes feasible. Extending the chip length and pushing material losses even lower—ambitions that are actively being pursued through improved fabrication—would bring the achievable squeezing into the range demanded by fault‑tolerant computing. The team's simulations suggest that pushing losses down to about 1.3 dB per metre—already demonstrated in other lithium niobate resonators—could unlock squeezing levels exceeding 30 dB, meeting the stringent demands of fault‑tolerant computing.

The road from a benchtop record to a deployable component is paved with engineering challenges: reducing scattering at the waveguide sidewalls, perfecting the adaptive poling that keeps the phase‑matching condition uniform along the entire length, and eventually integrating the pump laser on the same chip. But the Berkeley result proves that lithium niobate—a material with decades of history in classical telecommunications—can now also carry the quietest light ever produced on an integrated platform. As the fabrication techniques mature, squeezed vacuum may soon flow through circuits the size of a fingernail, opening a new era for quantum sensors and computers. For a field that has long chased the dream of noise‑free measurement, that quiet hum is starting to sound like a promise.
