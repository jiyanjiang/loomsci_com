How Gene Networks Learn to Talk: A Language Game for Non-Human Intelligence

Drafted May 19, 2026 · Published May 23, 2026 Artificial Intelligence Source: arXiv:2605.16321

Imagine standing before a shimmering web of genes — a network of molecular switches that regulate each other, remember past states, and make decisions without a single neuron. You want to ask it something. Not to simulate it on a computer, but to engage it in conversation — to have it speak in its own voice, on its own terms. Until now, that would have sounded like a category mistake. A preprint (arXiv:2605.16321) from Yanbo Zhang and Michael Levin at the Allen Discovery Center at Tufts University, with ties to the Wyss Institute at Harvard, argues that we have been thinking about the problem the wrong way. The path to dialogue with non‑neural intelligence, they propose, is not to teach the system our language, but to discover a game that both sides can play — and then let the game itself become the language.

This is Wittgenstein’s insight, transplanted from philosophy into the laboratory of dynamical systems. Meaning, he argued, lives in use: words acquire their sense not from dictionaries but from the roles they play in human activities, the “language games” we participate in. Zhang and Levin take that seriously. If a gene regulatory network (GRN) — a circuit of interacting genes that controls development and physiology — can be treated as the player in a carefully designed game, then its actions within that game become meaningful. Play becomes speech.

The reservoir that speaks. The central mechanism is a marriage of reservoir computing and reinforcement learning. The GRN’s internal dynamics are frozen and treated as a nonlinear reservoir: a complex, high‑dimensional dynamical system whose natural time‑evolution serves as a computational substrate. Unlike a typical neural network, the reservoir itself is never modified. Instead, only a linear input encoder and a linear output decoder are trained, using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), to maximize reward on a given reinforcement‑learning task. The GRN’s internal tangles of activation and repression, in other words, become the nonlinear core that a very thin trainable wrapper learns to steer.

Think of it like this: you are given a bucket of water into which someone has placed an intricate, hidden maze of pipes and baffles. You cannot change the plumbing, but you can learn which way to pour a stream of sand so that it emerges from a particular spout. With enough practice, the bucket appears to “decide” which spout to use, even though the plumbing itself never changed. The GRN is that bucket; the trained encoder–decoder is the hand that learns how to pour. The upshot is that the system’s internal dynamics are not overwritten — they are respected, and through the lens of game performance, interpreted.

The game as lingua franca. How can the decisions of a gene network ever mean anything to a human? The key move is to embed the dialogue inside a game whose rules are understood by both parties, even if their internal representations are radically different. The paper offers Tic‑Tac‑Toe as a crisp illustration. A human sees the board as a spatial grid where three in a row wins. An alien whose cognition is numerical might perceive the same game as selecting numbers from a magic square: three numbers that sum to 15 win. The two players inhabit completely incommensurable worlds, yet their actions are perfectly aligned because the game itself encodes the logic that constrains them both. The game serves as a lingua franca — a common tongue that needs no translation.

Zhang and Levin extend this idea into a full pipeline. A human prompt enters a large language model, which acts as a router: it selects, from a repertoire of reinforcement‑learning environments, the game whose semantics best match the user’s intent. Then, rather than just picking a game, the language model designs a specific environmental state — a concrete situation within that game — such that the most rational action for a well‑trained agent would be the response the user is looking for. The GRN agent, whose policy was previously shaped through training on that game, takes its action. Finally, a translator module interprets that action back into human language, again using the game’s rich semantic structure. The gene network has “spoken” not by forming words, but by behaving within a world where behavior carries unambiguous meaning.

Humans see Tic-Tac-Toe as a spatial game, while aliens interpret it as numbers summing to 15—yet both play the same game. This shared game acts as a universal translator, allowing meaningful communication between incompatible minds. (Source: arXiv:2605.16321)

The framework aligns a system's internal dynamics with human goals, creating a two-way translation between natural language and actions. This allows people to talk to and understand non-human systems as easily as talking to another person. (Source: arXiv:2605.16321)

What properties make a system talkative? The team tested their framework across a wide range of gene regulatory network architectures — varying their connectivity, regulatory logic, and dynamical properties — and across a battery of reinforcement‑learning tasks spanning classic control, locomotion, maze navigation, and even Atari‑style environments. Two striking patterns emerged.

First, well‑trained agents of very different internal design tend to converge on behaviorally similar policies. Whether the reservoir is a dense regulatory network of a few dozen genes or a simpler feed‑forward architecture, the actions they take in the same game become nearly indistinguishable once both have been optimized to high reward. This is what the authors call the Rational Agent Hypothesis: the idea that sufficiently competent agents, regardless of their internal makeup, will behave similarly because they are all shaped by the same external reward structure.

It is a finding that resonates powerfully with a sister idea from deep learning — the Platonic Representation Hypothesis, which holds that large neural networks converge on a shared latent representation of the world simply because they are trained to model the same reality (Huh et al., 2024). Here, the convergence is behavioral rather than directly representational, but the philosophical thread is the same: a strong enough external scaffold can pull radically different minds toward a common pattern of action.

Second, not all gene networks are equally easy to talk to. The team systematically measured how specific GRN properties — like the level of clustering, the presence of certain feedback motifs, or the degree of randomness in the wiring — affect learning speed and final reward. Some properties act as inductive biases that facilitate learning, making the network a more cooperative conversational partner; others suppress performance, as if the reservoir’s own structure resists being coaxed into the game. In effect, they have begun to map the temperament of different dynamical systems, quantifying what makes one biological circuit more “open” to dialogue than another.

The PPO shadow and the question of convergence. An important question, sharpened by earlier work on policy optimization (Schulman et al., 2017), is whether the behavioral convergence the authors observe is a deep, universal property of any optimizing agent, or whether it is partly an artifact of the particular algorithm used — Proximal Policy Optimization. PPO is known for encouraging stable, gradual updates that might steer different initial policies toward similar regions of behavior space. The paper does not perform the ablation studies that would isolate the role of the optimizer from the role of the game structure itself. So the Rational Agent Hypothesis, while evocative, remains to be tested under other families of reinforcement‑learning algorithms.

Similarly, reading the work alongside the Platonic Representation Hypothesis reveals a conceptual gap. Behavioral convergence — two agents taking the same actions — does not automatically imply representational convergence — that their internal states encode the world in the same way. The gene network’s “thoughts,” if they can be called that, are encoded in high‑dimensional trajectories of gene expression. Knowing that those trajectories lead to the same button press tells us they are aligned on the output, but not whether they share a geometry deeper down. Probing that geometry, perhaps with tools developed for neural network interpretability, remains a task for the future.

What it means for the rest of us. At its heart, this framework is an attempt to answer a surprisingly practical question: how do you talk to a dynamical system that was never designed to communicate? The answer is not to anthropomorphize it, not to bolt a language model onto it and pretend the model’s eloquence is the system’s own. The answer is to meet the system where it already lives, in the space of forces and flows, and to build a bridge out of shared experience — the experience of playing the same game, pursuing the same reward.

The implications reach well beyond gene networks. Microbial consortia, fungal mycelia, synthetic protocells, even materials with memory — any system whose internal dynamics can be harnessed as a reservoir could, in principle, be brought into a language game. The game does not need to be a video game; it could be a physical task, a pattern‑recognition puzzle, a chemical oscillation to be tracked. The point is that the game provides a semantic scaffold, and the system, through its own lawful behavior, learns to occupy a role within it.

Perhaps one day we will ask a colony of bacteria whether it prefers one environmental condition over another by inviting it to “play” a game whose moves correspond to metabolic choices. The response will not be words, but it will be an answer — an action that, in the context of the game, means something. The framework Zhang and Levin have assembled is, in this sense, less a technical protocol than a change in philosophical stance: if meaning truly resides in use, then the barrier between speaker and world is not the absence of a common tongue, but the presence of a common game. And the gene network, it turns out, knows more games than we suspected.
