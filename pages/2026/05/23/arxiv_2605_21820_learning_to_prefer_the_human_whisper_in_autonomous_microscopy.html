Learning to Prefer: The Human Whisper in Autonomous Microscopy

Drafted May 22, 2026 · Published May 23, 2026 Artificial Intelligence Source: arXiv:2605.21820

Here is a question that unsettles the gleaming promise of artificial intelligence in the laboratory: what if the most sophisticated autonomous microscope, trained on terabytes of data, still cannot see the physics that an experienced researcher recognizes in a single glance? A preprint (arXiv:2605.21820) from a team led by Yongtao Liu at Oak Ridge National Laboratory suggests not only that this is the case, but that the remedy may require a quiet revolution in how machines learn to do science — one that replaces the tyranny of the scoreboard with the subtlety of human taste.

For years, the dream of self-driving laboratories has rested on a deceptively simple premise: define a number — a scalar objective — that captures what “good” looks like, then let an algorithm hunt for the conditions that maximize that number. In microscopy, you might ask the algorithm to find regions with the largest electric response, the sharpest piezoelectric signal, the highest crystallographic perfection. Bayesian optimization, a powerful machine-learning framework, will then steer the measurement beam across the sample, refining its search with each new data point, zeroing in on the highest-scoring coordinates as if following the scent of a hidden prey. The logic is elegant, the mathematics robust, and the results often impressive.

But there is a catch.

The catch is that most scientifically worthwhile discoveries do not announce themselves with a single, tidy number. Ask a materials physicist to describe a “good” ferroelectric domain wall, and she will begin with the angle of polarization on either side, the character of the bound charge, the topology of the wall’s intersection with the surface. She will note whether the wall is head-to-head or tail-to-tail, whether it is likely to be conducting or insulating, whether its internal structure betrays a proximity to a phase transition. None of this complexity is reducible to a single scalar that you can plug into an optimizer without losing something vital — the expert’s integrated, interdisciplinary judgment, honed over a career of staring at images and thinking about what they mean.

This is the problem that Liu and his colleagues — including Ralph Bulanadi, Jefferey Baxter, Arpan Biswas, Hiroshi Funakubo, Dennis Meier, Jan Schultheiß, and Rama Vasudevan — set out to solve. Their creation, which they call Deep Kernel Pairwise Learning (DKPL), reimagines the autonomous experiment not as a blind optimization but as a conversation between the machine and a human mentor. The human does not provide a score. Instead, the human is shown two microscope images and asked a single, almost primitive question: which one is more scientifically promising?

The machine’s job is then to learn, from a modest number of such comparisons, a latent utility function — a mathematical representation of the expert’s tacit knowledge — that can guide subsequent measurements. The process feels more like an apprenticeship than a calibration. The human says, essentially, “this domain wall looks more interesting than that one,” and the machine gradually internalizes what “interesting” means, even if the human herself could not articulate it in a tidy formula.

To make the comparison process work with the high-dimensional complexity of microscopy images, the team embedded the comparisons within a deep kernel learning framework. Without getting lost in the architectural weeds: a deep neural network first crunches each image into a compact fingerprint, and then a Gaussian process model compares these fingerprints to infer which regions of the sample the expert would want to explore next. Crucially, the team added two refinements: confidence weighting, which lets the machine acknowledge when its predictions are uncertain, and equal-preference support, which handles the awkward but common situation where the expert truly cannot choose between two images because both look equally interesting, or equally uninspiring.

The results, tested on a model material system with a known ground truth — the loop-area response in band-excitation piezoresponse force microscopy on a lead zirconate titanate sample — were striking. The full DKPL framework, armed with both confidence weighting and equal-preference support, produced a utility map that closely mirrored the actual distribution of scientifically meaningful signal. When an autonomous experimenter used DKPL to select measurement points, the histogram of measured values shifted dramatically toward the most interesting regions of the sample, while a version of DKPL stripped of these refinements merely wandered, gathering a far larger fraction of uninteresting data. The machine had learned, in a concrete sense, what the expert cared about.

Then came the deeper test. The team turned DKPL loose on real, unsolved problems in ferroelectric domain-wall physics. In bismuth ferrite, they asked the expert to compare images of domain walls with different characteristic angles. The system rapidly learned to distinguish walls with strong 180° character — the kind where the polarization flips completely, creating a crisp, energetically costly boundary — from those with more gradual, lower-angle character. When DKPL was transferred, without retraining, to a fresh region of the same sample, its predictions of which walls the expert would find most interesting correlated tightly with the ground-truth angular measurements. The method had abstracted not just a map, but a concept: domain-wall sharpness as a proxy for scientific curiosity.

In erbium manganite, the challenge was subtler. The team wanted to find head-to-head and tail-to-tail domain walls — configurations where the polarization vectors point at each other or away from each other, respectively, creating bound charge and potentially exotic electronic behavior. These walls are rare, and their signature in standard amplitude images is ambiguous. Yet DKPL, trained on expert pairwise comparisons, learned to assign high utility to regions exhibiting strong tail-to-tail character and low utility to those with head-to-head character. The overlay images confirmed that the machine’s definition of “promising” converged with the physics that a domain-wall specialist would chase. This was not merely optimization; it was the faint stirring of judgment, acquired not from a labeled database but from a handful of side-by-side choices made by a human who knew what to look for.

Now, pause to appreciate what has — and has not — been accomplished. DKPL does not invent new physics from whole cloth. The materials under study were already well characterized; the domain walls themselves were not being discovered for the first time. The advance is methodological, not conceptual: a new way to encode human expertise as an active ingredient in the loop of autonomous experimentation, rather than treating it as a source of post-hoc interpretation. This is a quiet but consequential shift, because it acknowledges something that the hyper-automation rhetoric often obscures: that the most critical part of a discovery pipeline may not be the speed of the measurements but the quality of the questions.

And yet, a question raised by earlier work on preference-based learning lingers. In robotics, researchers developing systems like Pref-GUIDE (Ji et al., arXiv:2508.07126) found that human evaluators are themselves unstable measuring instruments. A tired scientist may judge an image differently on a Friday afternoon than on a Monday morning. Personal tastes shift; standards drift; the expert’s own understanding evolves over the course of an experiment, as she learns more about the sample even while teaching the machine about it. None of these temporal inconsistencies are addressed in the current DKPL framework. The latent utility function the machine learns is only as reliable as the stability of the human behind the comparisons, and that stability is not guaranteed.

This is not a fatal flaw but a generative tension. It points toward the next phase of development, where the autonomous system might need to model not just the expert’s preferences but the evolution of those preferences over time — a meta-preference learner that can distinguish between a genuine shift in scientific insight and a momentary lapse in attention. It also hints at a deeper philosophical pivot: the self-driving laboratory, as it matures, begins to look less like a faster version of a human technician and more like a collaborator engaged in a reciprocal relationship, in which the human and the machine each learn from the other.

What does this mean for the broader dream of autonomous scientific discovery? We are accustomed to thinking of automation as the progressive removal of the human from the loop. The automobile replaced the horseman; the compiler replaced the assembly programmer; the search algorithm replaced the librarian’s catalogue. In each case, progress meant making the machine sufficient unto itself. But DKPL suggests a different arc, one in which automation does not eliminate the human but rather amplifies the subtlety of human input, scaling expert judgment across vast parameter spaces that no single scientist could ever personally survey. It is not that the microscope needs less of the human; it is that it needs a different kind of human contribution — less about steering and more about valuing.

Perhaps, a decade from now, when a self-driving microscopy platform at a user facility narrows down from a billion possible measurement locations to two, and then pings a physicist at her home institution with a question — “which of these domain walls should I investigate?” — she will recognize in that query not a failure of automation but its highest achievement. The machine will have learned to prefer, and in learning to prefer, it will have absorbed the one thing that numbers alone can never encode: the human capacity to find something worth looking at, before anyone can say exactly why.
