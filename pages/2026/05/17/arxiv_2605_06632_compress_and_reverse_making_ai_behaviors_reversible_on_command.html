Compress and Reverse: Making AI Behaviors Reversible on Command

Drafted May 08, 2026 · Published May 17, 2026 Artificial Intelligence Source: arXiv:2605.06632

When a large language model is fine-tuned on new data, the new behaviors it acquires spread throughout its billions of parameters like ink through water. You might teach it to answer helpfully, or to refuse certain requests, or to adopt a specific writing style — but you have no structural control over where that knowledge lives inside the model, and no clean way to turn it off at inference time without modifying weights.

A team led by Yuping Lin and Zhen Xiang has now proposed a method to do exactly that. Their preprint (arXiv:2605.06632) introduces a two-stage pipeline — Loss-Constrained Dual Descent (LCDD) followed by a soft prompt they call SFT-Eraser — that compresses a supervised fine-tuning (SFT) behavior into a sparse, localized subnetwork and then selectively reverses it using only a short input trigger. The work does not merely locate correlated circuits; it demonstrates causal necessity: without the carrier subnetwork, the behavior cannot be expressed, and reversing its activation cleanly removes the SFT-induced response.

Why Correlation Is Not Enough

Standard SFT works by updating model weights across all layers. The behavior spreads broadly, making it difficult to understand which parameters actually cause the new response. Existing interpretability methods, such as circuit attribution, identify subnetworks that are correlated with the behavior after training. Yet correlation does not imply causation: ablating those subnetworks often fails to suppress the behavior, because other parameters can compensate.

The key question Lin and colleagues ask is whether it is possible to deliberately construct a model where the SFT-induced behavior is confined to a sparse, mechanistically necessary subnetwork — what they call a carrier — and where that carrier can be turned on or off at inference time using a soft trigger, all without changing any weights.

Compressing Behavior into a Carrier

The first stage of their approach, LCDD, solves a constrained optimization problem. The idea is to simultaneously learn a binary routing mask (which parameters participate in the carrier) and the corresponding model weights, under an explicit utility budget that ensures the carrier remains small. LCDD uses a technique akin to dual descent: it alternates between updating the mask to minimize the number of active parameters, and updating the weights to preserve the target behavior, while a Lagrange multiplier enforces the trade-off.

A new technique compresses a learned behavior into a small, removable part of the model. This makes it possible to turn the behavior on or off with a simple trigger, enabling precise control over what the model does. (Source: arXiv:2605.06632)

The result is a sparse subnetwork — typically containing only a small fraction of the model's parameters — that alone suffices to reproduce the SFT-induced behavior. Components outside the carrier are returned to their base-model state, meaning the rest of the model remains unchanged. This is not a post-hoc attribution; it is an engineered compression. The carrier is causally necessary by construction: remove it, and the behavior disappears.

The Eraser

The second stage, SFT-Eraser, takes advantage of the carrier's structure. Rather than modifying weights, the team optimizes a short learnable prefix (a soft prompt) that, when prepended to the input, drives the activations inside the carrier toward the values they would have had in the base model — effectively reversing the SFT effect.

The critical insight is that this reversal only works because the behavior is localized. When the team applied the same trigger optimization to a standard SFT model (without the carrier), it failed. The sparse structure is the key precondition: without it, the trigger cannot find a compact set of activations to shift. As Lin and colleagues demonstrate through ablation, structure matters more than trigger design.

The full pipeline works as follows. During normal inference without the trigger, the carrier preserves the SFT behavior. With the trigger, carrier activations are driven toward the base model, and the behavior is suppressed. The model itself remains untouched — only the presence or absence of the soft trigger changes the output.

Results Across Behaviors and Models

The team tested their method on three categories of SFT-induced behaviors: safety alignment (refusing harmful requests), fixed-response patterns (e.g., always answering with a specific phrase), and stylistic changes (e.g., adopting a particular tone). Experiments were run on multiple model families, including LLaMA and other open-weight architectures.

In every case, LCDD produced sparse carriers that preserved the target behavior with high fidelity. The carriers typically involved fewer than 1% of the model's parameters. When triggered by SFT-Eraser, the SFT behavior was reversed with high accuracy — the model reverted to its base-model response on the same inputs. The reversal was specific: behaviors outside the carrier were unaffected.

Ablations confirmed that the sparse structure is the operative factor. When the team attempted the same trigger optimization on conventionally fine-tuned models — without the compressed carrier — the reversal failed, even when they used the same trigger length and optimization procedure. This provides direct evidence that the LCDD carrier is causally necessary: the behavior is tied to those specific parameters, and shifting their activations is sufficient to suppress it.

A New Basis for Selective Control

The significance of this work lies not in a single new capability but in a new conceptual framework. By making the causal structure of SFT-induced behaviors both visible and manipulable, Lin and colleagues open a pathway to selectively suppress unwanted behaviors in deployed models — without retraining, without weight modification, and without affecting other capabilities.

The approach does have limitations. The current method requires constructing the carrier during training; it does not apply post-hoc to already-deployed models that were fine-tuned conventionally. The behaviors tested are relatively clean; more complex or entangled behaviors may be harder to compress into sparse carriers. And the soft trigger, while effective in these experiments, could potentially be exploited if an adversary learns to suppress safety behaviors — though the trigger itself is a learned sequence that would need to be kept private.

Nonetheless, the paper establishes a principle: SFT-induced behaviors can be deliberately localized into causally necessary subnetworks, and those subnetworks can be controlled at inference time using input prompts alone. This points toward a future where model behavior is not a monolithic consequence of training, but a structured, controllable property — something that can be understood, localized, and, when necessary, reversed.
