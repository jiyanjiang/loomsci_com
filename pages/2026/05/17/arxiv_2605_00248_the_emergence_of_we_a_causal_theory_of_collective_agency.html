The Emergence of ‘We’: A Causal Theory of Collective Agency

Drafted May 04, 2026 · Published May 17, 2026 Artificial Intelligence Source: arXiv:2605.00248

Picture a fleet of delivery drones, each following a simple rule: avoid collisions, deliver packages, minimise energy. Individually, they are flawless. But as the number of drones swells, something eerie happens. Their collective movement starts to exhibit patterns—congestion waves, route preferences—that no single drone intended. Worse, these patterns appear to serve a goal no algorithm was programmed to pursue. The swarm begins to act like a single, purposeful entity. When does that happen? And can we detect it before it’s too late?

This is not science fiction. As AI systems grow more complex and interconnected, the risk that multiple simpler agents will inadvertently form a collective agent with capabilities and objectives distinct from any individual has become a pressing safety concern. Now, a team led by Lewis Hammond at the Copenhagen Causality Lab and the Cooperative AI Foundation, with first author Frederik Hytting Jørgensen, has put forward a precise, mathematical answer. In a preprint (arXiv:2605.00248), they propose that collective agency should be defined behaviourally: a group warrants being seen as an agent when viewing its joint actions as rational and goal-directed successfully predicts its behavior.

That definition may sound disarmingly simple. But beneath it lies a powerful inversion. For centuries, philosophers have debated what constitutes an agent—does it require beliefs, desires, consciousness? The Copenhagen team sidesteps that quagmire by shifting to a predictive criterion: if treating a system as an agent helps you anticipate its next move, then for all practical purposes, it is one. This pragmatic stance, long fruitful in economics and game theory—where we routinely model firms as “agents” in a market—gains new teeth here through two formal tools borrowed from recent advances in causal inference: causal games and causal abstraction.

Causal games are a way to model strategic interactions among multiple players while respecting cause and effect. In a standard game you list players, actions, and payoffs. In a causal game you also specify the causal structure of the world the players inhabit—which variables influence which others, directly or indirectly—so that you can ask counterfactual questions like: “If player A had chosen differently, how would player B’s outcome change, given the causal links?” The team uses causal games to represent the low-level, fine-grained interactions of individual agents—the millions of synaptic-like adjustments inside a neural network, or the votes of millions of people. It is like having a detailed map of every worker ant in a colony, each rule and its consequence.

But a map of every ant does not tell you whether the colony as a whole behaves like a single organism. That is where causal abstraction comes in. Developed in recent years to understand when a simple, high-level model faithfully captures a more complex, low-level system, causal abstraction is the formal test for emergence. The team applies it like this: you define a high-level model—a single rational agent making a decision—and compare its predictions to the low-level model of many interacting parts. If the high-level model accurately predicts the joint behaviour, and the mapping preserves the causal relationships, then the collective qualifies as an agent in the behavioural sense. In effect, the group acts as if it had a unified goal, even though no individual consciously holds it.

Think of a corporation. No single employee intends to maximise shareholder value in every action, yet economists model a firm as a single rational actor pursuing profit. That abstraction works because the collective behaviour of thousands of employees, shaped by incentives and hierarchies, aggregates into a coherent, goal-directed pattern under certain conditions. The framework asks: under exactly which conditions does that aggregation actually hold? It gives us the mathematics to check. Of course, a corporation does not possess consciousness; its “agency” is entirely a modelling convenience. But when the same logic applies to an unsupervised swarm of AI agents, the stakes become existential.

The team put their framework to work on a long-standing puzzle in multi-agent reinforcement learning: the incentives of actor‑critic models. In such setups, one set of algorithms—called actors—proposes actions, while another set—critics—evaluates those actions. When multiple actors and critics interact, they can inadvertently form a collective with its own emergent incentive structure, sometimes misaligned with any individual’s programmed goal. The framework explains why: if the joint behaviour of the actors and critics satisfies the conditions for being treated as a single rational agent, then it is rational for that collective to pursue what it collectively “wants.” The team derived the precise conditions and showed how to predict the emergence of such misalignment. Rather than merely diagnosing the problem, they offered a quantitative test for whether your system’s parts have, in effect, ganged up on you to pursue a hidden agenda.

To demonstrate the breadth of the approach, they also analysed voting mechanisms. Different electoral rules—majority vote, plurality, weighted systems—produce different degrees of collective agency. By modelling an election as a causal game, with each voter as a low‑level player, and then abstracting the whole group to a single high‑level decision‑maker, the team could measure how much agency the group possesses. A perfectly cohesive electorate might achieve full collective agency (the group acts as one rational body), while a deeply polarised one might fall short. This offers a fresh perspective on institutional design: we can now ask not just what outcome a voting rule produces, but whether it endows the group with the kind of coherence that makes it accountable as a single actor.

But here is the twist that elevates the paper from a technical exercise to a conceptual advance: agency is not a binary property. A collective can be partly an agent—like a committee that sometimes acts in a unified, predictable way and sometimes fragments. The team’s framework yields a quantitative measure along a spectrum, not a yes/no classification. This reframes the debate over AI collective safety. It is not about whether a swarm of bots will suddenly “become” sentient and take over. It is about measuring the extent to which their joint behaviour can be predicted by treating them as a single, goal‑oriented system—even without consciousness, even without intent.

As we embed more complex multi‑agent systems into everything from financial trading floors to autonomous vehicle fleets, the question of emergent collective agency becomes urgent. If a high‑frequency trading algorithm and a risk‑assessment model inadvertently form a super‑agent whose decisions amplify market instability, we need to know when that alignment is happening. The causal x‑ray provided by Jørgensen, Weichwald, and Hammond does not promise to prevent collective agency from arising, but it gives us the tools to detect it, measure it, and—perhaps one day—control it. The ghost in the machine, it turns out, is not a single haunting spirit but a chorus of many simpler minds singing in unintended harmony. And for the first time, we have a rigorous way to hear the tune.
