Vacuum Fluctuations Feel a Solid's Quantum Geometry

September 04, 2026 Matter Science Source: arXiv:2602.07116

Picture a superconducting circuit in a darkened cryostat, chilled to within a fraction of a degree of absolute zero. Everything is still, or so it seems. The electromagnetic field inside the circuit is never truly at rest: its zero-point fluctuations keep currents and voltages faintly breathing even in the vacuum. This is not life, only the unavoidable jitter written into the fabric of quantum fields.

In a preprint (arXiv:2602.07116) posted on arXiv, physicists Yugo Onishi and Liang Fu at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology propose that this quiet motion can be turned into a probe of one of solid-state physics' most subtle quantities: the quantum weight. Their central claim is that vacuum fluctuations produce a measurable force between a circuit and a nearby insulator, and that this force depends on the material's quantum geometry. To be clear, the vacuum does not literally feel; it exerts forces through irreducible zero-point motion.

The hidden geometry of a solid

To understand why, start with a familiar fact: at temperature zero, an LC circuit — an inductor and a capacitor — still has residual electromagnetic energy. This is not a technical imperfection. The uncertainty principle forbids the field from being exactly zero, so the circuit's charge and flux fluctuate around nothing. In every mode of the field there remains half a quantum of energy, a consequence of Planck's constant rather than of any thermal agitation.

Onishi and Fu ask what happens when a genuine quantum material is placed in close coupling with this fluctuating circuit. Their answer is that the circuit's zero-point energy acquires a correction that is extensive — it grows with the material's volume — and that the correction is proportional to the quantum weight.

Here is the key definition: the quantum weight measures the quantum fluctuation of electric polarization in a solid's many-body ground state. In the authors' words, the zero-point energy density is "proportional to the quantum fluctuation of electric polarization in the many-body ground state, a fundamental quantum geometric property of solids known as the quantum weight." If you imagine pinning all the electrons' center-of-mass coordinates, the quantum weight tells you the characteristic radius of their irreducible fuzz. Unlike a classical thermal jitter, this fuzz persists at absolute zero; it is encoded in the geometry of the many-body wavefunction rather than in any motion we could freeze out.

Quantum geometry entered condensed-matter physics as a way to describe how electronic wavefunctions vary across momentum space. The Berry curvature governs Hall currents and topological invariants; the quantum metric measures the distance between neighboring wavefunctions. In a many-body insulator, the trace of the quantum metric over all occupied states gives the quantum weight, up to a volume normalization factor. It sets an upper bound on how localized electrons can be and has been linked to superfluid stiffness in flat-band superconductors. The new proposal suggests that this abstract geometric quantity can become a directly mechanical observable. Materials with large quantum weights are therefore the most promising targets; flat-band systems are natural candidates because their electronic wavefunctions are unusually spread in real space.

A circuit that feels geometry

That fuzz, in turn, determines how strongly the material responds to the circuit's vacuum field. A larger quantum weight means stronger polarization fluctuations, which means a larger zero-point energy shift. The result is a force. Onishi and Fu call it the quantum geometric force.

The force comes in two varieties. First, there is a repulsive force between the circuit and the material. The fluctuating magnetic flux produced by the circuit's inductor acts like a tiny magnetic dipole, and the diamagnetic response of the electrons pushes back. Second, there is an attractive force on the capacitor plates themselves. The zero-point current that sloshes through the circuit also accumulates charge, and opposite charges on the plates pull together. Strikingly, this attractive force is proportional to the quantum weight, inversely proportional to the plate area, and independent of the distance between the plates.

That last property is worth pausing over. An ordinary electrostatic attraction between capacitor plates grows as they approach each other. Here the force does not. The distance independence is a clue that the effect is not a conventional electrostatic response but a genuine signature of the material's many-body ground-state geometry. The force is repulsive between the circuit and the sample, attractive on the plates, and the two signs arise from different vacuum channels: magnetic flux-induced diamagnetism and charge-induced electrostatic pull.

There is also a conceptual parallel to the original Casimir effect. Casimir recognized that boundary conditions reshape the vacuum modes between two metal plates; here the material's quantum geometry reshapes the vacuum modes inside a circuit. The comparison is useful, though the physical origin is not identical: the Casimir force arises from mode counting, while the quantum geometric force arises from how those modes couple to the material's internal polarization.

The experimental road

Could this be measured? The team sketches two possible routes. In one, the capacitor plate is made from a mechanical resonator, so that the quantum geometric force shifts its oscillation frequency. In another, a SQUID — a superconducting quantum interference device — sits on a cantilever, and its fluctuating flux feels the repulsive force when brought close to a sample. Neither requires a fully coherent quantum state; the effect is fundamentally about zero-point fluctuations, which are always present.

An important question raised by earlier work on quantum geometry concerns whether such abstract geometric quantities can ever show up in a direct, macroscopic way. In the context of nonlinear optical responses, quantum geometry was shown to leave fingerprints in the bulk photovoltaic effect of topological semimetals — work by Ahn and colleagues made that connection explicit. There, geometry appeared in how light pushed electrons. Here, the proposal extends the idea to how the vacuum pushes a macroscopic slab, with the advantage that a force measurement is static and potentially free of the disorder that complicates transport probes.

Still, there are open questions. In their analysis the energy shift is positive in the setups they consider, as it is proportional to the positive-definite quantum metric. The behavior near a gap-closing transition — where quantum geometry often diverges — could amplify the force, yet the precise scaling has not been fully mapped. These are not reasons to dismiss the idea; they are signposts for the experimental and theoretical work that will follow. The anticipated forces are not large; they are subtle enough to demand careful shielding from vibrations and temperature drift. The proposal, however, is tied to frequencies of a microwave resonator, where high-precision detection is well established.

Why it matters

For anyone who has watched a mirror hang without touching anything, the notion that the vacuum exerts forces is already familiar. What is new here is the direction of the inference. Instead of using a known force to probe geometry, the proposal uses the vacuum itself as a mechanical sensor. The circuit does not need to be perfect; it only needs to retain its zero-point fluctuations, which is inevitable in any quantum system.

The immediate human impact is conceptual more than practical. This work gives experimentalists a new handle on quantum geometry — a property that has been invoked to explain everything from flat-band superconductivity to quantized transport. If the quantum geometric force is confirmed, it would mean that a macroscopic piece of material can be weighed, pushed, and observed through its most abstract quantum attributes. If confirmed, the technique could become a standard diagnostic: place a sample near a superconducting resonator and map its quantum geometry without needing electrons to flow through it. That matters because traditional probes of quantum geometry often require delicate transport measurements or optical excitation, which can be hard in insulating or fragile materials.

The next step is not philosophical but concrete: build the resonator, put a material in place, and look for the predicted frequency shift. The beauty of the proposal is that it translates a highbrow geometric quantity into a laboratory force. What remains is to see whether the vacuum really can feel the shape of a solid.
