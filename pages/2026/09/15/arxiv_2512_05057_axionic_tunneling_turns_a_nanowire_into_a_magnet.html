Axionic Tunneling Turns a Nanowire into a Magnet

September 15, 2026 Matter Science Source: arXiv:2512.05057

Why stop at measuring electric current where a sharp tip hovers a few billionths of a metre above a sample? Should a modest voltage also be able to summon magnetism where there was none? A team led by Vidya Madhavan at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, with first author Saikat Banerjee at Rutgers University and collaborators Anuva Aishwarya, Fei Liu, Lin Jiao, Eugene J. Mele, and Piers Coleman, reports evidence that the answer is yes. Their work appears in a preprint (arXiv:2512.05057) on arXiv.

Think of the experiment as a kind of atomic-scale stethoscope. The team positioned a nanowire of samarium hexaboride, a topological Kondo insulator, just above an antiferromagnetic crystal built from iron and tellurium. In a standard scanning tunneling microscope (STM), the voltage between tip and sample controls the current that tunnels across the vacuum gap. Here, the same voltage appears to control something more surprising: the magnetic orientation of the very last atoms of the tip.

A stethoscope, of course, listens rather than acts. In this junction, the electric field does act — through a response that is neither purely electrical nor purely magnetic.

The trick hinges on an axion-like magnetoelectric response. A topological insulator is a material that refuses to conduct through its interior yet carries protected electronic states along its surface. This refusal is not a matter of will, but a consequence of topological protection: the bulk is gapped, while the boundary must remain conducting. In certain strongly correlated systems, those surface states generate an extra coupling between electric and magnetic fields, an axionic E·B term in the electromagnetic response.

That coupling is the condensed-matter echo of a particle-physics idea. In practical terms, an electric field penetrating the material should produce a magnetization, and a magnetic field should produce a charge polarization. The two are not independent but locked together by the same mathematical term that describes the hypothetical axion of high-energy physics.

The surface states involved often behave like massless Dirac quasiparticles, with a linear relation between energy and momentum. For a Kondo insulator such as samarium hexaboride, strong interactions are thought to alter those Dirac states: the surface-state node develops a subtle gap that remains buried beneath the Fermi surface, leaving the surface metallic while granting it axionic character.

Why has this been so difficult to see? Most searches for axionic electrodynamics have relied on bulk optical or transport probes, where the signature can be subtle and contested. A tunnel junction offers a radically local alternative, sensitive to electronic structure on the scale of individual surface atoms.

To look for it, the team measured differential conductance — the change in tunneling current for a small change in voltage — which is a standard way to map the local density of electronic states. The measurements delivered a striking voltage-induced magnetization. With the tip and sample held at millivolt-scale biases, the spin polarization of the final atoms reversed sign when the voltage reversed. The magnitude, tunability, and reversibility of the signal all point to axion-like coupling.

It is a little like having the entire tip synchronized to a single magnetic “time zone” adjusted by the voltage, rather than letting the spin alignment of the last atoms drift out of step with the junction. But the analogy can only go so far: unlike a time zone, the induced polarization does not sit passively; it tracks the applied field in a distinctly voltage-odd way.

The antiferromagnetic surface provided an exacting ruler. Its iron moments form a canted magnetic pattern with both in-plane and out-of-plane components, arranged into stripes roughly 7.6 angstroms apart. By recording how the tunneling conductance varied along a line cutting across these stripes at a temperature of 1.7 kelvin, the team could read the spin contrast imprinted by the tip.

Magnetic stripes on an iron crystal become visible as a voltage-controlled signal when scanned with a special microscope needle. This matters because it gives scientists a new way to detect and steer unusual magnetism in future quantum devices. (Source: arXiv:2512.05057)

Even more telling, the team mapped the Fourier components of the conductance signal. One component tracked the antiferromagnetic wave vector, while another followed the tellurium lattice as a built-in reference. The ratio between these components became a sensitive indicator of spin contrast.

The decisive signature is voltage-odd behavior. The conductance maps reveal a component that changes sign with the applied voltage while tracking the antiferromagnetic wave vector. If the tip response were simply a static magnetic moment, reversing the voltage would not be expected to reverse the tunneling asymmetry in such a tightly correlated fashion.

In this picture, most of the signal is not a pre-existing magnetization. Rather, it is related to how an electric field inside the topological insulator can draw in a magnetic response — eventually causing the final atoms of the tip to adopt a voltage-tuned spin polarization.

The spin contrast also fades steeply as the sample warms, dropping near 10 kelvin, the proposed onset scale for the axion-like response. That temperature dependence strengthens the case that the effect is not merely a trivial remnant magnetic texture.

Where the axion hides

Why should a Kondo insulator produce this? At weak interactions, the surface of a topological insulator acts as a domain wall across which the Dirac mass changes sign, yielding gapless surface states. For strong interactions, a different kind of wall can develop: the mass acquires an imaginary part, and the surface gains axionic properties. This is not merely a formal trick. The imaginary mass encodes precisely the E·B coupling that turns an electric field into a magnetization.

Saying that the mass becomes imaginary can sound like an equation has wandered into a fairy tale. But here it has a concrete meaning: the surface node becomes gapped just below the Fermi surface, so the material remains metallic while still displaying the anomalous response that defines axion electrodynamics.

A local probe for a global phenomenon

The stakes go beyond one material. Demonstrating axion-like physics in a tunnel junction would give researchers a new way to test a phenomenon that has been sought mainly through bulk measurements. That, in turn, could sharpen the distinction between genuine axionic response and the many ways a strongly correlated magnet can imitate it.

But why stop at a tunnel junction? Should we consider using the effect to write magnetic information without applying a magnetic field? The millivolt-scale control of spin polarization observed here opens a door toward voltage-driven spintronics, where electric fields rather than currents or external magnets manipulate spin. That would be a significant simplification for future devices.

The authors do not claim to have found the particle-physics axion. They present the voltage-odd signal as evidence consistent with axion-like E·B coupling, and they argue that the data strongly constrain a conventional static-magnetism interpretation. The distinction matters: axionic physics is not merely another form of magnetism, but a fundamentally different way for electric and magnetic fields to communicate.

Odd-voltage signals reveal a magnetic stripe pattern that almost disappears near 10 kelvin. This sharp cutoff hints at exotic, particle-like tunneling in a strange quantum material. (Source: arXiv:2512.05057)

The experiments are likely just beginning. If the interpretation holds, a scanning tunneling microscope becomes a small-scale laboratory for axion electrodynamics, where electric fields twist spin texture and voltage replaces current as the control knob. The road ahead involves testing the same protocol on other topological magnets and pushing measurements to even cleaner surfaces. Perhaps one day, when experimentalists design next-generation spin-sensitive probes, they will reach for a nanowire of samarium hexaboride and simply turn a dial.
