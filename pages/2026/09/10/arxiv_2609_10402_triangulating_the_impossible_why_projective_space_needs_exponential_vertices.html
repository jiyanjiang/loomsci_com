Triangulating the Impossible: Why Projective Space Needs Exponential Vertices

September 10, 2026 Mathematics Source: arXiv:2609.10402

How many corners does it take to build a shape from flat triangular pieces? The answer is usually modest. A triangle needs three; a tetrahedron four; the d-dimensional simplex that generalizes them gets by with d+1. Replace that simplex with real projective d-space—a sphere in which every point has been identified with its antipode—and the cost leaps from a gentle linear rule to an exponential one. Florian Frick, Eric Myzelev, Arya Narnapatti, and Aliaksei Vasileuski at Carnegie Mellon University, together with Kaave Hosseini at the University of Rochester, now show why. In a preprint (arXiv:2609.10402), they prove that every simplicial triangulation of real projective d-space needs a number of vertices that grows at least exponentially in the square root of the dimension.

The result is counterintuitive because a d-dimensional simplex needs only d+1 vertices, yet real projective d-space, which can also be built from a d-dimensional sphere by a two-to-one identification, requires exponentially many in the square root of d. The antipodal identification is not a small change; it reweaves the space so thoroughly that local flatness cannot hide the global twist.

Think of triangulation as a form of cartography. To flatten a curved surface onto a page, mapmakers break it into triangular tiles; more complicated coastlines demand more tiles. Real projective d-space is not just complicated—it is double-sided in a merciless way, because every point is glued to its opposite. Any map of this space must carry that pairing through every tile, and the new theorem says the number of corner points must scale exponentially in the square root of the dimension. Unlike a paper map, where a clever projection can sometimes reduce the number of patches, here the antipodal pairing blocks every shortcut. This is not a property of the mapmaker's skill but of the space itself.

This is not a puzzle in abstract geometry alone. Real projective space is the space of all lines through the origin in one extra dimension, a stage on which much of topology and geometry is set. The paper can be read as a census of how much symmetry costs: a free mirror involution, with no fixed points, cannot be represented too cheaply by a simplicial scaffold.

The hidden mirror

Think of a CW complex as a scaffold for curved spaces. You begin with corner points, attach edges, then stretch membranes across loops, and continue into higher dimensions, each cell a room whose boundary lies in earlier rooms. A strongly regular complex is one in which this gluing is tidy enough for Morse theory to count cleanly. Real projective d-space can be built this way, and its antipodal map becomes a free cellular involution: it moves every room to its opposite without leaving any room fixed. This free mirror symmetry is the hidden source of the triangulation tax.

The engine is a topological generalization of the Figiel–Lindenstrauss–Milman inequality, a classical result in convex geometry. In its original setting, the theorem says that a centrally symmetric convex body always contains a large, nearly round central slice. A recent question of Frick, Hosseini, and Vasileuski asked whether an analogous bound could hold for topological spaces with free involutions; this paper answers it. The new theorem works for any finite strongly regular CW complex with a free cellular involution, v vertices, and f maximal cells: its Z/2-index is at most a constant times the product of the logarithms of v and f. The Z/2-index measures how many dimensions one must add to map the space to a sphere without breaking the mirror pairing; the more twisted the space, the larger the index.

But the index alone does not yet force the exponential lower bound. One also needs two facts. First, the equivariant index of the lifted d-sphere is comparable to d—the antipodal symmetry is genuinely d-dimensional, not a small perturbation. Second, a triangulation with few vertices must not be able to compensate by generating an enormous number of top-dimensional cells. That second fact is not automatic; controlling it is the paper's main technical achievement, and it leads directly to Morse theory.

Morse theory as a census

Morse theory reads the shape of a space by studying a scalar height function. Imagine flooding a mountain range: as water rises, new lakes appear at local minima, merge at saddles, and disappear at peaks. Morse theory records those critical events and uses them to reconstruct the landscape. The team applies this census not to a smooth mountain but to a carefully chosen function on the cell complex. They bound the dimensions of the Morse cells by a trace estimate for a constrained Hessian—the matrix of second derivatives that measures how sharply the landscape bends, restricted to directions that respect the symmetry.

That trace estimate is the quiet technical heart. It yields a new Morse-theoretic proof of the classical inequality for centrally symmetric polytopes, and it upgrades the whole argument to strongly regular CW complexes. The result is a contradiction. If a triangulation of projective d-space had too few vertices, the logarithmic bound on the index would shrink it far below its known value of order d. Since the number of maximal cells is itself controlled by the number of vertices, the only way out is for the vertex count to grow at least exponentially in the square root of d. Together with known constructions, this pins down the minimum vertex number to the exponent one-half, up to a slowly growing factor.

From matrices to graphs

The same inequality travels well. Frick and colleagues derive two further consequences. First, every triangle-free graph that is topologically t-chromatic must have at least exponentially many vertices in the square root of t. This sharpens the sense in which certain graphs cannot be both sparse and highly symmetric in their coloring behavior. Second, for sign complexes attached to matrices—combinatorial records of sign patterns that arise in learning theory—the index is at most a constant times d times the square of the logarithm of the number of columns for total matrices, and one logarithm factor worse for partial matrices. Here d is the VC dimension, a standard measure of how rich a hypothesis class is. The sign-complex route builds on earlier work by Alon, Hanneke, Holzman, and Moran on partial concept classes.

An important question raised by earlier work on covering type, including Karoubi and Weibel's framework, is whether the logarithmic product is sharp beyond the polytope case. The paper establishes sharpness for centrally symmetric Hanner polytopes and for the corresponding free simplicial complexes; it does not claim a fully explicit sharpness statement for every strongly regular CW complex. Likewise, a refinement of the matrix bound using VC dimension is sketched rather than made fully explicit, so the improved constants await further work. The relation to sign-rank is natural, but the strongest form of that connection is not yet fully proved. These are precise, well-posed gaps rather than vague doubts, and they chart the next problems.

Seen from a distance, the core contributions are solid and significant. The superpolynomial lower bound solves a recent question of Frick, Hosseini, and Vasileuski. The topological Figiel–Lindenstrauss–Milman theorem provides a new bridge between convex geometry and combinatorial topology. The Morse-theoretic reproof is likely to be useful far beyond this single application. A fair reading is that this is a major advance in topological combinatorics, with the relation to learning theory still developing.

So how cheaply can we triangulate projective space? The answer is now known up to the leading exponent: exponential in the square root of the dimension, and no cleverer partition can evade it. The deeper message is that symmetry is a tax on description. The more a space insists on identifying points with their opposites, the more corners we must pay to simulate it with flat pieces. That price, once hidden, now has a theorem behind it—and the method for proving it may soon be applied to other spaces where mirror symmetries make discrete geometry expensive.
