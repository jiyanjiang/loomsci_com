The Electron Microscope That Learned to Remember Motion

Drafted September 09, 2026 · Published September 10, 2026 Matter Science Source: arXiv:2603.29462

What if the very act of looking changes what you see? For electron microscopists, that question has been a quiet wound for half a century. Electron tomography can reconstruct a nanoscale object in three dimensions, but only if the object holds still. The moment it moves—melting, alloying, reshaping under heat—the reconstruction blurs, because the instrument averages over a specimen that no longer exists. Now a team led by Sara Bals at the University of Antwerp, with collaborators at research centers in Spain, has taught a reconstruction algorithm to treat time not as noise but as signal. Their work appears in a preprint (arXiv:2603.29462) and does something that has eluded the field: it builds a continuous 3D movie of a single evolving nanoparticle from a single tilt series, without stopping the action and with a far lower electron dose than previous approaches.

Think of trying to map a clay sculpture in a dark room with a single flashlight. Each shadow is a clue; collect enough shadows from enough directions, and the form emerges in three dimensions. That, in essence, is electron tomography: a beam of electrons plays the role of the torch, and a series of specimen tilts supplies the angles. But the analogy has a limit. Unlike clay, a heated nanoparticle does not wait politely while you circle it. Every projection must happen before the next bend of the experiment, and conventional reconstruction assumes that all projections describe the same object at the same instant.

The tyranny of the frozen specimen

In conventional electron tomography, the specimen is tilted in discrete steps; at each step an image is recorded; afterward, the stack of 2D projections is mathematically reassembled into a 3D volume. This works superbly for static or flash-frozen samples. But many transformations only happen while the sample is hot, reactive, or illuminated. If the specimen changes between the first angle and the last, the reconstruction tries to make a single sculpture out of shadows from a dozen different sculptures. The result is not motion but mush.

The field has long dealt with this by slowing the process down or freezing it. In situ electron tomography—imaging while the material is heated, strained, or exposed to gas—has been limited by the slow acquisition of tilt series and by algorithms that assume a static world. A dynamic experiment could be stopped at intervals, imaged, and resumed, but that imposes its own tax: repeated electron doses, interruption of chemistry, and a stroboscopic view that can miss the instants that matter. Worse, the electron beam itself can alter the specimen and its dynamics. The new work, by Timothy M. Craig at the University of Antwerp and co-authors including Luis Liz-Marzán and colleagues across Spain, attacks the paired obstacles at once: continuous tilting and a reconstruction that never pretends the sample sat still.

A movie from a stack of shadows

The heart of the method is a continuous tilt scheme. Instead of pausing at a finite set of angles, the specimen is rotated smoothly while projections are recorded; each projection therefore corresponds to a slightly later moment as well as a slightly different viewing direction. Traditional dynamic reconstruction would take this tilt-time series and reconstruct it as if time could be ignored, smearing the evolution into an average. The team's algorithm, which they call DIP-STER, inverts the problem. It treats the goal not as one fixed volume but as a time-dependent field—a 4D reconstruction—and learns how the 3D structure changes from moment to moment within a single tilt series. There is no separate ground-truth movie to train on; the network is self-supervised, deriving its own consistency requirements directly from the acquired projections.

Self-supervision is a strange kind of learning. Imagine assembling a jigsaw without the box picture, relying only on the fact that neighboring pieces must fit together. DIP-STER does something similar with electron projections: it optimizes a neural representation so that, at every time, the predicted 3D volume agrees with the projections available near that time, while remaining smooth enough to connect consecutive states. From that constraint, a continuous movie emerges. The analogy is not perfect—real jigsaws do not change shape as you assemble them—but the principle of internal consistency is the same.

The key distinction is that DIP-STER does not reconstruct one volume and then another; it learns a continuous function of time. This is the difference between a flipbook and a film. A flipbook has as many drawings as moments; a film can produce any frame. The neural network acts as that interpolation engine, constrained by the shadows it has actually seen.

The team first tested DIP-STER on synthetic Au nanostars and Au@Ag core-shell nanorods whose shapes were made to evolve in known, nonlinear ways. Against ground-truth simulations, the neural reconstruction tracked the dynamics far better than conventional approaches, even when the projections were corrupted with typical acquisition artifacts. The improvement was not merely cosmetic: quantitative measures of reconstruction error stayed low across the time series, while structural similarity remained high. This matters because any dynamic method must earn trust before being pointed at an experiment where the answer is not known in advance.

Heat as a teacher

The experimental tests were representative nanoscale transformations. In one, a gold nanostar was heated to 220 degrees Celsius. The reconstructed movie showed its sharp branches retreating and their mass redistributing into the core—a shape seen not as a blur but as a sequence of well-resolved states. The scale of the whole object was close to twenty nanometres, small enough that a single misstep in reconstruction would have erased the very feature the team wanted to see: the loss of branches and their redistribution toward the center.

In a second experiment, a silica-coated gold-silver nanocube was driven through two temperature plateaus—first at three hundred degrees Celsius, then at four hundred and twenty degrees Celsius—until the gold core gradually diffused into its silver shell. Frame by frame, a clean core-shell particle became an alloy, its internal boundary dissolving the way a sugar cube dissolves in warm tea, only the solvent here was the surrounding silver lattice. This is precisely the kind of slow, continuous change that static tomography would have flattened into an unusable average. DIP-STER turned it into a movie.

Electron microscopy is a war against noise and dose. Every electron that passes through the specimen carries information but also deposits energy. DIP-STER's continuous tilt scheme makes more efficient use of the electron dose by never stopping, and its self-supervised reconstruction shares that information across time. The result is a lower dose for a richer movie. This is not an incidental virtue; for beam-sensitive samples it is the difference between a structure that survives observation and one that is destroyed by it.

What this challenges

An important question raised by earlier work on liquid-phase electron microscopy is whether a method tuned to many particles and few frames can survive the opposite regime: a single particle and many tilts. That earlier research, by Ye and colleagues, recovered a molecule's 3D dynamics from movies recorded in a liquid cell. The Antwerp team's preprint answers, in effect, that the lessons transfer, but the geometry of the problem changes. Dynamic electron tomography demanded a different kind of temporal model, one able to disentangle what the sample is doing from where the beam is looking.

A more subtle tension runs through the method's foundations. Because the reconstruction prefers smooth temporal change, it may struggle with abrupt events—a sudden facet collapse, a fast phase flip—that are exactly the kind of discontinuity an experimentalist might most want to catch. The authors note that the network can sometimes confuse dynamics with viewing angle, a shadow of the deeper challenge: time and angle are intertwined in a continuous tilt series, and separating them is only as certain as the prior that says change should be gradual. Earlier work by Boulanger and colleagues on interlaced dynamic X-ray tomography with spatio-temporal implicit representations shares that ambition, but electron microscopy imposes a harsher dose budget and a different detector geometry.

A skeptic might ask: if the algorithm assumes continuity, is it simply assuming what it wants to see? The objection is fair. There is no independent ground truth in the experimental tilts; the method could in principle weave a plausible but false movie from noisy shadows. The team's response, implicit in the simulations, is to test against known synthetic dynamics, where the answer is known and the prior can be judged. What remains to be tested is whether the alignment powers demonstrated by Lim and colleagues for missing-wedge inpainting—the art of filling in viewing directions that a specimen cannot physically reach—can be folded into DIP-STER, so that sample drift and misalignment are corrected jointly with the movie itself.

What this research ultimately shows us is that the oldest problem in observational science—how to watch without disturbing—is slowly yielding to a new kind of instrument: one that does not merely record, but remembers. DIP-STER's memory is not will; it is a mathematical prior that favors continuity. Yet its effect is to turn a series of flat shadows into a narrative of form, allowing us to ask not just how a nanoparticle looks, but how it becomes. The question is no longer whether electron tomography can capture nanoscale motion in three dimensions; it is how far this continuous, dose-efficient remembering can reach into the fragile machinery of materials and life. We are left not with a completed map, but with a better instrument for asking questions—and in science, that is often the most valuable discovery of all.
