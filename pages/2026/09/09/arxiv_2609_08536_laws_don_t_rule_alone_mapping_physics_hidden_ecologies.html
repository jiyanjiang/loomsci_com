Laws Don't Rule Alone: Mapping Physics' Hidden Ecologies

September 09, 2026 Artificial Intelligence Source: arXiv:2609.08536

What if the reason our equations keep failing is not that they are wrong, but that they are lonely? A preprint (arXiv:2609.08536) from a team led by Xiaoyan Shen at Nantong University — with first author Xiongheng Bian at the same institution's School of Information Science and Technology — argues that many physical systems are governed not by one mathematical law but by several, each dominant in its own corner of parameter space. The team calls this an ecology of physical laws, and they want to make mapping that ecology the first step of discovery, before anyone goes looking for a single equation.

The assumption that was never checked

Every data-driven equation search — symbolic regression and its neural-network cousins alike — begins from a quiet assumption: the number of coexisting mechanisms is one. One governing law. One equation to rule them all. It is such a natural assumption that nobody checks it. Yet for any system where heat conduction, chemical reaction, and fluid flow trade control from one moment to the next, that assumption is not merely wrong — it is the primary bottleneck.

The paper's central proposition is disarmingly simple. Instead of hunting for a better equation, first determine how many independent mechanisms are actually present. Only then ask what those mechanisms are and where each one holds sway. The name the authors give this pre-step — physical law ecology — is not a flourish. It is deliberately biological: equations behave like species, each occupying a niche in the landscape of possible conditions, rising and falling as that landscape shifts.

Consider a candle flame. In the wax pool at its base, heat conduction dominates. In the blue zone just above the wick, diffusion combustion rules. Higher still, radiative heat transfer takes over, and at the tip, buoyant convection lifts the luminous gases away. One flame, four regimes, no single equation. The flame is not choosing between laws; these divisions are simply shares of explanatory power assigned by data. Yet for decades, automated discovery tools have tried to force such systems into one formula, like asking a four-piece orchestra to play from a single instrument's score.

A map of influence

The framework the team has built works in layers. First, it slices the data into spatial blocks and condition windows, then lets symbolic regression propose a pool of candidate equations. After removing duplicates, it computes how well each equation fits each small patch of the system and normalizes those fits into a continuous map of dominance weights. Every point in parameter space gets a set of weights, one per candidate law. This is the dominance weight field — a kind of political map of a material's hidden factions.

The second layer captures succession. As conditions change — a strain rate rises, a surface superheat grows, a galaxy's gas fraction climbs — the weights evolve. The framework then fits analytic laws to that evolution, so that it can predict not just what the system will do, but which underlying mechanism will be in charge when it does it. Where physics is irreversible, the method can impose a one-way constraint, forbidding a law from losing dominance once a phase transition has begun. In pool boiling, for example, the film-boiling law draws in more and more dominance as the surface grows hotter, and the constraint prevents unphysical oscillations that a purely statistical fit would happily invent.

The galactic coalition

A skeptic might object that this is just elaborate curve fitting with extra steps. The team anticipated that charge. They imposed topological de-duplication, embedded irreversible constraints, and used a standard statistical criterion to choose how many laws to keep automatically. Across four unrelated systems — stretching elastomers, boiling liquids, galactic dynamics, and evaporating droplets — the answer came back the same: three coexisting governing laws, not one. In engineering applications, the multi-law predictions cut error by roughly 70 percent compared with single-equation baselines, while the surviving equations remained fully interpretable.

The most provocative result, though, concerns dark matter and its most famous challenger. For decades, a small but persistent community has argued that galactic rotation curves can be explained by a single modified law of gravity called MOND, with no dark matter required. The new framework tested that idea on 163 SPARC galaxies, spanning more than three thousand spatially resolved measurements. It recovered not one universal acceleration law, but three gravitational laws whose weights shift in a coherent pattern. At galaxy centers, the Newtonian-law weight recovers; at the outskirts, a transition equation takes over; in gas-rich dwarfs, a floor-like law rises. The coexistence of these three laws, the authors argue, is difficult to reconcile with a single universal acceleration hypothesis.

This is where the paper stops being a tool and becomes an argument. What it challenges is not simply MOND, but a deeper habit of mind. We have trained our machines to search for the One True Equation. Yet in complex systems, the truth may be a shifting coalition. The question that follows is not whether this or that law is correct, but how many laws a system actually needs — and whether our insistence on a single elegant formula has been flattening entire ecosystems of physical law into a single, misleading caricature.

The team does not claim to have solved dark matter, or to have rendered MOND obsolete. The evidence suggests something subtler: that the search for a single governing equation may be an impoverished starting point. If the zeroth step of discovery is to count the laws before naming them, then we may have spent decades solving elegantly the wrong problem.

So the next time someone asks whether a complex system obeys this law or that one, perhaps the better response is another question. How many laws are we really looking at?
