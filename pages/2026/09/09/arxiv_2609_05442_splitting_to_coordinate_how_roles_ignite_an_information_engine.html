Splitting to Coordinate: How Roles Ignite an Information Engine

September 09, 2026 People Source: arXiv:2609.05442

What makes a group of identical agents split into leaders and followers? The naive intuition is that coordination demands consensus—everyone zeroing in on the same answer. But a new preprint suggests something more subversive: sometimes order is not agreement but differentiation. This is the unsettling claim at the heart of a paper by Maximilian Puelma Touzel at the Mila--Quebec AI Institute.

His work, a preprint (arXiv:2609.05442), asks how a society of noisy, self-interested learners can spontaneously invent roles—leader, follower, specialist—without anyone designing them. The answer is not a slogan but an engine. In thermodynamics, an information engine converts a measurement into work; here, agents convert a noisy social signal into differentiated action, and the payoff from coordinated role-play is the extracted work. Think of a Maxwell's demon sorting fast and slow particles to extract energy: the demon's measurement is the fuel. Here the "measurement" is a social inference, and the "work" is collective coordination. The paper calls this a collective information engine.

Underlying this whole premise is a simple game-theoretic fact. In an anti-coordination game, what is good for one agent depends on what others are not doing. Two drivers at an intersection both want to cross, but if both proceed, they collide; the best outcome is one goes first and the other waits. Turn-taking in conversation, deciding who speaks first in a meeting, choosing whether to attack or retreat in a standoff—all are anti-coordination problems. The value of information in such a game is exactly the value of knowing which role to take. It turns out that anti-coordination games are the simplest class in which differentiated role information has value, and the paper builds its minimal instance from them.

The roles themselves are prescribed by schemas—templates that map a context direction onto a suggested behavior. Here the paper reaches across disciplines. These schemas are dynamic: resources accrued through coordinated role-play feed back to shape and reinforce the very schemas that generated them. This operationalizes what sociologist William Sewell called the duality of schemas and resources, an influential but previously qualitative answer to the structure–agency debate. For sociologists, structure and agency have long seemed like two ends of a rope pulled in opposite directions; Sewell tied the ends into a loop. Individuals make choices, yet those choices redraw the rules under which future choices are made.

The loop that lights the fire

Puelma Touzel has shown that if agents infer their role from a noisy social signal, a population can "tunnel" from a uniform cloud of undifferentiated statuses into distinct role modes. Unlike quantum tunneling, this is not a particle crossing a physical barrier, but an information barrier being dissolved by feedback. The secret lies in the fact that role-following action feeds back into that signal, shaping the incentive to follow roles in the first place. It is a loop: an agent's guess about who it is changes what it does, what it does changes the public signal others read, and their reading changes who they think they are. The loop is not a bug—it is the motor.

The engine ignites when a quantity the paper calls the loop gain, Lambda, exceeds one. Lambda is the product of three ingredients: identity persistence—how strongly an agent remembers its previous role; cognitive capacity—how well it can process ambiguous social information; and social feedback channel fidelity—how clearly the signal about roles reaches it. Only when all three are strong enough does the uniform crowd split. Below that threshold, noise washes away any fragile differentiation. Above it, order appears, and does so without a central planner. This is not will or intention; it is a mathematical threshold—a fact about how strongly a loop amplifies its own whispers.

The signature of the repertoire

Once ignition happens, the way roles emerge is not a single event but a cascade. If the schemas form a degenerate set, the society suffers a single unheralded avalanche: everything splits at once. If the schema repertoire has a geometric hierarchy, the roles appear in a logarithmic cascade, one new bifurcation after another in orderly succession. Power-law, or Zipf, hierarchies split into two regimes depending on an exponent: a decelerating cascade with an informative early phase, or an accelerating one with a misleading early phase. Random, unstructured repertoires start quietly and then burst. Coherent, spiked repertoires produce an early bifurcation, a deceptive quiescent gap, and then an avalanche. Each spectral class prescribes its own signature rhythm of role emergence. The functional form is fixed by the repertoire's eigenvalue spectrum, ranging from monitorable logarithmic sequences to deceptive spike-then-avalanche onsets. One might say the spectrum is like a musical score: the same population, reading a different score, produces a different social symphony.

But schemas are not held fixed. Resource accumulation turns schema competition into something like natural selection—a replicator dynamics in which the more useful roles gain resources and thereby strengthen their schemas, draining others. The cascade type is selected endogenously, not imposed. This is the deepest move: the loop not only creates roles but chooses, through resources, which trajectory of role creation a society will take. A society can select whether its coordination arrives as a graceful logarithmic sequence or as a sudden, deceptive spike followed by collapse. In a sense, the society is not merely undergoing a transition; it is negotiating with its own spectral past.

It is tempting to draw parallels between these transitions and familiar phase transitions, such as water freezing into ice. But we should keep the differences in mind. In a physical system, the order parameter is a field like magnetization, and the symmetry breaking is governed by a temperature knob. Here, the "order parameter" is a social convention—an assignment of roles—and the control parameters include cognitive capacity and channel fidelity. A crystal does not choose its lattice; agents, at least in the effective sense of following a role, do. The analogy illuminates but also obscures, because the ice does not read its own phase diagram.

There is a practical payoff. Because the spectral class of the schema repertoire leaves a fingerprint in subcritical fluctuations, the paper derives an inversion formula that recovers the full cascade before the first bifurcation. This is early detection: by watching the covariance of behavior while the population is still near uniform, one can infer whether coordination will arrive as a quiet logarithmic sequence or as an unheralded avalanche. Forward extrapolation fails because the early signals are nearly indistinguishable, but the covariance inversion sees through the fog. Imagine a platform trying to forecast whether its user base will drift into healthy niches or suddenly fragment into warring camps; the pre-bifurcation covariance is the earliest reliable diagnostic.

A question of back-action

Of course, a model this clean rests on idealizations. The model's social signal is noisy, but the measurement itself is passive: agents do not experience a stochastic projection that collapses their state, and there is no finite measurement rate. Earlier work by VanSaders and colleagues on measurement-induced phase transitions in informational active matter has emphasized that a genuine measurement-induced transition turns on such back-action. Here the link is suggestive but incomplete. One might ask: is this really a measurement-induced transition, or is it an engine with a feedback threshold? The paper does not pretend otherwise; it frames the connection as a route to be built, not a road already paved. It also works in a self-averaging, infinite-population limit, where finite groups would show symmetry-breaking choices unpredictable from the class alone. Those idealizations are not weaknesses so much as scaffolding: they let the core loop be seen clearly before the messy additions arrive.

Yet the framework's relevance is not confined to theory. For agents interacting on a digital platform, adaptive platform design becomes a control lever. By tuning the social feedback channel or the schema capacity, a platform might throttle how quickly coordination emerges—whether to encourage healthy specialization or to dampen the kind of runaway coordination that worries researchers studying distributional AI. The model turns design questions into questions about loop gain and spectral class, which can be measured from behavioral data even before roles fully develop. A platform that understands its users' spectral fingerprint is no longer merely hosting a crowd; it is holding a dial on a social engine.

What this work ultimately shows is that differentiation is not a failure of coordination but one of its engines. The question is no longer whether a group must agree to act together, but how much disagreement it can convert into structure—and at what point the structure stops being a population of agents and starts being an institution with a momentum of its own. The paper opens a route to an information thermodynamics of agents, one in which roles, resources, noise, and measurement are not separate stories but a single continuous loop. We are left not with a final answer, but with a sharper question: if order can be born from noise, what is the thermodynamic cost of becoming someone in a crowd?
