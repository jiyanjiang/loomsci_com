Bell Pairs Without a Pump: Teleporting Microwave to Light

September 16, 2026 Quantum Physics Source: arXiv:2609.16364

Ask how a superconducting quantum computer will ever join an optical network, and most answers converge on a single, uncomfortable word: pump. Every existing method for converting microwave photons into optical photons—the two languages quantum hardware speaks—relies on a powerful laser pump, and that pump is a thermal bull in a quantum china shop. A preprint (arXiv:2609.16364) from a team led by Fangxin Li at the University of Chicago, with authors at the Pritzker School of Molecular Engineering and Argonne National Laboratory, asks whether we can remove the bull entirely. Their answer, at least on paper, is yes: generate the entanglement bridge without any optical pump at all, then teleport the quantum state across the divide.

The Pump Is the Problem

The mismatch is almost absurd in its scale. Superconducting qubits process information with microwave photons that oscillate a few billion times a second; optical fibers carry photons that oscillate hundreds of trillions of times a second. Directly shouting between the two frequencies means climbing an energy ladder of thousands of rungs at once. Existing transduction schemes attempt this climb with a laser pump. The pump supplies the missing energy, but it also floods the fragile quantum state with heat and noise. It is a translator who insists on screaming every sentence. This is not poetic license; the heat from the pump is precisely the kind of thermal excitation that destroys the entanglement a quantum network depends on.

Here is the conceptual detour that changes everything. Instead of converting the microwave state into an optical state, teleportation-based transduction consumes a shared resource: a pair of entangled photons, one microwave and one optical, known as a Bell pair. The microwave input is measured jointly with the microwave half of the pair, and the measurement result tells you how to rotate the optical half to recover the input state. The quantum information never has to change forms directly. It is sent through a wormhole made of correlation. Unlike a literal wormhole, this one requires no curved spacetime—only a shared quantum history that survives separation.

A Wormhole Made of Correlation

The catch has always been producing that Bell pair. The standard method, spontaneous parametric down-conversion, also requires a pump, and the pump's heat again contaminates the fragile entanglement. Li and colleagues propose a different factory: a single quantum system—a color center, an atom ensemble, or a phonon-mediated spin—resonantly coupled to both an optical cavity and a microwave resonator. They call it a microwave-optical no-optical-drive entangler, mercifully shortened to MO-NODE. A reflected optical photon, when it emerges from the cavity, carries entanglement with a microwave photon emitted by the spin. The spin acts as the common carrier of both halves—the reflected optical photon and the microwave photon emitted from its spin transition—and no optical pump is ever switched on. The spin is not choosing to be helpful; the cavity simply enforces the correlations through resonance.

Making entanglement is easy only in quantum mechanics textbooks; knowing you made it is the hard part. The scheme is heralded: the emitted microwave photon is captured by a superconducting transmon, and the transmon's final state acts as a receipt that the Bell pair has been produced. If the receipt never arrives, you try again. This makes the Bell-pair source loss-robust—loss destroys photons, but it cannot forge a receipt. In a field flirting with probabilistic success, heralding turns a coin flip into a confirmation. This is not cleverness on the part of the photons; it is measurement turning probability into evidence.

Receipts and Kilohertz

The team analyzed the same blueprint across three distinct physical platforms. Diamond color centers—single spins pinned inside a crystal—offer long coherence times and strong optical coupling. Atomic ensembles of rubidium provide collective enhancement, where many atoms act as one loud spin. Phonon-mediated systems route the microwave interaction through mechanical vibrations, turning a material's jiggling into an intermediary. Across these varied architectures, the theoretical estimates converge on a striking promise: heralding rates in the kilohertz range, with fidelities high enough to be useful in a quantum repeater chain. In a world where many entanglement sources struggle to fire once per second without destroying their own state, a kilohertz-scale source—if realized—would be a genuinely different regime. The comparison, however, deserves a careful eye.

An important question raised by earlier work on high-fidelity control of strongly coupled spin-photon interfaces is whether the optical cooperativity required here—the strength of the spin's coupling to the optical cavity relative to its losses—is actually attainable in the same device that also hosts a microwave resonator. The hybrid device designs proposed for pump-free Bell-pair sources point to a second, subtler issue: the reported kilohertz rates are intrinsic to the source. They do not yet include the losses of getting the photons out of the cavities, through filters, and into real optical fiber. Nor does the preprint provide a full end-to-end transduction fidelity, the number that ultimately matters for a network. A third open question concerns coherence budgeting. Color centers can maintain their quantum coherence for perhaps a millisecond; the protocol must generate, herald, and retrieve the microwave photon before that coherence leaks away. Whether the retrieval rate and the spin coherence budget close in the same experimental run remains, for now, unresolved.

Unclosed Budgets

This is where the dialectic bites. The standard assumption has been that optical pumping is an unavoidable tax on microwave-optical conversion. The new blueprint challenges that assumption by relocating the problem: instead of asking how to convert photons, ask how to generate the right correlations. It also challenges the prevailing engineering instinct that direct transduction is the only path. Teleportation-based transduction has been discussed for years, but it has often been dismissed as too exotic because Bell-pair sources were too slow or too dirty. Kilohertz-range heralded Bell pairs, if confirmed in the laboratory, would rewrite that dismissal. The remaining uncertainties do not erase the conceptual shift; they define the distance between a beautiful protocol and a working machine.

Perhaps the most provocative implication is not about quantum networks at all. It is about what counts as the right level of abstraction in a quantum device. The pump-free MO-NODE does not try to overcome noise with more power, or with better shielding, or with a cleverer laser. It tries to make the noise irrelevant by choosing a different physical resource—heralded entanglement rather than direct conversion. That is a philosophical move as much as an engineering one. We are left not with a finished device but with a sharper question: how much of the difficulty in quantum technology is intrinsic to the physics, and how much of it is the residue of the path we happened to take first? The answer, as this preprint suggests, may be less than we assumed. And the quiet thrill of the work is that the question is now experimentally legible.
