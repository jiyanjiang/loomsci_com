Proving the Unprovable: Certifying AI’s Probabilistic Consistency

August 12, 2026 Artificial Intelligence Source: arXiv:2608.11181

What if the most dangerous failure of a predictive AI isn’t that it lies to us, but that it doesn’t even know it’s contradicting itself? Imagine a medical model that estimates your risk of stroke — separately, for every imaginable combination of blood pressure, cholesterol, and age. You ask it a dozen questions, and each answer feels plausible. But do those answers, taken together, form a coherent picture of reality, or are they just a patchwork of plausible fictions that could never coexist under any single statistical worldview? The problem runs deeper than any single prediction: it is a question of internal consistency, and until now there was no efficient way to verify it. A preprint (arXiv:2608.11181) by Orr Paradise at EPFL, Oliver Richardson, Yoshua Bengio, and Shafi Goldwasser — spanning the universities of Montréal, MIT, and Berkeley — proposes exactly that: a method by which a probabilistic model can be forced to prove, to an outside verifier, that its web of conditional predictions does not secretly contradict itself.

This is not a philosophical puzzle dressed in mathematics; it is a direct assault on a gap that has haunted AI safety for years. The debate approach, pioneered by Irving and others, imagines two AIs arguing about a claim while a human judge listens, hoping that adversarial pressure exposes falsehoods. But Paradise and colleagues ask a more foundational question: before any debate can be meaningful, can a model even satisfy the basic requirement that its own probability estimates cohere? A model that lacks internal consistency is not a debater worth engaging — it is a weather forecaster who, when asked about rain tomorrow and about sunshine the day after, gives numbers that together add up to one hundred and fifty percent. The new work does not try to catch specific lies; it aims to certify that the model’s entire catalogue of answers can be reconciled with a single, honest distribution over the underlying world.

Understanding why this is hard requires a brief tour through the landscape of probabilistic checking. The paper’s target is a predictor: a pair of circuits, one that takes in a query and spits out a prediction, and another that spits out a confidence level. The predictor implicitly makes an exponential number of claims — one for every possible combination of the Boolean variables that define its input space. Checking that all these claims are mutually consistent would naively require checking exponentially many statements, which is like trying to verify that every brick in a cathedral was laid square by personally inspecting each one. The key insight is to borrow a trick from complexity theory: the interactive probabilistically checkable proof (PCP). In a classic PCP, a verifier can spot-check a few bits of a proof and, with high confidence, determine whether a theorem is true. The twist here is that what must be verified is not a mathematical theorem but the existence of a single probability distribution that simultaneously satisfies all of the predictor’s claims.

The team constructs such an interactive PCP. The verifier is given the two circuits describing the predictor. It evaluates them at only a handful of points — no more than a few dozen queries, regardless of the exponential number of possible statements. Alongside these circuits, it is given a “proof oracle”: an encoding of an alleged witness distribution that, if the predictor is indeed consistent, neatly explains all its predictions. The verifier then engages in a short conversation with a single untrusted prover, while also peeking at the static proof oracle—an encoded witness—at a few locations. If the predictor is inconsistent, any prover will be caught: the protocol guarantees that no matter how cleverly the prover tries to paper over the contradictions, the spot-checks will uncover the inconsistency with overwhelming probability. The price for this power, however, is that the verification is approximate and tied to a specific input distribution, not the true world distribution. The paper’s guarantee is that, if the predictor passes the check, there exists a witness distribution that is approximately consistent with the claims it makes under that distribution. This is a subtle but crucial caveat: a model could be perfectly consistent with respect to the queried distribution yet still be deeply wrong about rare catastrophic events that the distribution underrepresents.

To build the sparse witness at the heart of the protocol, the authors first tackle a seemingly simpler problem: verifying consistency for a set of explicit probabilistic claims, rather than ones generated by a circuit. Suppose someone hands you m statements, each of the form “given that X is a particular Boolean assignment, the probability that Y equals 1 is p.” Long ago, Nilsson showed that approximate consistency can be placed in NP, with a certificate whose size grows with the number of claims and the bit-precision of the numbers. Paradise and colleagues refine this: they show that with a small additive gap between soundness and completeness, the bit-precision dependence disappears. The sparse witness that emerges becomes the foundation for the full interactive protocol. It is as if, instead of needing a full map of every possible weather pattern, you only need a compact cheat sheet that tells you, for any handful of questions, whether the numbers jibe.

The philosophical implications reach well beyond the technical machinery. For decades, the PCP theorem stood as a monument in theoretical computer science: any mathematical proof can be rewritten in a format so robust that a verifier need only examine a constant number of bits to be convinced of its correctness. Paradise and colleagues have now shown that the same conceptual apparatus can be turned inward, to interrogate the internal coherence of a learning machine. They are not just verifying a proof; they are verifying the prover’s own mind. Not since the earliest debates about how to certify the safety of software have we seen such a clean wedding of complexity theory and the pragmatic demands of trustworthy AI. The protocol they construct is a first step — the authors are explicit that low-confidence, widespread errors can slip through, and that consistency alone does not guarantee truthfulness — but it is a step that provides something the community badly needed: a formal language in which to demand that a model account for itself.

A natural question, sharpened by earlier work on AI safety through adversarial debate, is whether consistency certification can ever be a practical safeguard. In a debate, an honest adversary can reveal hidden contradictions by asking pointed questions. The interactive proof approach, by contrast, does not require an adversary that understands the model’s weaknesses; it only requires a short exchange with a prover that claims the model is consistent. This is both a strength and a weakness. It means the verification does not depend on finding clever counterexamples, but it also means the guarantee is global: a model that fails the test is inconsistent with respect to the queried distribution, but a model that passes it may still be fragile in corners the distribution never visits. The paper’s authors frame this honestly, acknowledging that their protocol “checks consistency against a specified distribution, not the true world distribution.” The tension between these two approaches — adversarial probing versus mathematical certification — is not a sign of failure; it is a sign that AI safety, like security, will need layered defenses.

What this research ultimately shows us is that a question as slippery as “is this probabilistic model coherent?” can be given a crisp complexity-theoretic answer. The protocol does not deliver an unassailable guarantee of truthfulness, nor does it claim to. But it forces the model to put its own internal contradictions on trial, in a procedure that can be executed by a polynomial-time verifier with only a handful of queries. That is a remarkable transformation: from a property that seemed to demand exponential inspection to one that can be checked interactively, almost as easily as we check a digital signature. The deeper implication is that the boundary between what a machine can compute and what it can prove about itself may be far wider than we supposed.

We are left not with a final verdict but with a new kind of tension: the tension between coherence and truth. A model can be perfectly consistent yet perfectly wrong. But to ask for consistency first is to demand that the model take a stand, a single statistical worldview, rather than offering a different self-contradictory answer for every question. In a world where AI systems increasingly advise on medical decisions, legal judgments, and climate policy, that demand — that they at least do not contradict themselves — might be the most basic check we can perform. The fact that we now have a formal tool for performing it, and that the tool is built on some of the deepest ideas in theoretical computer science, changes the nature of the conversation. The question is no longer whether we can trust a black-box predictor; it is whether we can teach it to prove, to any skeptical auditor, that its own web of probabilities hangs together. Paradise and colleagues have shown that, in principle, the answer is yes.
