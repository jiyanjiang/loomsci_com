When Excited States Refuse to Stay Still

Drafted August 11, 2026 · Published August 12, 2026 Matter Science Source: arXiv:2608.08095

What if the maps we’ve been using to explore the quantum world have been drawn on a surface that never stops shifting underfoot? For decades, simulations that track how electrons and nuclei dance together after a material absorbs light have relied on a quiet deception: the nuclei, the heavy partners in the choreography, are asked to move across a landscape that belongs to the ground state, the territory of the unexcited. That, at least, was the assumption that made the mathematics tractable. But in a preprint (arXiv:2608.08095), a team led by Weibin Chu at Fudan University argues that this assumption has been quietly sinking whole classes of predictions. Their answer — a machine‑learning framework they call on‑the‑fly N2AMD — amounts to a new kind of cartography, one that redraws the landscape in real time, as the system itself moves through it.

I am not being dramatic. The problem is simple to state but almost punishingly hard to solve. When an electron in a solid absorbs a photon and hops into an excited state, the entire distribution of charges rearranges. The forces on the nuclei change. The potential energy surface — the mathematical terrain that dictates where the atoms want to go — is no longer the same one that existed a femtosecond ago. Yet standard nonadiabatic molecular dynamics (NAMD), the workhorse for simulating such processes, has traditionally locked the nuclei into moving on a static, ground‑state surface, with only the electronic state hopping between fixed levels. It is like trying to predict the path of a hiker using a map that was accurate yesterday, after a landslide has redrawn the valleys. The qualitative shape might still be recognizable, but the quantitative errors can be staggering. The Fudan team found, for instance, that when this static approximation is applied to a MoS₂/WS₂ heterostructure — a sandwich of two atomically thin semiconductors — it produces order‑of‑magnitude errors in the hot‑carrier lifetime.

To fix this, you would need—at every time step—a fresh calculation of the excited‑state energies, the forces on every nucleus, and the nonadiabatic coupling vectors that govern how electrons jump between states. Doing this from first principles for a realistic piece of condensed matter has, until now, been a computational pipedream. Conventional density functional theory, run at the level of a hybrid functional — the gold standard that incorporates a fraction of exact exchange to quiet the self‑interaction error — is already expensive for ground states. Repeating it hundreds of times along a molecular dynamics trajectory, for multiple excited states, and then computing the derivatives that yield forces and couplings, would be like asking a cartographer to map a shifting continent by hand, one pebble at a time.

Enter the equivariant neural network. The idea, stripped to its skeleton, is this: you train a machine to predict the electronic Hamiltonian — the operator that governs all the quantum mechanical behavior — directly from the positions of the atoms. Because the network respects the symmetries of the physical system (it is “equivariant” under rotations, translations, and permutations of identical atoms), it learns the correct functional form without ever being told the underlying physics. The derivatives that give forces and couplings are then obtained not by expensive numerical differentiation, but by automatic differentiation, the same machinery that trains the network itself. The result is a Hamiltonian factory that delivers accuracy approaching that of a hybrid functional, at a tiny fraction of the cost, and that can be called upon thousands of times during a simulation to recompute the true excited‑state landscape on the fly. The entire loop — electrons influencing nuclei, nuclei reshaping the electronic structure, each feeding back into the other — is closed for the first time for condensed matter.

Changwei Zhang, the paper’s first author, and the large collaboration that spans Fudan, South China Normal University, and the University of New Mexico, put their framework through a series of stress tests that read like a condensed‑matter wish list. In bulk silicon, a workhorse benchmark, they showed that the excited‑state forces predicted by the neural network deviate from high‑level quantum chemistry calculations by an amount that is small enough to be irrelevant for dynamics — and that the ground‑state approximation, by contrast, can be wildly off the mark. The computational cost scales so gently with system size that a simulation that would have been a career‑length undertaking becomes a routine overnight job. And the network, crucially, is transferable: trained only on untwisted bilayers of MoS₂, it accurately predicts the excitation forces for twisted structures with arbitrary angles — configurations it never saw during training. The map, once learned, generalizes to an entire atlas of similar materials.

But the real meat of the paper lies in three demonstrations that would have been impossible with the old methodology. The first is that MoS₂/WS₂ heterostructure, a system of intense interest for solar energy and optoelectronics. When the team ran a conventional NAMD simulation — the kind that freezes the nuclear motion onto the ground‑state surface — they found that excited electrons lingered in the conduction band for a long, leisurely time. But when they unleashed the on‑the‑fly machinery, allowing the nuclei to respond to the instantaneous electronic excitation, the dynamics changed qualitatively. The electrons migrated across the interface far faster, shattering the old picture and bringing the simulation into agreement with what experiment has long been trying to tell us: that charge separation in these heterostructures is a brisk business. It is the difference between navigating with a map that pretends the terrain never changed, and walking the actual land—feeling the ground rise and fall beneath your feet.

The second case is a photoinduced ferroelectric switching in bilayer hexagonal boron nitride. Here, the absorption of light triggers a sliding motion of the two layers relative to each other, flipping the material’s polarization. The team tracked, trajectory by trajectory, the evolution of the electronic levels and the collective displacement of the atoms, watching as the ensemble snapped from one polarization state to the other. No previous NAMD method could have captured this coherent structural transformation, because the driving force — the rearrangement of the electronic cloud upon excitation — was precisely what the static‑surface approximation erased. The land itself had changed shape, and for the first time, the simulation could follow the consequences.

The third demonstration is perhaps the most visually arresting. In anatase titanium dioxide, a material central to photocatalysis, the team watched, in real time, the birth of a polaron: a self‑trapped state where an electron distorts the surrounding crystal lattice and then sits snugly inside the distortion it has created. The simulation shows the hole‑hosting wavefunction condensing from a delocalized smear spread over several titanium atoms into a compact bundle on a single atom, all within 350 femtoseconds. This is a process that has been discussed for decades but never observed directly in a dynamic simulation at the hybrid‑functional level. It is the quantum equivalent of watching a stone drop into a pond and seeing the ripple become a whirlpool.

What makes these triumphs more than mere computational case studies is the philosophical shift they represent. For as long as condensed‑matter theory has existed, materials have been understood primarily through their equilibrium properties: the crystal structure at rest, the band structure of a perfect lattice, the phonon spectrum of small vibrations around a minimum. But the world is not at equilibrium. Sunlight, electrical currents, and catalytic cycles are all far‑from‑equilibrium phenomena, operating in a regime where the system’s very identity is being remade moment by moment. The tools we had — static density functional theory, perturbation theory, equilibrium Green’s functions — were like listening to a symphony by reading the sheet music, never actually hearing the instruments play. On‑the‑fly N2AMD hands us the audio recording. It lets us hear, for the first time, how the melody of electronic excitation bends and reshapes the musical score itself.

To be sure, this is not a finished symphony. The neural networks must be trained for each new class of materials, and the training requires reference data from expensive calculations — though once the network is trained, the cost per simulation is negligible. The method inherits all the approximations of the underlying hybrid functional, which, like all density functionals, can fail for strongly correlated systems or for processes that involve long‑range charge transfer in materials with large band gaps. And the sheer complexity of coding and maintaining the interface between machine‑learning force fields and NAMD engines means that widespread adoption will require not just new algorithms, but new habits of thought among the computational physics community. The cartographer’s map is now alive, but the cartographer must learn to draw while walking on shifting ground.

However, the paper does not ask us to suspend our critical faculties. The robustness of the method is demonstrated across multiple systems, with multiple checks against higher‑level theory, and with a transparency that invites replication. The examples are chosen not for their flashiness but because they are canonical problems — the hetrostructure, the ferroelectric, the polaron — for which the limitations of the old paradigm were most painfully obvious. The message is not that machine learning will replace physics, but that it can return physics to its proper domain: following nature where it actually leads, rather than where our equations can comfortably tread.

So, what should we make of a world where simulations can finally keep pace with the real‑time dance of electrons and nuclei? I am reminded of the old chestnut that physics advances not by discovering new truths, but by discarding assumptions that were never explicitly stated. The assumption that excited‑state dynamics could be adequately modeled on a ground‑state landscape was one of those quiet background convictions — so deeply embedded in computational practice that it had ceased to feel like an assumption at all. This paper names it, challenges it, and provides the machinery to move beyond it. The road from this demonstration to predictive, first‑principles design of photocatalysts, solar cells, and quantum materials remains long, but the direction is now unmistakable.

And perhaps, when future historians of science look back at the early decades of the 21st century, they will see in this convergence of neural networks and quantum dynamics something more than a technical convenience. They may see the moment when we stopped trying to predict the behavior of excited matter by freezing it, and started to watch it live.
