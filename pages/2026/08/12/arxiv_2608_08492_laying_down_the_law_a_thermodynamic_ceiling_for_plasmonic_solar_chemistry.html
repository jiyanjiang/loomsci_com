Laying Down the Law: A Thermodynamic Ceiling for Plasmonic Solar Chemistry

Drafted August 11, 2026 · Published August 12, 2026 Matter Science Source: arXiv:2608.08492

In 1961, William Shockley and Hans Queisser published a calculation that became the north star of photovoltaic research. They asked a deceptively simple question: how much of the raw energy in sunlight can a single semiconductor actually turn into electricity? Their answer — roughly 33.7 percent — has guided solar‑cell design for more than six decades. It gave engineers a ceiling to aim for and a yardstick against which every new material could be measured. But for solar‑driven chemistry, where light is used not to push electrons through a wire but to drive bond‑breaking and bond‑making at a catalyst surface, no equivalent ceiling existed. That gap has now been closed. In a solo‑authored preprint (arXiv:2608.08492), Seungwoo Lee at Korea University’s KU‑KIST Graduate School of Converging Science and Technology has erected a rigorous thermodynamic and electromagnetic framework that draws an outer boundary for what plasmonic solar chemistry can achieve.

Lee’s scaffold separates the problem into three nested layers: a universal detailed‑balance limit, mechanism‑specific bounds that distinguish hot‑carrier from photothermal conversion, and a source‑conditioned level that admits real experimental data. At each step, more of the messy physics that governs a real plasmonic nanoparticle is folded into the calculation, and the allowed efficiency never rises — it can only shrink. The result is a hierarchy that captures irreversibility at every stage, from photon absorption to final chemical product, much as Shockley and Queisser did for photovoltaics, but with an extra burden: everything must happen inside one physical nanostructure that must serve all wavelengths and all reaction pathways at once.

Think of it as a clock tower that must keep time for an entire city. In a photovoltaic cell, you can pretend each colour of light has its own private sundial, individually tuned. But a plasmonic catalyst — a single nanoparticle, or a patterned surface — must handle the full solar spectrum, from ultraviolet to infrared, and simultaneously juggle direct metal–adsorbate excitation, nonequilibrium hot carriers, and photothermal heating. The tower cannot show different hours for different neighbourhoods; its face must strike a compromise. Lee shows that this single‑geometry penalty is not slight. In one benchmark, requiring a shared physical structure cut the predicted useful chemical work by nearly 34 percent compared with independent optimization for each wavelength. Unlike an actual clock tower, which can be designed with a single perfect face, the plasmonic structure is forced to offer a different response — a different face — for each photon, and the laws of electromagnetism strictly constrain how well that job can be done.

At the heart of the framework lies a chemical diode theorem that crystallizes the tug‑of‑war between forward and backward reactions. Detailed balance, a cornerstone of thermodynamics, demands that at equilibrium every forward reaction is exactly matched by its reverse. When you try to pull a chemical product out of the system, that reverse path fights back, and the harder you pull — the larger the chemical load — the stronger the back‑reaction becomes. Lee captures this as a diode‑like current‑voltage relation for chemistry: a closed‑form expression gives the stall free energy, beyond which no net product can be generated, and the maximum‑power point where the system delivers the most useful work. For a simple step‑function absorption spectrum, this tight‑coupled cycle reproduces the classic Shockley‑Queisser efficiency of 33.68 percent. When the chemical load is fixed at 1.23 electron‑volts — the thermodynamic threshold for splitting water — the ideal single‑junction ceiling settles at 30.58 percent, in perfect agreement with known limits. But the real damage comes when the model admits that hot carriers are the only chemically useful species.

A hot carrier is an electron that has been excited far above the energy band where ordinary charge transport happens; it is, in a sense, a fleeting, energetic sprinter. If only these sprinters count, the picture darkens. When Lee imposes a Fowler hot‑carrier kernel — a simplified picture in which only carriers above a threshold energy can trigger chemistry — the ceiling collapses to a meagre 8.53 percent. The sunlight’s abundant low‑energy photons, which carry most of the solar flux, are suddenly dead weight; they heat the material but cannot do the chemical job. This dramatic drop is not a bug of the framework; it is a faithful reflection of how much less work can be extracted when the catalyst’s usable electronic population is restricted.

Equally sobering is the challenge of building complex molecules. Producing a single carbon‑monoxide molecule from CO₂ requires two electrons; a molecule of formate needs four; more complex fuels demand six, eight, or even more. Lee derives a universal arrival‑storage criterion: the dimensionless product of the carrier‑arrival rate and the lifetime of an incomplete intermediate must exceed a threshold that grows rapidly with each additional electron. Under the dilute flux of one‑sun illumination, the product flux scales as a high power of the arrival rate, making multi‑electron products exponentially scarce. Nature, it seems, imposes a stiff kinetic penalty on building complexity.

The framework’s third layer, the source‑conditioned level, brings experimental reality into the equations. Lee draws on wavelength‑resolved product‑selectivity measurements from operando studies, such as those reported by Tagliabue and colleagues (arXiv:2606.09612). Those measurements reveal, for a gold on p‑type gallium nitride (Au/p‑GaN) catalyst, which chemical products — carbon monoxide, formate, or hydrogen — emerge when specific colours of light are absorbed. By combining such selectivity maps with the absorbed optical power, one can bound the chemical work that a given structure might deliver. But the approach immediately meets a subtle boundary. The selectivity data were recorded at constant absorbed optical power, not under the variable local fields and transport conditions that a three‑dimensional geometry dictates. An important question sharpened by those operando studies is whether selectivity maps — recorded at constant absorbed optical power — can fully constrain the efficiency ceiling when the catalyst’s three‑dimensional geometry reshapes local electric fields, ion diffusion, and heat dissipation beyond what a simple optical‑absorption picture captures. Lee acknowledges this gap, leaving the coupling of yield kernels to geometry‑dependent transport as a clear next step.

The most chastening insight from the full framework may be this: enhanced product formation is not the same as net solar‑energy conversion. When Lee performed a complete system accounting for a conditional Au/p‑GaN CO₂‑reduction case, the ledger revealed that the electrical work needed to bias the cell and the thermodynamic work to separate the products together outweighed the incremental chemical free energy stored. Under those conditions, the net useful power turned negative — illumination increased the rate at which a certain product was formed, but the overall process was consuming stored free energy rather than adding to it.

Yet the value of Lee’s work lies precisely in its refusal to offer wishful shortcuts. By cleanly separating universal constraints from mechanism‑specific ones, and by couching the analysis in the language of semidefinite programming — a mathematical toolkit that gives rigorous outer bounds — the framework provides a common language. Theorists can now ask, with far greater precision, how much a hot‑carrier pathway can in principle deliver, and experimentalists can gauge how their best measurements fare against the unforgiving thermodynamic ceiling. The single clock tower may never keep perfect time for all citizens, but now we know the outermost error of its bells. The ceiling has been drawn; the task is to climb toward it.
