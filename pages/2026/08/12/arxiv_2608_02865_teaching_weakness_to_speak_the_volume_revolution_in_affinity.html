Teaching Weakness to Speak: The Volume Revolution in Affinity

Drafted August 11, 2026 · Published August 12, 2026 Matter Science Source: arXiv:2608.02865

For a century, biochemistry has asked the same blunt question of molecular interactions: how much does it take? The textbook method is a titration — you fix a volume in a cuvette, drip in more of one protein, and watch the binding curve rise. It sounds simple. But lurking beneath that simplicity is an assumption so deep that most researchers rarely notice it: they are changing the numerator, the number of molecules, while the denominator, the reaction volume, remains quietly fixed. This assumption creates a ceiling that has left a vast realm of biology — the realm of the very weak handshake — almost completely uncharted.

A team led by Daishi Fujita at Kyoto University’s Institute for Integrated Cell-Material Sciences has now shattered that ceiling. In a preprint (arXiv:2608.02865) on the arXiv, they propose a different question: not “how many,” but “how close.” By shifting experimental control from molecular number to accessible volume, they unleash a strategy that makes millimolar-affinity interactions — so feeble that they have been functionally invisible to standard assays — not just measurable, but quantitatively rigorous. It is a conceptual turn that recasts vague notions like “local concentration” into a first-principles variable, and in doing so, it inverts the way we think about molecular encounters.

To appreciate the shift, consider how concentration works. Concentration C is simply the number of molecules N divided by the volume V they roam. The conventional titration varies N while holding V constant. For any given interaction, there is some bulk concentration that half-saturates the partner — the dissociation constant Kd. If the affinity is too weak, you would need such a high concentration of one protein that it would exceed solubility or aggregate, hitting a practical wall long before any measurable binding. The millimolar range, the weak-interaction frontier, has thus remained a blind spot: according to a survey of over 1,400 reported protein–protein interactions, not a single conventional measurement exceeds a Kd of one millimolar. Nature is full of weak, transient encounters that orchestrate signaling, cytoskeletal dynamics, and compartmentalization, but the tools of bulk biochemistry simply cannot read them.

Now watch the denominator. If you can shrink V, you can crank up the effective concentration without adding a single extra molecule. This is the intellectual kernel of the work: instead of pouring more protein into a flask, you confine the handful of molecules you already have into a space so small that their paths inevitably cross. The team formalized this by tethering two interacting partners inside a DNA nanocavity — a nanoscale scaffold built from DNA helices whose dimensions can be controlled to within nanometres. By anchoring each partner at a specific point on the scaffold, the accessible volume of each becomes a precisely defined region, and the overlap of those regions sets an effective concentration, ceff, that maps directly onto the same molar scale as the old bulk experiments. The vague “proximity effects” of textbooks become a quantitative knob: turn up the overlap, and you increase ceff; turn it down, and you dilute it.

The physical realisation is as elegant as the concept. The team used a luciferase complementation system: a large enzyme fragment, LgBiT, and a small peptide fragment, SmBiT, that produce light only when bound. In bulk, many SmBiT variants bind so weakly that they would never light up. But inside the nanocavity, where the tethers hold them in overlapping volumes, they find each other often enough to glow. By competing the bound SmBiT with a titrated soluble inhibitor and fitting a Bayesian model, the researchers extracted absolute dissociation constants. The weakest variant they measured, SmBiT230, has an estimated Kd of 9.6 mM — roughly fifteen times weaker than the highest conventionally measured protein–protein constant — all from a total protein amount under a picomole. A standard plate reader delivered a signal-to-noise ratio up to a thousand, leaving enormous headroom for still weaker interactions.

The geometry does more than simply amplify weak signals; it can upend a binding hierarchy altogether. Picture two suitors competing for the same companion in a labyrinthine mansion. One suitor is intrinsically more charming (higher bulk affinity), but is tethered in a distant wing and rarely even crosses paths with the object of desire. The other, though less attractive, happens to be anchored in the same drawing room, and thus wins by sheer proximity. This is not a metaphor; it is a statement of the ceff framework. The team demonstrated this inversion experimentally: by changing the tethering points on the DNA scaffold, they could make the LgBiT fragment bind preferentially to SmBiT over a competitor that, in free solution, would have dominated. The calculation of geometric interaction weights predicted the winning partner with near-perfect accuracy, showing that spatial design can override intrinsic chemical affinity.

This design principle then enabled rational screening for modulators of weak interactions — a task almost oxymoronic in conventional screening, where you hunt for tight binders that disrupt complexes. The team targeted the adhesion between the integrin beta1A tail and talin, a cytoplasmic interaction of millimolar weakness that helps govern whether cells stick or crawl. Inside the nanocavity, the reporter was a luminescent SmBiT–LgBiT pair that could be displaced by the target integrin–talin binding, turning the light off. A compound that stabilised integrin–talin would thus dim the signal. The screen identified an optimised peptide that reduced luminescence and, in parallel cell experiments, enhanced cell adhesion in a dose-dependent fashion — all without the compound itself binding tightly to either partner. It is a proof of concept for a pharmacologically neglected region: drugs that coax weak endogenous complexes together rather than hammering them apart.

The significance of the work, however, goes deeper than any single measurement or screening hit. For over a century, biochemistry has been a science of well-stirred pots. The concentration-based paradigm treats molecules as particles in a gas, ignoring the fact that inside a cell, proteins are not uniformly distributed — they cluster, corral, and crowd within membrane compartments, phase-separated droplets, and filamentous networks. The volume-based paradigm begins to close the gap between the homogeneous maths of mass action and the cellular reality where geometry dominates. By making the denominator the experimental variable, the team has given precise meaning to what biochemists have only gestured at with phrases like “local concentration,” and in so doing, they have opened a conceptual conduit from the design of synthetic nanostructures to the logic of biological organisation.

Of course, the DNA nanocavities are artificial. Translating the volume-based principle to read out interactions inside a living cell, with its far messier topology, is a distant prospect. But the intellectual pivot — from number to volume — already provides a new lens for seeing the molecular world. Perhaps the deepest surprise is not that we can now measure millimolar interactions, but that we had been measuring everything under the assumption that crowding doesn’t matter, when nature has been using geometry to tune encounter probabilities for billions of years. By learning to control space as precisely as we once controlled concentration, we are finally catching up to what the cell has known all along.
