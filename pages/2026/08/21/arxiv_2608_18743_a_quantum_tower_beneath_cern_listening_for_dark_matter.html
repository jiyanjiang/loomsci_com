A Quantum Tower Beneath CERN: Listening for Dark Matter

Drafted August 20, 2026 · Published August 21, 2026 Cosmos Source: arXiv:2608.18743

Every day, protons make thousands of laps around CERN’s Large Hadron Collider, racing through an underground ring and passing a network of access shafts that rarely draw attention. One of those shafts, PX46, may soon be repurposed for a very different kind of instrument. Instead of accelerator hardware, it would hold a hundred-metre-tall atom interferometer — a quantum sensor built not to smash particles together, but to let them fall and listen.

In a preprint (arXiv:2608.18743), Gianluigi Arduini, Michael Werner, and a large international collaboration lay out the technical case that this shaft is “uniquely mature and implementation-ready” for the Atom Interferometer CERN Experiment, or AICE. The authors write that the site presents “no technical showstoppers.” The proposal builds on earlier conceptual and implementation studies, and it is endorsed by a broad proto-collaboration that already spans dozens of institutions.

From an empty shaft to a quantum sensor

Think of a standard optical interferometer, which splits a light beam into two paths and then recombines them to reveal tiny phase differences. AICE does something similar with clouds of ultracold strontium atoms. Laser pulses act first as a beam splitter, then as a mirror, and finally as another beam splitter, coaxing each atom into a quantum superposition of two vertical trajectories before bringing those paths back together. If anything has disturbed the atoms along the way — a faint dark-matter field, a passing gravitational wave — the final pulse records it as a shift in the interference pattern.

The initial design uses strontium-87 and a single-photon clock transition at 698 nanometres, a particularly stable atomic line. The atoms spend several seconds in free fall, giving small effects time to accumulate over the long vertical baseline. That length matters enormously: the farther an atom falls while in a superposition, the more phase a tiny external kick can imprint on the final signal.

AICE would not operate as a single interferometer. Multiple atom sources placed along the shaft would form gradiometers, pairs of interferometers interrogated by the same laser pulse train. Comparing signals along different baselines cancels noise common to both arms, such as fluctuations in the laser. This common-mode rejection is central to the design. It is why one vertical tower can support several simultaneous measurements, each sensitive to a different kind of new physics.

The engineering side is substantial. The proposal describes modular vacuum sections, magnetic shielding draped along the full tube, a surface building to house the lasers, and phase-stabilised optical fibres that carry light down into the shaft. The design borrows heavily from the VLBAI and MAGIS experiments and from the AION-10 technical design report, scaling a strontium gradiometer architecture up to the long baseline available at PX46. In effect, the collaboration wants to take a laboratory-scale quantum sensor and place it inside one of the most carefully controlled underground environments on Earth.

A broad science case

The primary target is ultralight dark matter, a class of extremely light bosonic fields far below the mass of the electron. If such a field oscillates, it can nudge atomic transitions and alter the relative phase of the two interferometer arms. AICE would listen for those nudges across a mass range where other experiments are largely blind. Related searches target axion-like particles, another class of light bosons that may couple weakly to ordinary matter.

The same instrument can test the Einstein equivalence principle, the foundational idea that all objects fall at the same rate regardless of composition. By comparing the free fall of different atomic species — first strontium-87 and strontium-88, later ytterbium-171 — AICE would look for tiny differences that would signal a crack in general relativity. A later dual-species configuration would co-launch two kinds of atoms and interrogate them over the full baseline, turning a single tower into a precision test of whether gravity and dark matter care about what an atom is made of.

There is also a gravitational-wave programme. Existing detectors such as LIGO catch high-frequency signals from stellar-mass mergers, while the future space-based LISA observatory will watch much lower frequencies from supermassive black holes. Between those bands sits a largely unobserved middle range, below one hertz, where AICE could act as a pathfinder. It would not replace LIGO or LISA. It would bridge them.

An honest blueprint

The proposal is deliberately staged. It defines a Baseline configuration and a more ambitious Stretch configuration, each with projected sensitivities in several dark-matter channels. It also looks further ahead to a possible kilometre-scale successor that could sharpen the gravitational-wave reach even more. What is striking is the document’s engineering concreteness: access doors, crane operations, radiation shielding, cooling circuits, and a construction timeline tied to the next long shutdown of the LHC.

At the same time, the proposal does not claim to have solved every problem. An important question raised by earlier work on long-baseline atom interferometers concerns the large-momentum-transfer beam splitters. These pulsed devices boost an atom’s momentum and therefore its sensitivity, but a detailed noise budget for them is still missing. The authors acknowledge this openly. Nor do they yet provide a complete technical pathway for every parameter in the most aggressive scenario. That is not a weakness of the blueprint. It is a precise map of where the remaining work lies.

The next step is concrete. If site preparation proceeds during the upcoming long shutdown, AICE could be installed without disturbing the High-Luminosity LHC programme. After that comes the hard part: commissioning the first interferometers in the shaft, characterising the beam splitter noise, and then running the long, quiet integration periods that dark-matter and gravitational-wave searches require. The proposal does not promise a discovery. It offers something more useful: a realistic path from an empty access shaft to an instrument that may, for the first time, let us listen to the dark side of the universe.

None of this will change daily life tomorrow. But the questions AICE addresses — what dark matter is, whether gravity treats all matter equally — sit at the core of how we understand the cosmos. A wrong assumption there would eventually ripple through everything from fundamental physics to cosmology. AICE is a proposal, not a result. Yet it turns a quiet corner of CERN into a place where some of those questions might finally get an answer.
