Building a Superconducting Diode That Reverses Without a Field

Drafted August 20, 2026 · Published August 21, 2026 Matter Science Source: arXiv:2608.19099

One-way valves are among the humblest and most indispensable components in modern electronics. You can hear their work in a radio and feel it in the warmth of a phone charger. They steer alternating current into direct current, protect circuits from reverse voltages, and convert radio waves into audible signals. Conventionally, such valves—diodes—are made from semiconductors, which waste a small but real amount of energy as heat.

Now a team of condensed-matter physicists has built a diode from two atomically thin crystals that carries current without resistance, and it does so with no external magnetic field. Its polarity can be flipped by the history of a magnetic field, a behavior the researchers call "programmable by magnetic-field history." The work appears in a preprint (arXiv:2608.19099) on arXiv.

The result comes from Shengbiao Sun at the Southern University of Science and Technology in Shenzhen, working with colleagues led by Shuo Wang at the International Quantum Academy and Shenzhen Branch of the Hefei National Laboratory. Together with collaborators in Ningbo and Guangzhou, they report what the authors call a "field-free SDE with an efficiency reaching 35.7%" in a van der Waals heterostructure made of niobium diselenide (NbSe₂), a classic superconductor, and chromium tribromide (CrBr₃), a layered magnet.

The superconducting diode effect, or SDE, has been pursued for more than a decade because it promises to combine the low heat generation of superconductors with the directional control of a diode. Yet most realizations require an external magnetic field, and achieving a large, controllable asymmetry at zero field has remained difficult. In that sense, the new report is genuinely surprising—not only for the size of the asymmetry, but also for the way it reverses.

A Two-Way Street Made Asymmetric

In a conventional superconductor, the current is carried by Cooper pairs—electrons bound into a single quantum state that flows without scattering. For a given temperature and material, there is a maximum current, called the critical current, above which superconductivity collapses. In most superconductors, the critical current is the same whether the current flows left or right. The material is, in the language of transport, a two-way street.

A superconducting diode effect appears when this symmetry breaks: the forward critical current differs from the reverse critical current. To create that asymmetry, researchers have previously used an external magnetic field or engineered a state in which Cooper pairs carry a finite total momentum. In the new NbSe₂/CrBr₃ stack, the authors identify a "leading symmetry-allowed cubic term odd in Cooper-pair momentum" that creates unequal critical currents while leaving the equilibrium condensate at zero momentum.

In plain terms, one contribution to the energy that governs the superconducting order changes sign when the current reverses, and this sign-changing piece is enough to tilt the critical-current landscape. The condensate itself does not need to be pushed into a moving state. That is the conceptual break with the more familiar finite-momentum pairing route.

This matters because finite-momentum pairing usually requires careful engineering. A moving condensate tends to be fragile, and preserving it often demands an applied magnetic field or a specially designed interface. The cubic term suggested here is different: it breaks left-right symmetry through the form of the order parameter itself, while the condensate remains at rest. In effect, the asymmetry is built into the shape of the superconducting state, not into its motion.

The Magnetic Ingredient

The magnetic half of the heterostructure is CrBr₃, a van der Waals magnet. Its chromium atoms carry magnetic moments, and in the relevant stacks the material behaves as a layered ferrimagnet. Neighboring layers display both ferromagnetic alignment, where moments point the same way, and antiferromagnetic coupling, where neighboring layers oppose each other. Because the oppositely oriented layer moments do not cancel exactly, the whole stack keeps a net magnetization.

Ferrimagnetism is less familiar than simple ferromagnetism, but it is also more flexible. In a ferromagnet, all the magnetic moments lock into one direction, and reversing that direction usually requires a coercive field. In a ferrimagnet with competing interlayer couplings, several different layer-by-layer arrangements can be stable. Which arrangement the material chooses can depend on how the external field was applied, not just on its current value.

When placed next to NbSe₂, this magnet exerts a local exchange field on the superconductor—a spin-dependent influence that breaks the twofold symmetry between forward and backward current. This is not a matter of will, but a consequence of how the magnetic order couples to the superconducting electrons. The exchange field acts as an internal bias, replacing the external magnetic field that earlier diode designs required.

History in the Sweep

The most striking observation comes when the team sweeps a perpendicular magnetic field up and down. The diode polarity does not simply track the applied field. Instead, it undergoes reversals that are nonmonotonic and hysteretic: the state after a sweep depends on the direction from which the field arrived. The team writes that these behaviors "cannot be explained by Meissner screening or conventional ferromagnetic proximity."

Meissner screening is the familiar expulsion of magnetic flux from a superconductor, and conventional ferromagnetic proximity would produce a more direct relationship between the magnet’s overall moment and the diode polarity. The measured reversals are more complicated than either mechanism can account for. The polarity can swing back and forth as the field increases, and the pattern is not retraced when the field decreases.

Micromagnetic simulations and a generalized Ginzburg-Landau theory point instead to the layered ferrimagnetism in CrBr₃. Coexisting ferromagnetic and antiferromagnetic interlayer couplings produce a history-dependent interfacial exchange field acting on NbSe₂. In other words, the magnet can settle into more than one internal configuration, and which configuration it chooses depends on the field path it has just traveled. The superconducting layer then inherits that history through the exchange field.

An Independent Assessment

An independent look at the preprint suggests that the main experimental claims are well supported by the combination of transport data and simulations, but two questions remain open. The first concerns alternative origins of the asymmetry. An important question raised by earlier work on altermagnetic proximity is whether the observed field-free behavior could also arise from an altermagnetic exchange field rather than the layered ferrimagnetism proposed here.

Zhu and colleagues have shown that altermagnets, in which neighboring magnetic sublattices have opposite spins but are related by crystal symmetry, can imprint a distinctive nonreciprocal signature on a superconductor. The diagnostics of the new paper favor ferrimagnetism, but a definitive exclusion of altermagnetic proximity remains a task for future experiments. That is not a weakness of the transport data so much as a genuine scientific puzzle: several magnetically driven mechanisms can produce nonreciprocal supercurrents, and separating them requires more than a single device geometry.

The second open question is microscopic. The generalized Ginzburg-Landau theory can accommodate the cubic term and reproduce the qualitative behavior, but the paper does not yet derive this term from the underlying band structure of the heterostructure. As a result, the connection between the proposed mechanism and the measured 35.7% efficiency is still qualitative rather than quantitative. That is not a failure of the experiment; it is a sign of how much remains to be understood about magnetic van der Waals proximity.

The Next Switch

Despite those open questions, the promise is concrete. A programmable, field-free superconducting diode could become a building block for ultralow-power superconducting electronics. A circuit element that can switch its preferred current direction by field history, rather than a continuously applied field, would reduce heat and simplify control in cryogenic logic or memory. The next experimental steps are equally concrete. One is to test whether the effect survives at higher temperatures or in thicker stacks, since the exchange field from CrBr₃ can be altered by interlayer spacing and strain. Another is to probe the band structure directly and check whether the cubic term can be derived microscopically.

So, there is much more work for Shuo Wang and colleagues to do in superconducting electronics. And if the nonmonotonic reversals they mapped in this heterostructure are any indication, the team is well placed for the challenge. The authors describe the result as "a distinct mechanism for nonreciprocal superconductivity"—and for the rest of us, it is a reminder that a humble one-way valve can still surprise.
