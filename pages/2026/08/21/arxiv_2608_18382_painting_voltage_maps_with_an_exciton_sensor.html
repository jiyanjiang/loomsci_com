Painting Voltage Maps with an Exciton Sensor

Drafted August 20, 2026 · Published August 21, 2026 Matter Science Source: arXiv:2608.18382

If you have ever attached metal contacts to a piece of atomically thin semiconductor, you know the suspicion that follows. The current seems fine, but what are you actually measuring? The material under the contacts, the interface where metal meets crystal, or some uneasy combination of both? For a two-terminal measurement, there is no way to separate them. The more you lower the carrier density—precisely the regime where exotic electronic states like to emerge—the worse the problem becomes, because the contact resistance tends to swell while the material itself might still be conducting quietly beneath it.

This frustration is the starting point for a new preprint from a team led by Feng Wang at the University of California, Berkeley (arXiv:2608.18382). Working with colleagues at the Kavli Energy NanoScience Institute and the National Institute for Materials Science in Japan, first author Ha-Leem Kim and collaborators describe a workaround. Instead of reading the voltage with metallic probes, they read it with light. In the authors' phrasing, this is "a noninvasive wide-field technique that replaces local voltage probes with a proximal monolayer MoSe₂ exciton sensor."

A contact problem

Electrical transport in a two-dimensional crystal is usually far more revealing when measured with four probes rather than two. Two outer contacts push current through the sample; two inner contacts measure the voltage drop across a well-defined region. The beauty is that the inner contacts carry almost no current, so their own contact resistance does not corrupt the measured potential difference. But this trick only works if you can place four electrodes precisely on the sample—and if the sample is large enough to accommodate them without the leads themselves changing the physics.

When a semiconductor is only a few atoms thick, even a good metal contact can introduce a resistance far larger than the channel itself. The result is a classic measurement blind spot. At high carrier densities, where the material is metallic, a two-terminal signal can be dominated by the metal–semiconductor interfaces, so the intrinsic channel resistance disappears into the noise. At low carrier densities, the situation is more extreme still. This is exactly where correlated states—superconductivity, charge order, strange metallicity—are predicted or observed in many van der Waals materials. Yet the very contacts used to interrogate them may be hiding the answer.

The problem is easiest to see if you think about how a two-terminal voltage is spent. Part of the applied bias is consumed at the first contact, part at the second, and only the remainder drives the channel. If the contacts are particularly resistive, most of the bias is lost before it ever reaches the material of interest. The resulting current then reflects the contacts, not the atomically thin channel. This is why contact resistance is more than a nuisance: it is an information barrier.

The Berkeley team's target is bilayer molybdenum diselenide, MoSe₂, a two-dimensional semiconductor that can be tuned through a metal–insulator transition by changing its carrier density. To measure what the channel itself is doing, the researchers stack a monolayer MoSe₂ sensor just above the bilayer target, separated by a thin slab of hexagonal boron nitride. The sensor does not touch the target. It sits close enough to feel the local electrostatic potential, but far enough away to leave the current flow completely undisturbed.

This is the heart of "proximal exciton sensing." The monolayer sensor is not merely a passive film; it is an optical material whose reflectance depends on its own electron density. A nearby target layer can change that density through ordinary electrostatic gating. If the target is locally charged, it shifts the sensor's charge state, which in turn alters how strongly the sensor reflects light. The result is a non-contact readout of the target's potential, pixel by pixel and in real time.

An optical voltmeter

The sensing mechanism is built on an exciton: a bound pair of an electron and a hole inside the monolayer. An exciton absorbs and reflects light at a specific wavelength, and its resonance shifts when the local electron density changes. In simpler terms, the monolayer's reflectivity acts as a readable gauge of its local environment. By shining focused light at one spot, measuring the reflectance, and comparing it to a calibrated response, the team can infer the electric potential at that spot. Then they move to another spot and repeat.

Think of it as a narrow strip of optical film laid just above the sample, close enough to be influenced by the target's potential without ever making electrical contact. Unlike a metal needle, which can inject carriers, heat the system, or distort the field lines, the optical probe is passive. It reads the potential the way a thermometer reads temperature—by responding to its surroundings rather than disturbing them.

Of course, a raw reflectivity change is not yet a voltage. To convert the optical signal into a quantitative map, the team performs a pixel-wise in situ calibration. At each probed position, they step a calibration voltage through a small range while recording how the exciton reflectance shifts. This gives a local conversion factor between optical response and electrostatic potential. They then apply a small bias—tens of millivolts—and measure the resulting potential changes across the channel. Because the calibration is done at every pixel, variations in sensor sensitivity or local strain do not need to be guessed; they are measured.

Operationally, the measurement alternated between two phases. During the calibration phase, the source and drain were held at the same voltage, so no current flowed and the potential was uniform. During the biasing phase, a small differential bias was applied to drive current. By interleaving these two phases and averaging many frames, the team suppressed slow drifts in the optical setup and extracted the bias-induced potential changes cleanly.

The result is a two-dimensional voltage map of a live, biased semiconductor device. Focused light beams take the place of the inner voltage leads in a four-probe measurement. Where a conventional probe would require a delicate metal contact, the optical method uses only photons. And because the approach is wide-field, the team can build a spatial picture of transport rather than a single number.

Mapping the metal–insulator transition

The power of the technique becomes clear when the team uses it to watch bilayer MoSe₂ cross from metal to insulator. At liquid-helium temperatures, the voltage maps in the metallic regime reveal large potential drops near the contacts and a much gentler gradient in the central channel. This is precisely the spatial signature expected when contact resistance dominates the two-terminal response. The optical method sees past that screen.

The potential profiles are almost shockingly physical. Near the source and drain, the voltage changes sharply. In the interior, it slopes gently. A uniform resistor would show a straight line across the channel; the sharp end drops are the contact resistance made visible. The optical maps thus give researchers something that no global measurement can provide: a direct view of where the voltage is actually being lost.

The contrast is striking. Even when ordinary two-terminal resistances reached millions of ohms, the optically derived channel resistance remained below one kilohm. In other words, the material itself was still conducting well while the contacts were making the whole device look much worse. As the density is lowered further, the channel resistance rises and eventually crosses a fundamental scale known as the resistance quantum. That crossing marks the metal–insulator crossover, and it matches the expected ordering of electronic states rather than being buried beneath contact artifacts.

The maps also reveal something that a global two-terminal measurement can never show: spatial heterogeneity. The reconstructed local conductivity is not uniform. Even after smoothing, the potential and conductivity maps retain texture in both the insulating and metallic regimes. Some of this structure traces to bubbles that formed during the heterostructure transfer—a reminder that van der Waals devices are assembled, not grown, and that local disorder can coexist with clean global transport. Rather than being a nuisance, these maps turn disorder into a visible feature that can be correlated with fabrication steps.

This matters because the metal–insulator transition is not merely an academic boundary. In a clean two-dimensional system, the transition separates a phase where electrons flow freely from one where they localize. The exact position of that boundary, and the microscopic arrangement of conducting and insulating regions, can reveal the balance between interactions, disorder, and quantum fluctuations. Optical voltage profiling gives researchers a way to watch that boundary move and deform in space, rather than inferring it from a single current–voltage curve.

Why heterogeneity matters

For researchers who work with atomically thin semiconductors, the human impact is immediate. Contact resistance has long made it hard to know whether a newly fabricated device is intrinsically good or merely well-contacted. Optical voltage profiling offers a way to answer that question without perturbing the device. The same stack can be mapped repeatedly while carrier density, magnetic field, or temperature is changed, because no physical leads are attached to the sensitive channel region.

There is also a broader lesson in the method's modesty. It does not invent a new material or a new many-body state. It improves the measurability of systems that already exist. In condensed matter physics, that is often the bottleneck: the tools for seeing are just as important as the objects being seen. A technique that replaces opaque two-terminal information with a quantitative, spatially resolved voltage map changes what can be asked.

The approach is also attractive because it is not limited to one specific material. The proximal exciton sensor can in principle be transferred over any conducting target that electrostatically couples to the monolayer. That opens a path to imaging transport in twisted bilayers, moiré structures, and other buried devices where conventional four-probe measurement is difficult.

The next practical step is to deploy the same exciton-sensing scheme across a wider set of van der Waals devices—those with stubborn contacts, fragile doping, or strongly inhomogeneous channels. If the calibration protocol proves portable, optical voltage profiling could become a standard companion to electrical transport studies. In a field that is increasingly defined by complex stacks and buried interfaces, learning to read voltages with light may be exactly the kind of quiet advance that makes the next discovery visible.
