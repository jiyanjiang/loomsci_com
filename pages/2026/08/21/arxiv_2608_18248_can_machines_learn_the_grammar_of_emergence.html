Can Machines Learn the Grammar of Emergence?

Drafted August 20, 2026 · Published August 21, 2026 Matter Science Source: arXiv:2608.18248

What would it mean to claim that we understand an enzyme—not merely to describe it, but to know how to build it from scratch? For much of molecular biology's history, that question has hovered between ambition and naivety. We have learned to read sequences and predict folded structures with astonishing accuracy. Yet a construction manual—a genuine recipe for assembling complexity—has remained strangely out of reach. A preprint (arXiv:2608.18248) from a team at the University of Chicago, led by Arjun S. Raman with first author Caden Proctor of the Genomics, Genetics, and Systems Biology program, now proposes such a recipe. The team's wager is that the order in which complexity arises can itself be learned.

Here is the paradox at the heart of the work. Modern generative models—transformers, diffusion models—are extraordinarily good at producing plausible things. They can write sentences, draw digits, and design proteins that fold in silico. But generating an outcome is not the same as knowing how that outcome was constructed. A machine that imitates a finished building may have no idea which walls are load-bearing. The transformer architectures introduced by Vaswani and colleagues learn to attend to dependencies, but they do not, by themselves, articulate a constructive hierarchy. The Chicago team asks a harder question: can we infer, from an ensemble of finished systems, the sequence of constraints that brought them into being?

This is where the method takes a philosophical turn. The authors borrow from statistical phylogenetics—the discipline that reconstructs evolutionary relationships from patterns of shared and variable traits. At the heart of that discipline is entropy: a measure of how much a feature varies across an ensemble. A position in a protein that is nearly identical in every relative is low-entropy, strangely frozen. A position that differs wildly from one sequence to the next is high-entropy, a site of constant novelty. The team first infers entropic scales from the data; it then trains a Layer-Restricted Boltzmann Machine, or LRBM, that imposes a one-way information flow from the most invariant, global features to the most variable, fine-grained ones.

A Staircase of Constraints

Think of it as building a cathedral rather than photographing one. The lowest-entropy layer fixes the overall plan: the cruciform shape, the nave, the flying buttresses. Only later do the higher-entropy layers add the carvings, the stained-glass patterns, the particular stone. A Boltzmann machine is a network that learns to reproduce statistical regularities; the "restriction" in the LRBM is that information may travel only from low-entropy scales to high-entropy scales, never back. Generation becomes construction. You must settle the global skeleton before the fine-point details. This is not an arbitrary aesthetic choice; it is, the authors argue, a statistical logic that emergent systems share.

Now, you might be thinking this sounds like just another deep generative model wearing a new name. In one sense, you would be right—the mathematical machinery of Boltzmann machines and layer-wise training is familiar. But the shift is subtler than the architecture. Most generative models ask: given a noise distribution and a target ensemble, can we produce new samples that belong to the ensemble? The LRBM asks a different question: can we learn the order in which a system must be constructed if we want to build it from the ground up? That is a question about explanation, not merely generation. It is the difference between a recipe and a photograph of a cake.

The model's first two applications are elegant but almost too easy. Applied to handwritten digits—binarized pixel grids, with no labels telling the machine which digit is which—it infers global shapes before pen-stroke texture, much as a human might recognize an "8" by its loops before noticing the slant. Applied to natural language, it builds sentences from stable syntactic scaffolding toward lexical variation. The same algorithm, with no domain-specific tuning, follows the same hierarchical grammar. But a skeptic could say: of course. Images and sentences have obvious coarse-to-fine structure. The real test is biology, where the hierarchy of constraints is hidden and the stakes are existential.

Fold Before Function

Chorismate mutase is a metabolic enzyme that has become a proving ground for protein design, because its function depends on a precise fold. The team constructed a model of this enzyme family and then built 160 constructive trajectories of synthetic sequences, testing them alongside an ensemble of natural sequences in living cells. The designed enzymes worked. Some functioned with sequences up to 53% divergent from the nearest natural homolog—a distance that, in protein space, feels less like a neighbor and more like a foreign language.

Here the dialectic sharpens. The fold emerged at intermediate layers of the model; function arrived only when all layers were satisfied. In the authors' words, fold was necessary but insufficient for function. That is a disturbing sentence for the common protein-design intuition. We like to think that if you get the structure right, the function will follow. The LRBM's hierarchical picture says the story is more layered. You can satisfy the intermediate constraints and obtain a plausible folded shape, yet the enzyme remains silent until the deepest, most variable layer has been satisfied. This silence is not a choice the molecule makes; it is a statistical consequence of an incomplete hierarchy of constraints. The final layers appear to encode fine-grained, surface-exposed interactions that turn a folded object into a catalyst. Function, in this family, is the last word.

The Burden of Proof

An important question raised by earlier work on topology as a language for emergent organization, by Bailey, is whether inferred entropic scales correspond to topological persistence—the features that survive across scales rather than dissolving into noise. The new work does not settle that correspondence, but it sharpens it: perhaps entropy and topology are two dialects of the same underlying grammar. Similarly, earlier work on expanding functional protein sequence space with high-entropy generative models, by Zamponi and colleagues, asked which level of global constraint maximizes functional diversity. The LRBM result sits in an interesting tension with that approach. Instead of treating entropy as something to maximize uniformly, it ranks constraints by entropy and applies them in a prescribed order. This turns sequence space from a fog of possibilities into a staircase.

Still, a dialectical reader should hesitate before declaring victory. The number of entropic scales is chosen heuristically, not derived from a formal statistical test; a different boundary-drawing procedure might partition the same data into fewer or more layers. The experimental validation is powerful, but the paper does not yet include control non-hierarchical models that would show fold-before-function is unique to the LRBM rather than a generic feature of constrained generation. And the simulated evolution used to derive the model partly mirrors its own assumptions, so the living-cell assay carries more than its share of the evidential weight. These are not fatal flaws. They are the next questions.

What the work ultimately offers is not just another generative model but a change in the question we ask. Before, we asked whether a machine could make something that looks like an enzyme. The new question is whether a machine can infer the order in which an enzyme must be built. The provisional answer, in this family, is yes. We are left not with a closed loop but with a sharper question: if emergence has a learnable grammar, what other grammars—molecular, neural, social—are waiting to be read from the ensembles we have already collected? The grammar of emergence may be written in entropy, and we have only just learned to spell.
