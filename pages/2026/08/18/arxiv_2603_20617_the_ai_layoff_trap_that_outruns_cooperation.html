The AI Layoff Trap That Outruns Cooperation

August 18, 2026 Other Sciences Source: arXiv:2603.20617

Why would a firm automate away the very customers who buy its products? And why would every firm, at once, do the same while knowing the danger? In a new preprint (arXiv:2603.20617), Brett Hemenway Falk of the University of Pennsylvania and Gerry Tsoukalas of Boston University argue that the answer has little to do with ignorance. The real culprit is a contradiction buried inside the logic of competition itself.

Picture a ring of farms drawing water from a shared aquifer. Each farmer installs a more powerful pump because it puts more water on his own rows. The private gain arrives immediately; the falling water table is distributed across everyone. No single farmer can afford to stop pumping alone, even as the shared crop begins to fail. This is not a perfect image of an economy—demand is not a fixed stock of groundwater—but the shape of the trap is the same. In formal dress, it is what Hemenway Falk and Tsoukalas call a demand externality.

The engine of the model is maddeningly asymmetric. When a firm automates a task, it keeps the entire cost saving. The lost wages, however, do not vanish harmlessly. They become lost demand for products, and that damage spreads across all firms in the market. Each automation decision is therefore a private gain and a public wound. In a task-based model of a transitioning economy, this asymmetry turns locally smart choices into an automation arms race that displaces far more workers than anyone would have chosen together.

The most unnerving result is that competition, usually our medicine for corporate power, becomes an accelerant. A monopoly would feel the demand collapse it creates; with many rivals, each firm can hide its small slice of the damage in the crowd. Hemenway Falk and Tsoukalas show that the gap between what firms do and what a cooperative plan would choose—the over-automation wedge—widens as the number of firms grows. More unsettling still, the welfare arithmetic finds both firm owners and workers poorer than they would be under that cooperative benchmark. Even “better” AI, the kind that saves more labor at lower cost, digs the trench deeper. Neither free entry nor flexible wages can fill it in.

Here a reasonable reader objects: cannot workers retrain, share in equity, receive a basic income, or simply see capital taxed so the gains flow back? The authors test the usual remedies in turn—wage adjustments, free entry, capital income taxes, worker equity, universal basic income, upskilling, and Coasean bargaining. Their conclusion is uncomfortable: each may redistribute the harm, but none removes the incentive to create it. The trap does not spring from poverty of tools; it springs from the fact that the damage you cause is mostly borne by others.

One tool can change that arithmetic. A Pigouvian automation tax—named for the economist Arthur Pigou—attaches a price to the externality at its source. Instead of compensating workers after the flood, it makes each firm feel the demand destruction it releases downstream. When the private cost of automating includes some of the public consequence, the rational automator moves closer to the cooperative ideal. The arms race slows because the racecourse now charges a toll.

This challenges a comforting assumption: that informed markets are enough to prevent collective self-injury. Here the firms are informed, rational, and trapped nonetheless. What the paper ultimately offers is not a specific tax rate but a shift in the question. The debate about AI layoffs is not only about how to repair the aftermath; it is about whether we price the automated act of displacement itself. We are left with a harder, better question—how to set that price, and who gets to decide.
