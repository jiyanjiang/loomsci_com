Reading the Integer: Thermal Hall Tomography in Graphene

August 14, 2026 Matter Science Source: arXiv:2608.12586

Some numbers are easy to write down and almost impossible to measure. The Bogoliubov–de Gennes Chern number is one of them: a single integer that is meant to count the chiral Majorana modes running along the edge of a chiral superconductor. For decades, candidate materials have not yielded that reading, because the magnetic signatures usually invoked are not topologically protected. In a preprint (arXiv:2608.12586), Kumar Ghosh of E.ON Digital Technology in Hannover proposes a way to read the integer directly, using sideways heat instead of magnetism.

Part of the promise is that topology is robust. Small wrinkles in the material, mild disorder, even modest changes in the pairing amplitude, should not change the integer, because it counts a global winding rather than a local magnitude. That same robustness is why a direct reading would matter. It would not be a fit, an extrapolation, or a model-dependent reconstruction; it would be a number that the material itself hands over.

The name itself carries a hint. The Chern number originated in geometry as a way to count how a field winds around a closed surface; in a superconductor, the relevant surface is the momentum-space Brillouin zone, and the field is the pairing wavefunction. The fact that a single integer can carry such a strong physical consequence — the number of protected edge channels — is one of the quiet wonders of condensed matter physics.

To see why this matters, picture an ordinary superconductor as a still lake. Electrons pair into Cooper pairs and flow without resistance. A chiral superconductor is not still; the pairing itself carries orbital angular momentum, breaking time-reversal symmetry. The edge then hosts one-way traffic lanes: chiral Majorana modes, excitations that are their own antiparticles. The number of lanes is not a matter of material luck. It is fixed by topology, by the winding of the superconducting wavefunction in momentum space, and that winding is precisely the Chern number.

Why the usual probe fails

For magnetic probes, the trouble is subtle. A chiral superconductor should show a magnetic signal, but that signal can shrink to zero for higher angular-momentum states even when the topological integer remains. The integer, in other words, has more to do with how the pairing winds in momentum space than with how strongly the material magnetizes. Here rhombohedral graphene changes the calculation. Its superconductivity emerges from an interaction-driven orbital ferromagnet, and magnetic imaging has already resolved what Ghosh calls "rewritable time-reversal-breaking domains" inside the superconducting phase.

That last point matters more than it might seem. In most candidate superconductors, the chiral domains are difficult to isolate or control. In rhombohedral graphene, the domains are rewritable: the same magnetic imaging that reveals them can, in principle, prepare a domain of known chirality. The integer's sign then becomes a controllable experimental variable rather than an accident of cooling.

The normal state, meanwhile, makes the problem harder in a different way. Quantum oscillations reveal a Fermi surface too intricate to reconstruct pocket by pocket. That complexity would be a serious obstacle for any measurement that tries to infer the integer from the normal-state geometry.

Could sideways heat do what magnetism could not? Ghosh's proposal says yes.

Heat as the topological witness

In a thermal Hall measurement, a temperature difference along one direction drives a heat current in the perpendicular direction. Much of the experimental difficulty is related to how a chiral edge mode can carry heat along the boundary, eventually causing the transverse signal to plateau at a universal low-temperature value. That plateau is proportional to a fundamental constant times the same integer that counts the edge modes.

Because the plateau returns the integer directly, the intricate normal state need not be fully mapped first. Just as a reliable measurement can rest on finding a quantity that is protected from microscopic detail, the thermal Hall conductance is protected by topology. This is the central appeal.

The comparison with magnetic probes is not merely practical. A magnetic signal is a bulk property, susceptible to all the messy details of the normal state. The thermal Hall plateau, by contrast, is fixed by the boundary, where the topological edge modes actually carry the heat. Counting them is not so different from counting lanes on a one-way ring road rather than measuring the average speed of every car.

A map in momentum space

Let us follow the logic in momentum space. The phase of the superconducting gap can wind like a vortex around certain zeros. The occupied regions of momentum space — the Fermi pockets that electrons actually inhabit — enclose some of those vortices and leave others outside. For pairing confined to a single band, the invariant equals the total winding of the vortices enclosed by the occupied regions. It does not depend on how those regions decompose into pockets.

This rule turns a global topological question into a local bookkeeping problem: which vortices are inside the occupied region, and with what winding. The normal Fermi surface may be distorted by trigonal warping — a triangular deformation of the Fermi contour imposed by the carbon lattice — and the Cooper pairs may carry finite momentum. Ghosh shows that these complications can be packaged into an antisymmetric part of the Hamiltonian. That part is "topologically inert": direct Chern calculations across 525 parameter points keep the integer unchanged.

But quantization has an enemy. When the zero-energy gap closes along a surface in momentum space, a Bogoliubov Fermi surface appears, and the plateau can disappear even though the formal integer is still defined. Ghosh derives a single inequality that marks where this happens. Inside that boundary the plateau remains measurable; outside it, the quantization breaks down.

What an experiment would see

The proposal is unusually concrete. The low-temperature plateau should read out the integer, and its sign should reverse with the imaged domain. A written domain wall should carry double the integer's worth of Majorana channels. The thermometry required already resolves single thermal quanta in encapsulated graphene at millikelvin temperatures.

The experimental bar is high but not unreachable. Millikelvin thermometry of encapsulated graphene already resolves single thermal quanta, which is precisely the sensitivity needed to see the plateau. The remaining challenge is not the thermometer; it is the sample.

Perhaps the largest open question is whether a real sample will resolve a single domain, or whether averaging over many domains will blur the plateau. An important question raised by earlier work on chiral superconductors from parent states with non-uniform Berry curvature is whether the occupied-vortex rule survives in realistic multiband materials. Ghosh's numerical evidence across the parameter scan is consistent with it, but the preprint does not claim a general proof beyond the models it studies. Independent Berry-curvature analyses of the same system emphasize how fragile the plateau can be. The useful resolution is that the preprint makes its assumptions explicit, and the inequality for the Bogoliubov Fermi surface gives experimentalists a clear boundary to test.

If the plateau is measured, we would finally possess a direct reading of a chiral topological invariant in a superconductor. That would not merely confirm a prediction; it would give experiments a new tomographic handle on the topology of pairing. Perhaps one day the same sideways heat could be used to read other topological integers in other quantum materials. For now, the preprint does something rarer than promising an answer: it lays out, with unusual concreteness, exactly where and how to look.
