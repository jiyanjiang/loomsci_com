How a Sharp Curl Inequality Finally Found Its Optimizers

August 19, 2026 Mathematics Source: arXiv:2607.23827

For most of the past three decades, one inequality on the sphere has functioned as an unclaimed summit in geometric analysis. Open since at least a 1998 paper by Tristan Rivière, and still open even in the physically most relevant case of three dimensions, it asks how small a differential form’s “curl” can be relative to its overlap with that curl. The answer matters because the inequality sits at a junction where conformal geometry, spin geometry, and the variational problems of mathematical physics all meet. A preprint (arXiv:2607.23827) from Guofang Wang at the University of Freiburg and Mingwei Zhang at Wuhan University and the University of Freiburg now claims that summit.

The landscape of sharp constants

Sobolev inequalities are among the oldest organizing principles in analysis. They formalize a simple intuition: if a field’s derivatives are controlled, the field itself cannot be too large. The sharp version of such an inequality requires two further things. It needs the best possible constant, and it needs the identification of the special fields that achieve equality. These special fields are not technical footnotes; they reveal hidden symmetries.

Cast your mind back to a first course in vector calculus and you will recall that curl measures how a field rotates at a point. On higher-dimensional spheres, the curl generalizes to differential forms, but the same geometric question remains: how much can a form twist before it must align with its own twist? The sharp curl-Sobolev inequality puts a precise floor under that alignment.

The landscape around this summit has been active. Earlier work has sharpened spinorial Sobolev inequalities, identified optimizers in Sobolev–curl inequalities, and probed sharp constants for curl-Sobolev inequalities on spheres. Each thread brought the field close, but the sharp result for the relevant dimensions remained out of reach. That is the gap this paper fills.

The difficulty is not merely technical. The curl, unlike a gradient, is sensitive to the sphere’s curvature in a way that the standard Sobolev inequality is not. A proof must therefore separate the contribution of curvature from the contribution of twisting, without losing sharpness. That balance has defeated several natural approaches.

Conformal invariance is what makes the result unusually mobile. A conformal transformation preserves angles but not distances, and the quantities in this inequality transform in a controlled way under such rescalings. Because of that, a proof on the sphere automatically transfers to flat Euclidean space. That is why the authors can state, without additional work, that the same sharp inequality holds in ordinary space.

The result in context

As the authors write, the paper solves “a longstanding problem, going back at least to Rivière 1998 and open even in the physically most relevant case n=3.” To see what that means without the full notation: on a sphere of dimension one less than a multiple of four, take any differential form of the appropriate middle degree. The inequality compares two integrals. The numerator measures the form’s curl raised to a dimension-dependent power, integrated over the sphere; the denominator measures the overlap between the form and its curl. The theorem says the quotient cannot drop below a constant proportional to the sphere’s surface area raised to the reciprocal of the dimension.

The second half of the theorem is just as important: the authors classify all extremals. They prove that the minimizing forms are precisely positive Killing forms, up to orientation-preserving conformal transformations and addition of forms in the kernel of the exterior derivative. A Killing form is a rare differential form whose covariant derivative is completely prescribed by its exterior derivative, a condition that makes it a kind of taut string among fields. Unlike a stretched string, however, such forms exist only in special dimensions where the sphere’s geometry cooperates.

Three dimensions matter here for a physical reason. The Hopf map, the Dirac operator, and many soliton models live naturally on the three-dimensional sphere. Readers who last met the Hopf map in topology may remember it as a standard source of continuous winding. The new theorem turns that topological intuition into a quantitative statement about energy.

The authors derive several geometric and variational applications. One is that on the sphere the round metric is the unique optimizer for a natural conformal invariant. In the paper’s phrasing, “the round metric is the unique optimizer for the conformal invariant.” Another concerns the energy of the Hopf map; there the theorem confirms a conjecture of Rivière.

Perhaps the most striking application for physicists involves the Faddeev-Skyrme energy, which assigns a cost to how twisted a field configuration is and has been used to model knotted solitons in nuclear physics. The theorem establishes global minimality of the Hopf map in its full predicted range: for every coupling constant up to and including the square root of two, the unique global minimizers are precisely the expected rotations of the Hopf map. As the authors note, this had been expected since Ward’s 1999 work.

In the presence of Dirac zero modes, the same circle of ideas yields a sharp lower bound for the magnetic field and characterizes the equality cases in terms of Killing spinors. The authors write that this “yields a sharp criterion for the existence of zero modes and answers a question of Frank-Loss” in three dimensions. This is a strong sign that the inequality is not an isolated curiosity but a structural fact about the sphere’s geometry and the fields that live on it.

Independent assessment

From a writer’s perspective, the most broadly useful contribution may not be the inequality itself but the machinery behind it. The proof introduces a decomposition of the covariant derivative into three parts — one isolating first-order changes in magnitude, one isolating zeroth-order twisting, and a residual part — and pairs it with a sharp Kato-type inequality. That sort of reusable decomposition has a way of migrating into neighboring problems.

Yet an important question raised by earlier work on spinorial Sobolev inequalities remains. The proof of equality requires simultaneous saturation in several independent estimates, and the paper does not fully address whether the intermediate constants can be made uniform across all dimensions. In the closely related spinorial setting, such uniformity has been subtle enough that leaving it open here is not merely a formality.

Trajectory and takeaway

The immediate consequences are already substantial: a sharp curl-Sobolev inequality with full extremal classification, confirmation of a conjecture for the Hopf map energy, global minimality for the Faddeev-Skyrme energy across the predicted coupling range, and a sharp criterion for Dirac zero modes. But the deeper trajectory points toward a family of sharp statements that can now be attempted with the same decomposition. If the tools export to other curvature-sensitive energies, the paper’s legacy will be measured less by the theorem it proves than by the collection of sharp bounds it makes possible. The summit has been claimed; the surrounding ridge line is now visible.
