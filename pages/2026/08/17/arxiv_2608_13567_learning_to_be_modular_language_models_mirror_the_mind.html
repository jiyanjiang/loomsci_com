Learning to Be Modular: Language Models Mirror the Mind

August 17, 2026 Artificial Intelligence Source: arXiv:2608.13567

What if the brain’s division of labor—language here, formal reasoning there, social inference somewhere else—is not an accident of evolutionary history but a law that any sufficiently powerful intelligence must obey? That is the uncomfortable question a team led by Pengrui Han, with Jacob Andreas and Evelina Fedorenko, dares to test in a new preprint (arXiv:2608.13567). They did not scan a single human brain. Instead, they opened up large language models and asked whether their internal parameters had organized themselves into something that looks like the human cortex.

In cognitive neuroscience, this division is not a vague metaphor. Patients with focal brain damage lose one ability while others survive intact; a person can lose the power to name an object while still solving an equation. The human brain is less a general-purpose processor than a city of specialized districts—one neighborhood for language, another for formal reasoning, a third for imagining what other minds believe, a fourth for reasoning about objects and forces. The city metaphor, like all spatial metaphors for the brain, strains if pressed too far: neurons form gradients and overlapping assemblies, not fenced-off boroughs.

Functional specialization is the central concept here. It means different jobs are not distributed evenly across all neurons but concentrated in particular regions. Why does this pattern exist? It could be an evolutionary accident, a frozen quirk of our particular lineage. Or it could be a deep principle—something that any intelligent system, given enough pressure to compute efficiently, will rediscover. Han and colleagues found a way to test this without leaving the digital world.

They turned to attribution patching, a gradient-based technique that estimates how much each neuron contributes to a task, as a computationally cheaper stand-in for directly perturbing every neuron. Think of it as tapping each wire in a vast, humming circuit to hear which connections carry the signal for language and which for arithmetic. The circuit analogy limps, of course: a neural network’s wiring is not discrete, but distributed across billions of floating-point weights that no single wire fully owns. The method worked by constructing minimal pairs. For 46 tasks across four cognitive domains, the researchers designed original and alternative inputs that differed in one crucial feature and thus demanded different correct answers. If the same neurons consistently distinguished those minimal pairs within a domain, that was evidence of a module; if different domains recruited different neurons, that was evidence of segregation.

The result was surprisingly clean. Tasks drawing on the same cognitive domain recruited overlapping sets of neurons. Tasks from different domains recruited distinct populations. The model’s internal city had neighborhoods. This is not to say the model consciously designed them; it is what happens when gradient descent—the optimization process that adjusts weights in response to prediction errors—discovers that specialization pays. The finding mirrors the brain’s own division of labor, yet it emerged from a system with no genes, no developmental path, and no biological reward for efficiency.

For neuroscientists, this is both a gift and a provocation. A language model is not a brain, of course. But as a computational object trained on text, it offers a way to ask whether modularity is something that selection specifically favors, or something that simply arises when a learning system becomes complex enough.

A Skeptic’s Challenge

Here is where the dialectic sharpens. Could this apparent modularity be an artifact of the probing method? Attribution patching is a first-order approximation, not a full causal account. An important question raised by earlier work on neural network modularity—such as the differentiable weight mask study by Csordás and colleagues—is whether tools designed to find modules will always find them, even when the underlying structure is more diffuse. A related line of work on syntactic agreement in these models has shown that different syntactic tasks recruit shared units—which is precisely the within-domain overlap the modular account predicts, not evidence of clean per-task separation. The team acknowledges this. Attribution patching offers a gradient-based causal approximation, and it may not fully rule out confounds such as shared perturbation structures or a fixed neuron budget that forces apparent specialization.

So the researchers did something more decisive. They ran ablations—replacing the activations of the neurons the analysis had flagged as domain-specific with values from the alternative input—and watched what happened to performance. The contrast was stark. Accuracy on tasks within the ablated domain dropped by 25.9%, while tasks outside the domain lost only 2.5%. A tenfold difference. This is not a module that merely appears in a weight mask; it is a module whose removal selectively damages one kind of cognition and leaves others mostly intact. The model is not choosing to be modular. The modularity is doing real work.

This convergence between biological and artificial systems is the paper’s most provocative claim. Natural selection and gradient descent are radically different optimization processes. One operates over millennia through inheritance and death; the other over hours through backpropagation and data. Yet both land on a common architectural compromise: specialized regions that can be independently recruited and, when necessary, independently sacrificed. It is as if two blind architects, working with entirely different tools, each independently arrived at the same floor plan. The architects, we should be clear, are not minds designing something; they are optimization pressures shaping what survives.

Earlier work on the largest public model families, including Llama 3, has documented units that specialize in particular syntactic operations. The new study goes further by linking those units directly to the cognitive domains mapped in the human brain. As the authors put it, “The convergent emergence of modularity in brains and neural networks suggests that it may be a fundamental property of intelligent systems.” That is a measured claim, not a triumphant one. They do not say it is proven. They say it is suggested—and that suggestion, coming from two such different routes to intelligence, is difficult to dismiss.

The Deeper Question

Yet the questions remain open. Does modularity scale with model size? Does it emerge only after enough training, or is it present from the start? The authors are candid: attribution patching is not causal surgery, and future work will need more direct methods. This is a beginning, not an end.

What this research ultimately challenges is our assumption that the brain’s divisions are a biological quirk. Perhaps they are a solution—not the only solution, but a recurring one—to the problem of organizing knowledge. We are used to thinking of intelligence as something that fills whatever container it occupies. The surprise is that two very different containers keep drawing the same map.

We are left not with a definitive answer about the nature of intelligence, but with a sharper question: why do biological evolution and gradient descent converge on the same architecture? In science, a better question is often the most valuable discovery of all.
