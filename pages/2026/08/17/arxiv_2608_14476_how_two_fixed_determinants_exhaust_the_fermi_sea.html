How Two Fixed Determinants Exhaust the Fermi Sea

August 17, 2026 Matter Science Source: arXiv:2608.14476

Quantum many-body physics has a quiet obsession: how much structure must we impose to describe a swarm of electrons? For decades the answer looked heavy. The wavefunction must change sign whenever electrons swap places, and the usual fix is a Slater determinant, a specific antisymmetric arrangement of single-particle states. A preprint (arXiv:2608.14476) by Giuseppe Carleo and Riccardo Rossi at EPFL's Institute of Physics in Lausanne now proves something almost scandalous: a single fixed determinant, specified before you know anything about the system, can do nearly all of it. In the first-order Sobolev norm that governs variational energy, one determinant suffices for any fermionic wavefunction in up to three spatial dimensions; in the stronger norm that controls Monte Carlo variance, at most one more is needed.

Call it the Fermi passport problem. Every electron's wavefunction must carry a signature that flips sign whenever electrons trade places, and a Slater determinant is the standard passport: it encodes that rule in a fixed matrix arrangement. For decades researchers have treated this passport as something to tailor for each system, like a customs officer stamping every passenger's papers by hand. The new preprint says no: a single stamp, manufactured in advance and shared by everyone, is enough, while the rest of the information can travel in a smooth symmetric envelope. The passport imagery has limits, of course—electrons are not passengers, and their signature is not a mark on paper but a sign change in a mathematical amplitude.

The landscape of fermionic representability

Neural quantum states are now the most active frontier in computational fermion physics. Instead of hand-building wavefunctions, researchers train a network to output the amplitude for each electron configuration, and a Monte Carlo procedure roams the configuration space to evaluate energies. The approach has been spectacular for molecules, solids, and cold atoms. But there is a catch: a raw neural network is not automatically antisymmetric. To get the sign-change right, most architectures multiply the network's output by a Slater determinant, or many determinants, and the number has been creeping upward.

If a variational ansatz cannot represent the true ground state, no amount of optimization will close the gap—a problem called variational completeness. The usual argument has been that one determinant is too rigid, so researchers stack large numbers of them. That makes training more expensive and more prone to getting stuck. A minimal universal representation has therefore become a kind of holy grail, with earlier work on exact antisymmetric representations and Bi-Lipschitz ansätze attacking the same question from different angles.

There is a precedent for this kind of conceptual collapse. In density functional theory, the Hohenberg-Kohn theorems showed that the ground-state density, not the full wavefunction, carries all the information. That did not hand practitioners an algorithm, but it reframed the search. Carleo and Rossi's theorem does something similar for fermionic sign structure: it identifies a fixed object that absorbs the antisymmetry, leaving a symmetric object to carry the physics.

What the proof actually says

To see why this matters, recall what a Slater determinant is. If you have a collection of single-electron states, a determinant arranges them in rows and columns so that the whole object automatically changes sign whenever electrons are swapped. That property makes it a valid fermionic wavefunction. The question the paper answers is whether one such determinant, fixed once and for all, can serve as the antisymmetric backbone for any interacting system. The answer, remarkably, is yes in the norms that matter.

The centerpiece is a single theorem with two variants. In the Sobolev norm that governs variational energy, a single universal determinant is enough. The determinant is fixed in advance—independent of the system, the particle number, and the target state—and it simply multiplies a smooth bosonic wavefunction. As the paper states, any fermionic wave function is approximated by a smooth bosonic wave function multiplied by the determinant, which is “specified in advance, independent of both the system and the state." That is a strong statement: the antisymmetry requirement can be outsourced to one prechosen object, and everything else may remain smooth and symmetric.

The surprise deepens when one asks for more control. Variational Monte Carlo does not only care about energy; it also relies on the variance of the local energy, which tells the sampler how noisy the estimates are. That quantity is controlled by a stronger Sobolev norm. There, a single fixed determinant handles every lower-dimensional case; only in the full three-dimensional setting does the proof require another fixed determinant. As the authors put it, "antisymmetry therefore costs at most two universal determinants and no expressiveness." In other words, the stubborn sign-change symmetry does not squeeze the space of possible wavefunctions; it merely demands a fixed, universal signature.

The construction exploits a geometric loophole. A real determinant vanishes on a surface whose removal forces a sharp cutoff and an infinite kinetic-energy price. A complex determinant vanishes on a lower-dimensional set, so a smooth cutoff can be inserted without paying that price. A pair of complex determinants, chosen so their zero sets overlap only in an even smaller region, makes the deletion harmless. This logarithmic-deletion mechanism is what frees the bosonic factor to carry all the remaining expressiveness. It is the precise sense in which the paper concludes that "generalized Slater-Jastrow neural quantum states are variationally complete."

A complex determinant's zero set is so thin that it can be erased with vanishing energy cost, unlike a real determinant's wall-like nodal surface. This makes a fixed complex determinant a universal building block, with smooth factors completing a simple Slater–Jastrow description of fermions. (Source: arXiv:2608.14476)

Two fixed determinant surfaces intersect only at collision points, which can be removed without energy cost. That lets one approximate any smooth fermionic state with just two determinants—a universal shortcut for quantum simulations. (Source: arXiv:2608.14476)

Earlier work has approached the same bottleneck from different directions. One construction, often summarized as "fermions are bosons plus one," shows that any fermionic wavefunction can be lifted exactly to a symmetric function on an enlarged space. A more recent Bi-Lipschitz ansatz offers a different route to efficient sign handling. The new theorem does not compete with these so much as clarify the target: in the norms that actually govern variational calculations, the antisymmetric part can be fixed in advance, which shifts the hard problem to the symmetric part.

An independent assessment

In our reading, this is a landmark because it separates antisymmetry from expressiveness. For a field that has treated determinant count as a proxy for accuracy, the theorem says something more fundamental: the sign structure is not the bottleneck. Researchers can stop worrying about how many determinants to include and focus on the quality of the bosonic factor. That is a conceptual liberation, and it arrives at a moment when neural wavefunctions are becoming the main computational engine across quantum chemistry and condensed matter.

Yet the result is a density theorem, not an algorithm. It guarantees the existence of a bosonic factor and a fixed determinant, but it says nothing about how smooth or complex that factor must be in practice, nor how an optimizer will find it. An important question raised by earlier work on efficient antisymmetric representations is whether the fixed determinant's nodal surface can faithfully track the target state's sign structure. The construction also requires a compact support, which may push fine details into the bosonic factor near the nodal surface. These are not flaws in the theorem, but they mark the distance between a mathematical guarantee and a practical ansatz.

On the practical side, the result suggests that generalized Slater-Jastrow networks, which pair a determinant with a symmetric neural network, need not grow their determinant count as systems scale. That could simplify architectures and reduce the number of parameters devoted to sign structure.

Trajectory and takeaway

The immediate consequence is that determinant selection can become a settled conceptual question rather than an open tuning parameter. The next milestones are more quantitative: constructing bosonic factors that remain compact while capturing strong correlations, and understanding how optimization behaves when the determinant is fixed. If those follow, generalized Slater-Jastrow neural quantum states will move from a clever trick to a guaranteed foundation. The Fermi sea, in this picture, is not an obstacle that demands ever more elaborate scaffolding. It is a symmetry with a surprisingly small price—at most two fixed signatures, and an open door for everything else.
