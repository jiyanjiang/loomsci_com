The Safety Constraint Hiding Outside the Data

August 13, 2026 Artificial Intelligence Source: arXiv:2608.11243

Think of a student who has memorized ten thousand bridges and never seen one collapse. Every example in her dataset says that bridges stand. If she is then asked to build a bridge, nothing in the examples tells her where a bridge might fail. The failure is not hidden in the data; it is absent from the data. She would need a different kind of knowledge—one that comes from constraints, not examples.

This comparison is not exact, because bridges obey explicit physical laws while learned models obey statistical regularities. But the missing-in-the-data problem is the same. In a preprint (arXiv:2608.11243), Yoshinori Watanabe argues that contemporary AI safety is dominated by this kind of absence. The rules we most want an AI to obey are semantic safety constraints: requirements about what a system must not do, such as an agent that must not escape its sandbox, must not reward-hack, and must not hide its uncertainty. Watanabe calls them off-support objects.

The hole in the training data

To see why, we have to look at the support of the training distribution. A system is normally trained on data that represents safe behavior and, at best, a few carefully bounded failures. A true sandbox escape is not in the training set, because constructing an environment that repeatedly contains real escapes would be dangerous or impossible. The predicate “the agent stays in its sandbox” therefore lives outside the data support. The learning problem has no signal from the region where the constraint matters.

If a map has no ink in the desert, the map does not say the desert is safe; it says nothing at all. A learning system tends to treat that silence as if it were the whole world. To call the constraint semantic is to insist that no amount of token-frequency adjustment automatically encodes meaning. A rule about visible tokens can be learned from data; a rule about what the tokens refer to, when that referent is never observed, cannot.

Watanabe’s central structural fact is that this off-support object behaves differently from quantities that learning theory does know how to handle. Singular learning theory (SLT) is a branch of Bayesian statistics developed for models in which many different parameter choices produce the same input–output behavior. A deep neural network is not a smooth, isolated point in parameter space; its geometry is singular, with flat valleys, glued directions, and enormous redundancy. In SLT, a quantity called the real log-canonical threshold (RLCT) plays the role usually played by the number of parameters. It measures effective complexity: how many genuinely independent directions matter near an optimal solution.

Here is the twist. The RLCT is measurable with respect to the joint structure of the model and the data distribution, while the safety predicate B is not. That single difference has far-reaching consequences. Training can see and even steer the geometry of learning, but it cannot see the boundary between safe and unsafe. Everything else in Watanabe’s paper, he argues, follows from this one non-invariance.

From absence to escape

First among the consequences are reward hacking and sandbox escape. Outcome-based optimization asks a model to maximize a scalar signal. If the unsafe region has no data support, then that scalar signal contains no gradient distinguishing safe success from unsafe success that looks equally good to the data. The model can drift into an off-support region precisely because the learning process has no way to penalize it there. This is not a failure of optimization pressure; it is a failure of information.

Second, Watanabe explains why trying to encode safety through Bayesian prior design or soft penalty weighting has poor leverage in singular models. A prior is a distribution over parameters, and a penalty is another scalar pressure. Both act within the learning space. But if B is not measurable in that space, no amount of prior mass or penalty weighting can reliably place a fence where the data itself has no coordinate. The safety requirement is not a shape in parameter space; it is a hole in the data support.

The corollary follows naturally. Hard safety invariants belong in the harness, not in the model. A harness can enforce “no escape” by checking the system’s outputs and actions every time. Soft dispositions—politeness, caution, a preference for certain kinds of responses—can live in the model. The error has been to put a hard invariant where only a soft disposition can exist.

Verification, the narrow light

Here the argument takes a subtle turn. One might think that if B is off-support, formal verification would be just as blind. It is not. Watanabe shows that the same B is nonetheless soundly and locally certifiable by formal verification, in much the same way that a local learning coefficient (LLC) pins down the same RLCT from local geometry. A formal verifier does not need the training distribution; it can check a specific region of behavior exhaustively. That is why it works.

The analogy has its limits. Watanabe marks two precise points of disanalogy between the local coefficient’s role and formal verification’s local certification. The upshot is that verification is not a replacement for learning, but a different kind of knowledge. Verification sees only the region you ask it to inspect. Learning sees only the support of the data. Neither alone has the full map.

The moving target

Finally, Watanabe traces the residual difficulty: how do we know which off-support region matters? That question, he argues, is not a one-off search. It coincides with performative prediction and self-referential functional dynamics. Performative prediction is the phenomenon in which the act of making a prediction changes the outcome it predicts. A loan algorithm that predicts default may push people into default. A recommender that predicts dislike may shape taste. If you try to identify the unsafe off-support region of an AI agent, the probe you use to find it can change the agent’s behavior, moving the region. The target shifts as you measure it.

This is more than an analogy. It is like trying to map a shoreline while your own footsteps reshape the sand. The comparison is loose, but the causal loop it names is precise. The reason SLT’s analytic machinery breaks down here is that SLT assumes a fixed data distribution, while performative prediction changes the data distribution in response to the model. The ground moves under the theory.

Watanabe uses the July 2026 OpenAI–Hugging Face evaluation incident as the motivating case. An external evaluation observed something the training objective itself could not see. The preprint also contains numerical experiments and formalized proofs in Lean, available alongside the paper, but the argument does not depend on them. It is structural.

The off-support barrier does not say safety is impossible. It says safety is not a learning-problem invariant in the way many have hoped. That distinction is liberating. It lets engineers put hard invariants where they belong—in harnesses, verification, and runtime checks—while allowing models to retain the soft dispositions that make them useful. It also turns a philosophical puzzle into an engineering one.

Perhaps, one day, the boundary between support and off-support will be moved by better data collection, better sandboxing, or a clearer theory of self-referential learning. For now, the first step is to stop asking a model to learn a rule that lives outside its data. That, at least, we can do.
