Proving That Spacetime Must Split: Bartnik's Conjecture Resolved

Drafted June 03, 2026 · Published June 04, 2026 Mathematics Source: arXiv:2606.03873

For nearly four decades, a single mathematical question has stood at the boundary of our understanding of spacetime: can a universe that is globally well-behaved and filled with ordinary matter ever escape the grip of singularities? In 1988, the mathematician Robert Bartnik sharpened this into a precise conjecture. If true, it would say that any cosmos that is free of singularities, causally tame, and gravitationally attractive must be rigidly uniform — a static spatial geometry sliding through time like a single, unvarying frame. The conjecture became a cornerstone of the effort to understand the precise link between matter, geometry, and the inevitability of gravitational collapse. Now, mathematicians Robert McCann and Argam Ohanyan have provided the first complete proof. Their work appears in a preprint (arXiv:2606.03873).

The quest to prove Bartnik's conjecture belongs to a broader programme: the rigidity of spacetime. The famous Hawking–Penrose singularity theorems show that under generic conditions, a universe like ours must have begun in a singularity. But those theorems rely on global arguments; the splitting conjecture asks a sharper question: if a spacetime is everywhere well-behaved — no singularities, a well-defined flow of time, compact spatial slices, and matter that only attracts — must it be a simple product of a time line and a fixed space? Proving this would provide a kind of converse to the singularity theorems, demonstrating that singularity-free spacetimes are essentially static and utterly rare.

Partial results accumulated over the decades. Galloway, Newman, and others made significant headway, but a full proof remained elusive. The difficulty lay in the need to construct a global time function smooth enough to show that the spacetime must split. Standard techniques from Riemannian geometry — the celebrated Cheeger–Gromoll splitting theorem — could not be directly transplanted to the Lorentzian setting because the distance function along timelike curves is far less cooperative. What McCann and Ohanyan achieved is a synthesis of two powerful, previously separate technologies: the viscosity solutions of the Lorentzian eikonal equation developed by Zhu, Wu, and Cui, and the elliptic (p)-d'Alembertian approach that the authors themselves had helped pioneer in earlier joint work with Braun, Gigli, and Sämann.

The conjecture itself is elegantly stated. A spacetime is globally hyperbolic if it possesses a Cauchy surface — a spatial slice from which every past and future event can be determined. The strong energy condition requires that matter always exerts an attractive influence on the geometry. And timelike geodesic completeness means that any freely-falling observer can continue indefinitely, never hitting an edge. The conjecture then asserts: “a timelike geodesically complete, globally hyperbolic spacetime which has compact Cauchy surfaces and satisfies the strong energy condition must split isometrically as a Lorentzian product.” In other words, if the cosmos is free of singularities and filled with ordinary stuff, it cannot help but be a uniform expanse.

The proof unfolds by studying the distance from a faraway spatial hypersurface. Viscosity solutions give a robust notion of the distance function even where it fails to be smooth — a kind of minimal supersolution to the eikonal equation. McCann and Ohanyan then bring in the (p)-d'Alembertian, a family of nonlinear elliptic operators that, for (p<1), act as sensitive detectors of spacetime rigidity. They show that the distance function constructed via viscosity satisfies a weak (p)-d'Alembertian comparison — an inequality that, in the limit, forces the function to behave as if the operator vanished. From this, they deduce that the Busemann function — the asymptotic slope of the distance — is actually smooth and that its gradient is parallel. That parallelism forces the metric to split. As the authors put it, their methods “combine the construction of global viscosity solutions to the Lorentzian eikonal equation by Zhu–Wu–Cui with our recently developed elliptic approach.”

The achievement is genuine, but it also invites a closer look. An important question sharpened by the earlier (p)-d'Alembertian work of Braun et al. is whether the elliptic regularity needed for the comparison principle holds uniformly across the entire spacetime, especially near the cut locus where the distance function is not naturally smooth. The current proof demonstrates the vanishing of the (p)-d'Alembertian on the region where the distance is smooth, and then argues that the viscosity solution itself smooths out the rough edges through a strong tangency property. Yet a fully self-contained verification of the critical step — that the viscosity eikonal solution is simultaneously smooth and has vanishing (p)-d'Alembertian everywhere — would strengthen the result and make it more widely applicable. This is not a flaw but a refinement left for future work. The proof, as it stands, is still a convincing breakthrough that resolves a decades-old open problem.

What this proof provides is a missing cornerstone. The Hawking–Penrose singularity theorem tells us that under very general conditions, spacetime must begin in a big bang. But it left open the possibility of a rigid, perfectly uniform cosmos that somehow avoids a beginning. Bartnik's conjecture was the statement that such a cosmos is mathematically impossible. The conjecture is now confirmed. The methods McCann and Ohanyan introduced — fusing viscosity solutions with the (p)-d'Alembertian — are likely to become a standard toolkit for tackling other rigidity questions in Lorentzian geometry, from extensions of the positive mass theorem to splitting theorems in the presence of a cosmological constant. The door that was ajar for nearly forty years is at last wide open.
