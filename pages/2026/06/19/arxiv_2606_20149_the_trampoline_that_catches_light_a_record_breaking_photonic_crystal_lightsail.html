The Trampoline That Catches Light: A Record-Breaking Photonic Crystal Lightsail

June 19, 2026 Matter Science Source: arXiv:2606.20149

Picture a trampoline woven from spider silk, suspended in a dark room. Now imagine training a spotlight on it — not to illuminate, but to push. The trampoline is so impossibly thin that it ought to melt or tear. Instead, it bounces. Not because anything touched it, but because light itself, that weightless wash of photons we swim through every day, has leaned against it hard enough to make it move.

This is not an allegorical thought experiment. A team led by Richard A. Norte at Delft University of Technology has built exactly such a trampoline — a photonic crystal membrane just two hundred nanometers thick, patterned with billions of holes, suspended on delicate tethers across a millimetre-wide gap — and they have measured, for the first time in a structure of this scale, the tangible push of light. Their work appears in a preprint (arXiv:2606.20149) that sets a new benchmark for what ultrathin materials can survive when a high-power laser gets serious.

The idea of a lightsail — a spacecraft propelled not by rocket fuel but by beamed optical energy — has hovered at the edge of serious engineering for decades. The logic is beguilingly simple. Light carries momentum. Bounce light off a mirror, and the mirror recoils. Make the mirror light enough, and it accelerates. Make it fast enough, and perhaps it reaches a nearby star within a human lifetime.

The catch lies in the word "enough." To intercept a meaningful fraction of a laser's push, the sail must be large. To accelerate under that push, it must be light — which means thin. And to survive the laser's intensity without absorbing enough heat to vaporize itself, it must reflect nearly perfectly. These three constraints — large area, ultralow mass, high reflectivity — pull in opposite directions. Physicists and engineers have been triangulating between them for years, each incremental advance on one front retreating on another.

What Norte and his colleagues have done is to bring all three demands into coexistence for the first time in a single device, at a scale where the resulting motion from radiation pressure alone crosses into the visible. When a high-power near-infrared laser was aimed at their gossamer trampoline, it responded with a displacement more than twice the size of a typical bacterium — 1.75 micrometres — moving in direct response to nothing but incoming photons.

To appreciate what makes this possible, we need to understand the device itself, and here the trampoline analogy turns out to be more than a casual metaphor. The team's membrane is not a continuous sheet. It is a photonic crystal — a layer of silicon nitride, itself thinner than the wavelength of the laser light it reflects, sculpted with a periodic array of circular holes. Each hole is precisely 657 nanometres across, arranged in a repeating pattern with a spacing of 862 nanometres. To the naked eye, the membrane would look like something between lace and a diffraction grating. To the laser, it looks like a mirror.

This is the first layer of the conceptual architecture: a properly designed photonic crystal can reflect 99% of incident light even though it is only a fraction of the wavelength thick. The mechanism is not simple metallic reflection. It is resonance — the periodic pattern of holes sets up standing optical modes that collectively cancel transmission, forcing the light back the way it came. The physics is intricate, but the result is stark: a mirror that weighs almost nothing.

A tiny trampoline etched with a light-trapping pattern moves under gentle laser pressure. This motion could one day propel ultra-thin sails for interstellar spacecraft. (Source: arXiv:2606.20149)

But reflectivity alone is not enough. A rigid mirror cannot move far under the gentle pressure of photons. The second architectural layer is compliance. Norte's team suspended their photonic crystal on four hair-thin tethers, creating what they call a trampoline geometry. The tethering decouples the stiffness of the pad from the stiffness of the supports, so that the entire structure yields gracefully to the faintest optical force. This is the crucial engineering insight: by scaling the device up in area while keeping the tethers slender, the compliance grows with the design, not against it.

The fabrication process is itself a minor marvel. The team starts with a silicon wafer coated in silicon nitride. They pattern the photonic crystal holes and the trampoline outline using electron-beam lithography, then release the structure from the underlying silicon with an isotropic etch — a chemical process that eats away the silicon substrate uniformly, freeing the suspended membrane without damaging its nanometre-thin features. The resulting devices are the largest subwavelength-thickness photonic crystals ever made: millimetres across, billions of holes, each one a precise aperture in a precisely engineered optical resonator.

A large, ultra-thin lightsail made of silicon nitride can be released from its silicon base without tearing. This ensures the sail can withstand laser radiation pressure for future space propulsion. (Source: arXiv:2606.20149)

When the laser strikes the suspended trampoline, the displacement is immediate and measurable. In the first instants, the membrane moves toward the laser — negative displacement, in the team's sign convention — pushed by the momentum transfer of reflected photons. This is the pure radiation-pressure regime, the clean signal that lightsail researchers dream of. The total excursion, while minuscule in absolute terms, is fifty thousand times larger than any previous lightsail optomechanical response. It is a displacement you could see under a simple optical microscope, a macroscopic shudder driven by the most ethereal of causes.

But here the story takes a turn that is simultaneously sobering and instructive, and it is worth pausing to understand why. The trampoline absorbs a small fraction of the laser's energy — even at 99% reflectivity, that fraction is not zero — and the absorbed energy becomes heat. The heat does not distribute evenly across the pad. The laser's beam has a Gaussian profile, bright at the center and falling off toward the edges, and the resulting temperature gradient creates compressive stress. The center of the pad, hotter than its margins, wants to expand but is constrained. It buckles.

Thermal buckling is not a subtle phenomenon. When it sets in, the trampoline's effective stiffness collapses, and the displacement grows rapidly — no longer primarily from radiation pressure, but from the mechanical release of built-up thermal stress. The team observed this transition clearly in their measurements: the eigenfrequency of the device, a sensitive indicator of stiffness, began to decay even at low optical powers, long before the total displacement reached its peak. The final large deformation is a hybrid — part photon push, part thermal buckle, and disentangling the two quantitatively is not something the current experiment achieves.

This matters because it cuts to the heart of what lightsail research is ultimately for. An important question sharpened by earlier work on interstellar propulsion, notably a comprehensive 2016 roadmap by Philip Lubin and colleagues (arXiv:1604.01356), is whether a sail can survive sustained illumination at the gigawatt-per-square-metre intensities that a real interstellar mission would demand. The answer, at least for the materials and geometries tested here, is not yet. The thermal buckling observed by Norte's team appears at power densities below the Starshot requirement, and the experiment provides no physical basis for extrapolating from a sub-millisecond transient to the sustained acceleration that would push a gram-scale craft across light-years.

None of this diminishes what the Delft team has accomplished. It simply clarifies what the accomplishment is. This is not an interstellar propulsion demonstration. It is something arguably more valuable at this stage: a working testbed where the competing demands of reflectivity, mass, area, and thermal load can be studied together in a single structure, at the limits of what nanofabrication can currently achieve.

There is a broader narrative here about the relationship between incremental laboratory advances and the grand visions that motivate them. The Breakthrough Starshot concept, for example, envisions a sail that can survive laser intensities several gigawatts per square metre — an order of magnitude beyond what the Delft device has been shown to handle. But you cannot engineer a gigawatt-capable sail without first understanding what happens at lower intensities, when the sail is large enough to study without the distorting effects of tight laser focusing, when the displacement is large enough to measure cleanly, when the device is compliant enough to reveal the interplay between optical and thermal forces, and when the fabrication process is reliable enough to produce these fragile structures at scale. That is precisely what this paper provides.

The work also sits in an interesting relationship with alternative lightsail architectures. A recent experimental study by Campbell and collaborators (arXiv:2508.05035) demonstrated that corrugated nanolaminate films can achieve high reflectivity while being relatively straightforward to manufacture — a different point in the design space, trading some of the photonic crystal's exquisite optical control for fabrication simplicity. The Delft trampolines occupy the opposite corner: optically optimized, structurally delicate, exacting to produce. Neither approach has yet solved the thermal problem, and it is not obvious that either approach alone ever will. The question, for those who think seriously about laser-driven interstellar travel, is whether a hybrid architecture — photonic crystal optical surfaces on a corrugated thermal substrate, perhaps — might combine the strengths of both. The team's fabrication platform now makes it possible to start asking such questions experimentally.

This experimental advance does not bring Proxima Centauri closer. It brings something subtler: the ability to watch, in exquisite detail, what happens when ultralight materials meet ultrabright light. The Delft trampoline is not a spacecraft. It is a laboratory for the physics that might one day make spacecraft possible, and what it reveals is that the space between vision and implementation is filled with genuine, interesting science.

Perhaps, in the decades to come, when the thermal buckling problem is solved and the first true lightsail leaves the laboratory, the researchers who built it will look back on these trampolines — these delicate millimetre-wide webs that first showed, in a dark room in Delft, that light can push hard enough to see. They will recognize them as the moment when lightsail research stopped being a thought experiment and became an experimental science.
