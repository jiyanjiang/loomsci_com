Connecting Localization and Gravity: The Hidden Transition in Rotated Space

Drafted June 01, 2026 · Published June 06, 2026 Matter Science Source: arXiv:2601.20931

What does a quantum particle getting stuck in a disordered lattice have to do with the curvature of spacetime? At first glance, the two phenomena inhabit entirely separate domains of physics—one solid-state, one cosmological. But a new preprint by Pasquale Marra at Sophia University (arXiv:2601.20931) suggests that the link may be closer than anyone suspected, hiding in the mathematics we use to describe quantum states.

The story begins with one of the cleanest models of localization: the Aubry–André model. Unlike Anderson localization, which relies on random disorder, the Aubry–André model uses a quasiperiodic potential—a pattern that never quite repeats, like the tiles of a Penrose mosaic. When this potential is strong enough, electron wavefunctions become trapped; when it is weak, they spread across the lattice. The transition between these two regimes is sharp and well understood. But Marra noticed something: the standard Aubry–André model treats position and momentum as fixed coordinates. What if we allowed ourselves to rotate them?

Rethinking the Coordinates of a Quantum Lattice

In quantum mechanics, position and momentum are canonically conjugate operators—they obey the famous commutation relation [ x, p ] = i ℏ. This relationship is fundamental, but it is not unique. You can replace x and p with any pair of operators that satisfy the same algebra, effectively rotating the phase space. Think of it like rotating a map: what was north becomes north-west, and your sense of direction shifts accordingly. The physics should remain the same, because the commutation relation is preserved.

Marra constructed a family of Hamiltonians by taking the Aubry–André model and substituting the original position and momentum operators with an arbitrary pair of canonically conjugate operators X and Y. These new operators are linear combinations of x and p: a rotation in the abstract space of canonical operators. The resulting model looks different—the hopping amplitudes become periodically modulated—but it is mathematically equivalent to the original. Or so it seemed.

A periodically modulated lattice swaps the roles of position and momentum through a subtle rotation. This hidden swap causes unexpected transitions between localized and extended states. (Source: arXiv:2601.20931)

What Marra discovered is that this rotated model harbours a hidden localization transition. In one rotated basis, the wavefunctions are localized; in the conjugate basis, they are delocalized. The system behaves as an insulator in one coordinate system and a metal in the other. This is not a contradiction—it is a feature of the new coordinates. The transition occurs when the two hopping amplitudes in the rotated Hamiltonian become equal, a condition that marks the boundary between regimes where the system is localized in X and delocalized in Y, or vice versa.

The Signature of Multifractality

To characterize this hidden transition, Marra computed two measures: the inverse participation ratio (IPR) and the normalized participation ratio (NPR). The IPR tells you whether a wavefunction is spread out (small IPR) or concentrated (large IPR). The NPR does the opposite—it is large when the wavefunction is spread. In the conventional Aubry–André model, the IPR jumps from near zero to near one at the transition. But in the rotated model, something stranger happens: right at the transition, both the IPR and the NPR vanish simultaneously in the thermodynamic limit.

A sharp transition between localized and delocalized quantum states appears when a key parameter crosses zero. This hidden shift determines how electrons behave, impacting the design of novel materials with tailored conductivity. (Source: arXiv:2601.20931)

This simultaneous vanishing is the hallmark of a multifractal state. A multifractal wavefunction is neither fully extended nor completely localized—it has pockets of structure on many scales, like a coastline that looks jagged whether you view it from a satellite or from the shore. The states at the hidden transition exhibit a non-integer fractal dimension, between zero and one, in both the rotated and the original bases. They are, in a sense, hovering between order and disorder, belonging fully to neither regime.

This is not merely a mathematical curiosity. Multifractal states appear in a range of physical systems, from the metal–insulator transition in disordered systems to the quantum Hall effect. But here they emerge from a purely deterministic, quasiperiodic potential, and they do so in a way that is precisely controlled by the rotation angle. The model provides a tunable laboratory for studying multifractality.

When a Lattice Becomes a Spacetime

The most surprising finding comes when Marra examines the Hamiltonian precisely at the transition point. There, the model coincides with the lattice Hamiltonian of a massless Dirac fermion moving in a curved spacetime background. This is the language of analog gravity: systems where the mathematics of general relativity emerges from condensed-matter physics. The same equations that describe electrons in graphene near the Dirac point also describe particles in a gravitational field. Marra's model offers a new, and remarkably simple, platform for this connection.

The emergence of curved spacetime from a straightforward modification of a well-known model is unexpected. It suggests that the connection between localization and gravity is not accidental but may be woven into the fabric of quantum mechanics itself. The hidden transition is not just a phase change in an abstract operator space; it is a doorway between different ways of describing reality.

An important question raised by earlier work on analog gravity is whether such connections are robust or merely coincidental. Marra's result sits in an interesting tension with the common view that gravitational analogues require carefully engineered systems. Here, a simple rotation of operators—a mathematical transformation—is enough to reveal the same structure. This implies that the geometry of spacetime may be encoded in the algebra of quantum operators, waiting to be uncovered.

What This Means

Marra's work does not claim to have built a gravitational analogue in the lab. The model is theoretical, and the transition occurs at a fine-tuned point in parameter space. But it reveals something about the architecture of quantum theory: that the choices we make about which operators to use are not neutral. They can hide entire phase transitions, and sometimes those hidden transitions point to deeper connections.

For condensed-matter physicists, the rotated Aubry–André model offers a new paradigm for studying localization and multifractality. For high-energy theorists, it provides a concrete, tractable example of how gravitational physics can emerge from a simple lattice Hamiltonian. And for anyone who wonders whether the universe's laws are all connected—the answer, at least in this preprint, is a quiet yes.
