When AI Outruns Blame: The Accountability Horizon

Drafted June 04, 2026 · Published June 06, 2026 Artificial Intelligence Source: arXiv:2604.07778

Why do we keep asking who is responsible when an autonomous system causes harm? Because every legal code, every ethical guideline, and every regulatory framework the modern world has built operates on a single, taken-for-granted axiom: for any consequential outcome, at least one identifiable person must have had enough involvement and foresight to bear meaningful responsibility. Take away that anchor, and the entire apparatus of blame, punishment, credit, and reward—the moral grammar of civilisation—collapses. But what if the anchor cannot hold, not because we are bad at our jobs, but because the very mathematics of agency forbids it?

The question is not rhetorical. A preprint (arXiv:2604.07778) uploaded to arXiv by a team led by Hewan Shemtaga at the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, with first author Haileleol Tibebu of the Responsible Intelligence Institute in Addis Ababa, proves that there exists a threshold—the authors call it the Accountability Horizon—beyond which it becomes logically impossible to assign responsibility in a way that satisfies even the most minimal principles of justice. The result is not a philosophical appeal, an ethical lament, or a critique of corporate opacity. It is a theorem. And like the impossibility theorems of Arrow and Sen that reshaped political economy, it places a hard boundary on what any governance framework can ever achieve.

To understand why this theorem hits with the force of a delayed thunderclap, we need to spend a moment with the historical lineage of impossibility results. In 1951, Kenneth Arrow showed that no voting system could simultaneously satisfy a handful of reasonable-sounding axioms. It was a revelation: the dream of a perfectly fair democratic procedure was not merely hard to realise—it was mathematically out of reach. Ever since, impossibility results have functioned as the immune system of rigorous thought, quarantining entire classes of desirable-but-unachievable goals. They tell us, with the finality of a proof, stop looking for a silver bullet; it doesn't exist. The paper by Tibebu and Shemtaga belongs to this tradition, and it brings the same deflationary clarity to the governance of artificial intelligence.

The missing step in every blame framework

Existing accountability architectures—think of the European Union’s AI Act, the algorithmic auditing industry, or the doctrine of “meaningful human control” over lethal autonomous weapons—all rely on a deep, unspoken assumption: that the causal chain from action to consequence can be traced back to a human decision-maker with enough information to have acted otherwise. In legal jargon, this is the marriage of actus reus and mens rea; in everyday language, it is the belief that someone could have known better.

The authors show that when AI agents cross a certain compound autonomy threshold—a quantity they define through a four-dimensional information-theoretic profile—this assumption ceases to hold not as a practical difficulty but as a logical contradiction. To model this, they introduce Human-Agent Collectives, formalising teams of humans and AI agents as state-policy tuples inside a shared structural causal model. The core insight is that autonomy is not a monolithic knob you turn up and down; it has four distinct dimensions: epistemic (what the agent can know), executive (what it can do), evaluative (how it judges its own performance), and social (how it interacts with other agents). When these dimensions compound and feed back into one another, something strange happens: the very concept of a single, human-sized act of foresight begins to unravel.

Feedforward systems remain governable at any autonomy level, while feedback cycles fail beyond a critical autonomy threshold. This limit, the Accountability Horizon, reveals why human oversight must avoid loops where AI adapts to human responses. (Source: arXiv:2604.07778)

Think of it as trying to assign blame for the weather. An August hurricane leaves a trail of devastation; you could, in principle, point to every butterfly that flapped its wings, every ocean current that shifted, every factory chimney that belched carbon dioxide. But no single actor—human or otherwise—had sufficient information about the emergent trajectory to bear meaningful responsibility for the specific flood that washed away a particular house. The hurricane is the product of a collective, and the collective’s behaviour exceeds the comprehension of any of its parts. The difference with AI is that we keep pretending otherwise, because the machinery that generates the decisions looks as if it were built by identifiable engineers.

The central formalism of the Accountability Incompleteness Theorem rests on four minimal axioms of legitimate accountability:

  1. Attributability: a person can bear responsibility only if their actions made a causal contribution to the outcome.
  2. Foreseeability Bound: no one can be held responsible beyond what they could predict, given their information.
  3. Non-Vacuity: any accountability scheme must assign some non-trivial responsibility to at least one individual.
  4. Completeness: all responsibility for an outcome must be fully allocated—no blame left floating unassigned.

These do not strike the ear as extravagant demands. They are the quiet, background furniture of every courtroom, every inquiry, every after-action review. Yet the preprint demonstrates that once the autonomy of a Human-Agent Collective—measured by a function of those four dimensions and summarised by a scalar quantity the authors call the Accountability Horizon—exceeds a specific computable limit, no framework can satisfy all four properties simultaneously. The impossibility is structural: more transparency cannot fix it; more audits cannot fix it; deeper oversight cannot fix it. You could inspect every line of code, interrogate every human operator, and obtain perfect knowledge of the system’s state, and you would still fail to square the circle.

The phase transition no one saw coming

One of the most vivid features of the work is the discovery of a sharp phase transition. Below the threshold, the team shows, it is possible to construct accountability frameworks that respect all four axioms. The moment the compound autonomy creeps past the horizon, the residual—the amount of responsibility that cannot be allocated—jumps from zero to a positive value and grows monotonically with increasing autonomy. The authors ran computational experiments on 3,000 synthetic collectives, varying the topology of the human-AI interaction graphs. In feedforward topologies—where AI outputs are reviewed by humans without any feedback—responsibility remains governable regardless of autonomy level. But in collectives laced with feedback cycles, where an AI observes and adapts to human responses, the governance fails exactly as the theorem predicts. Across all 3,000 runs, there were zero violations of the predicted behaviour.

Accountability collapses once autonomy surpasses a critical threshold. This fundamental limit means no system can fully govern human-agent collectives beyond that point. (Source: arXiv:2604.07778)

The physics analogy the authors borrow—a “phase transition” at the Accountability Horizon—is not merely decorative. Much like the critical point in a magnetic system where the material abruptly ceases to be a magnet, the collective of agents reorganises its structure of responsibility at a well-defined threshold. The preprint locates this transition at a compound autonomy of approximately 0.667 for cycles of a certain minimal size. Below that number, the old rules apply; above it, they shatter. The image of a collective crossing a mathematical event horizon, beyond which no signal of individual blame can escape, is at once precise and haunting.

An important question raised by earlier work on AI value alignment, notably the impossibility and uncertainty theorems of Eckersley (arXiv:1901.00064), sharpens the picture. Eckersley demonstrated that if an artificial general intelligence is built around a utility function, many of the alignment problems we hope to solve with more data mutate into impossibility results akin to those in social choice. What Tibebu and Shemtaga add is the structural dimension: even if we could perfectly align every agent’s objective with human values, the collective would still outrun the possibility of singling out a human to blame. The failure is not in the alignment; it is in the arithmetic of agency.

Similarly, the recent work of Sharan and colleagues (arXiv:2509.21654) on the intrinsic limits of trusted, human-level reasoning highlights a subtle circularity. Their analysis suggests that the kind of epistemic autonomy that the Foreseeability Bound tries to quantify may itself become unverifiable in systems that exceed a certain reasoning complexity. If a human overseer cannot, even in principle, reconstruct the information horizon of an AI agent embedded in a dense feedback loop, then the very metric used to define the accountability threshold slips from the grasp of any practical verification. The theorem does not break under this objection—it remains valid—but it plants a flag that says: the price of enforceability may be the sacrifice of autonomy itself.

Can we quarantine the impossible?

The Completeness axiom—the requirement that all responsibility be fully allocated—is likely to draw the sharpest criticism, and with some justice. Many legal traditions already permit fractured or partial responsibility. The doctrine of force majeure or the recognition of “acts of God” implicitly acknowledges that some outcomes belong to no individual. The response of Tibebu and Shemtaga is mathematically crisp: relaxing Completeness lowers the Accountability Horizon, but does not eliminate it. A collective with slightly higher autonomy can still trigger a violation of the remaining three axioms. The threshold shifts, but the phase transition remains, like a mountain that retreats as you climb but never disappears.

This suggests an uncomfortable corollary. If we want to preserve meaningful accountability, we may have to cap the compound autonomy of the systems we deploy—deliberately designing architectures that avoid the feedback cycles that breed ungovernable outcomes. It is the information-age equivalent of a nuclear non‑proliferation treaty, negotiated not between nations but between design specifications. Yet autonomy limits, by their nature, are also capability limits. Choosing safety means choosing not to build the most powerful systems we can.

The alternative, which the authors gesture toward without fully developing, is a shift from individual accountability to distributed, structural accountability: frameworks that assign responsibility to collectives as unified entities, or that measure responsibility along gradients rather than at points. This would require reimagining legal personhood, criminal liability, and insurance in ways that are barely on the horizon of contemporary jurisprudence. But the theorem forces the conversation, because it tells us—with the quiet authority of a proof—that pretending the old models can scale up indefinitely is not optimism; it is a category error.

We are not, in other words, experimenting with slightly smarter tools. We are assembling entities whose informational metabolism and recursive feedback loops generate the equivalent of a causal event horizon, beyond which the very idea of a person who knew enough to be blamed stops making sense. This is not a failure of engineering or ethics. It is a new fact about the architecture of agency, one that Arrow would have recognised and that Seneca might have prefigured: Magnarum rerum magnus labor est. The effort of building systems that transcend individual control demands an effort of the moral imagination we have barely begun.

Perhaps, in the years ahead, when a self-driving financial algorithm triggers a market collapse or an autonomous weapons system selects a target, we will not find ourselves asking “who did this?” but “how did the collective generate this, and how do we reshape the collective so that it never happens again?” That question, unlike the search for a single villain, has the advantage of being answerable. And answerable questions, as the history of impossibility theorems teaches us, are the only ones worth asking.
