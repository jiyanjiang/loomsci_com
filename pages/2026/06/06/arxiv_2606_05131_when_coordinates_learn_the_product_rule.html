When Coordinates Learn the Product Rule

Drafted June 04, 2026 · Published June 06, 2026 Artificial Intelligence Source: arXiv:2606.05131

Imagine a cartographer who must draw a map of a landscape that never stops shifting. The ground twists, rivers change course, and any fixed coordinate grid soon becomes useless. But this cartographer has a secret weapon: a theory that promises that if she only maps how the landscape affects what she can measure, the chaos will become linear. The idea goes back to the 1930s, when the mathematician Bernard Koopman showed that any deterministic dynamical system—however tangled—can be represented as a linear operator evolving functions of the state, not the state itself. If you can find the right “eigenfunctions,” each of them simply gets multiplied by a complex number every time the clock ticks, like the pure tones of a vibrating string. Then the future of any observable is just a superposition of these independently evolving tones.

The Koopman picture is beautiful. But how do you actually find those eigenfunctions from data? That question is the central struggle of modern Koopman learning. A preprint (arXiv:2606.05131) from a team led by Matthew J. Colbrook at Imperial College London and the University of Cambridge, with first author Kelan Gray, tackles the problem head‑on with a method called deep embedded multiplicative dynamic mode decomposition (DeepMDMD). Their insight is as bold as it is simple: teach the coordinates to obey the algebraic rules that the true Koopman operator must satisfy, and do so while letting them learn their own shape from the data.

The product rule that must be obeyed

To see why algebra matters, picture a system as simple as a pendulum. Its angle and angular velocity trace a loop in a two‑dimensional space, but the Koopman theory says you should think in terms of observables—quantities like “sin(angle)” or “energy.” The Koopman operator advances any observable forward by one time step. The crucial feature is that this operator is multiplicative: evolving a product of two observables gives exactly the product of the evolved observables. In symbols, K(f·g) = (Kf)·(Kg). If you have eigenfunctions phi₁ and phi₂ with eigenvalues lambda₁ and lambda₂, then their product phi₁·phi₂ is also an eigenfunction with eigenvalue lambda₁·lambda₂. This is not some formal decoration; it is the deep algebraic structure that keeps the Koopman representation internally consistent.

The workhorse method for approximating the Koopman operator from data is dynamic mode decomposition, or DMD. In its basic form, you pick a finite dictionary of functions—maybe a few hundred monomials or Fourier modes—and you compute the best linear fit that advances the whole dictionary one time step. The problem is that a finite dictionary is almost never closed under multiplication. Multiply two of your functions and you land somewhere outside the dictionary, and the best‑fit operator no longer respects the product rule. This leads to spectral pollution: eigenvalues that wander off the unit circle and forecasts that accumulate errors like a message passed from person to person, distorting with each retelling. (Unlike a message distorted by gossip, however, the pollution here reflects a fundamental failure to preserve algebraic relations, not mere noise.)

What Gray and colleagues realized is that you can cure this problem if you design the dictionary itself as a learned partition of a latent space, and then force the Koopman approximation to satisfy the multiplicative law exactly on that partition. They call the procedure DeepMDMD, and it works by alternating two steps that look like a dance between an autoencoder and a discrete‑time master equation.

Learning coordinates from data

The first step compresses the full state—say, a 158,624‑dimensional velocity field from a cylinder wake—down to a low‑dimensional latent space using a neural‑network autoencoder. Inside that latent space, the method creates a partition, a set of N cells. The dictionary consists of the indicator functions of those cells; if a data point falls in cell n, the corresponding dictionary entry is 1, otherwise 0. The twist is that the partition is not fixed by some geometric rule, as in ordinary MDMD. It is learned, and it is learned while the Koopman matrix K is being computed so that it simultaneously captures the dynamics and respects the product rule.

The algorithm proceeds iteratively. Given a current partition, it computes the optimal multiplicative DMD matrix—the one that advances the cell functions forward exactly one time step while satisfying the condition that the product of two cell functions, when evolved, matches the product of the evolved functions. This is a non‑trivial algebraic constraint, but the authors show it can be solved efficiently. Once the matrix is fixed, the autoencoder’s weights and the positions of the cell centroids are updated by gradient descent. During this update, the hard cell membership is replaced by a soft assignment: each latent point contributes to multiple cells via a Student’s t‑kernel, making the whole pipeline differentiable. The two steps repeat, each sharpening the other, until the reconstruction error settles.

The result is a compact set of learned coordinates—the cell functions—that together form a closed algebra. Multiply any two cell functions and you get a linear combination of other cell functions within the same partition. Because of that closure, the finite‑dimensional Koopman approximation inherits the exact multiplicative property of the true infinite‑dimensional operator. Its non‑zero eigenvalues lie on the unit circle, and the dictionary is shaped by the dynamics rather than by an arbitrary geometric choice. This is what the authors mean by “algebra‑preserving” Koopman learning.

The team tested DeepMDMD on a suite of systems that push conventional methods to their limits. For a nonlinear pendulum, the learned dictionary remained robustly compact, while geometric MDMD partitions needed far more cells to achieve the same accuracy. In the chaotic Lorenz‑96 system, DeepMDMD’s eigenvalues hugged the unit circle tightly, whereas standard extended DMD produced a spray of spurious modes. The method then scaled to high‑dimensional fluid flows. In a cylinder wake represented by 158,624 degrees of freedom, DeepMDMD gave stable latent‑space forecasts even when 40% Gaussian noise was added after training; state‑space MDMD, by contrast, collapsed almost immediately. A noisy lid‑driven cavity at Reynolds number 20,000 pushed the advantage farther: only DeepMDMD preserved coherent vorticity structures and the long‑time autocorrelation statistics.

The soft‑to‑hard gap and what remains

The performance is striking, but the paper is honest about what it leaves unresolved. The training uses soft cell assignments to make the clustering differentiable; the final model, however, uses hard assignments. This soft‑to‑hard gap has no theoretical justification in the current work. An important question, sharpened by recent analyses of trustworthy Koopman learning, is whether the algorithm’s success implies that the hard partition eventually approximates a closure under multiplication, or whether the gap masks some residual algebraic inconsistency. As Conradie and colleagues have argued, Koopman approximations deserve rigorous invariance diagnostics, not just visual checks of the eigenvalue cloud. A quantitative metric for spectral pollution reduction is sorely missing.

A further concern, raised in dialogue with the authors of a recent guide to Koopman learning, is that the finite‑dimensional multiplicative DMD is constructed on the already‑learned partition; there is no proof that the alternating scheme converges to a genuinely invariant subspace. The method is an empirical demonstration, not a convergence‑theorem pipeline. Dietrich, in a study of the algebra of Koopman eigenfunctions, has reminded us that the simplicity of the product rule hides profound infinities; jumping from the continuous algebra to a finite discrete one inevitably demands care that can easily be overlooked.

Nevertheless, DeepMDMD suggests a practical rule that may steer the field: learn the coordinates, and then constrain the algebra. It unites two strands that have long been separated—deep learning for representation discovery and structure‑preserving numerical methods for Koopman approximations. By forcing a learned coordinate system to obey a simple multiplication law, the researchers are imposing a kind of grammar that may mirror how nature organizes its own emergent regularities. The road ahead will require mathematicians to formalise the soft‑to‑hard transition and to develop quantitative error bounds, while engineers will test whether a Koopman model that respects its own algebra can improve real‑time control of turbulent flows or climate forecasting. The core idea, however, may outlast this particular algorithm. The Koopman program, once a theoretical curiosity, is learning to speak the language of data, and it is learning to obey its own grammar.
