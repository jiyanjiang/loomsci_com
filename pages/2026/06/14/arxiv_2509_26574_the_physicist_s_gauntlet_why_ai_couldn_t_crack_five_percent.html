The Physicist's Gauntlet: Why AI Couldn’t Crack Five Percent

Drafted May 12, 2026 · Published June 14, 2026 Artificial Intelligence Source: arXiv:2509.26574

Picture a first‑year graduate student, newly assigned to a research group, being handed a stack of problems by their advisor. “These aren’t in any textbook,” the advisor says. “But if you can crack them, we might have a paper.” After three sleepless weeks, the student trudges back, having solved exactly one.

Now imagine the advisor is fifty working physicists, the problems span the entire landscape of modern physics, and the student is the most advanced large language model on the planet. You now have a fair picture of the sobering result delivered by the CritPt benchmark.

In a preprint (arXiv:2509.26574), a team co‑led by Minhui Zhu at Argonne National Laboratory and Minyang Tian at the University of Illinois Urbana‑Champaign, together with over fifty active physicists, reports that the best‑performing model—GPT‑5 in its highest‑thinking mode—solves just 5.7% of the full challenges. Even when the models were given access to coding tools, the top score barely cleared one in ten. For perspective, a human graduate student scoring that low on a qualifying exam would be politely asked to reconsider their career. For a machine that can breeze through law‑board exams and beat grandmasters at Go, it is nothing short of a cold shower.

What makes this result so striking is not merely the low number. It is the nature of the problems that stumped the models. The CritPt benchmark is the first large‑scale effort to test AI on research‑level physics problems—the sorts of open‑ended tasks a principal investigator might use to probe a student’s readiness for independent work. None of the problems were scraped from existing textbooks or online forums. Each was freshly designed by a physicist actively working in the field, deliberately crafted to require multi‑step reasoning, physical intuition, and the ability to chain together concepts from different subfields. The benchmark spans condensed matter, quantum physics, astrophysics, high‑energy physics, and more—a breadth that would challenge even a broad‑thinking postdoc.

Top accuracy scores hide a wide spread in thinking steps and cost among models. That difference matters because the research aims to pinpoint where extra reasoning stops improving performance—the “critical point” of AI intelligence. (Source: arXiv:2509.26574)

But here the story grows more interesting than a simple failure. To make answers machine‑verifiable, the team had to constrain the problems so that each has a unique, checkable final answer. That is a rigorous and necessary choice—without it, grading would descend into subjective chaos. Yet a deep question arises from this very constraint: does the ability to produce a checkable final answer capture what we actually mean by “scientific reasoning” at the research frontier? Much of what physicists do—posing the right question, designing an experiment, recognising a pattern that contradicts theory—is not about arriving at a pre‑ordained answer. It is about navigating the space of possibilities before an answer exists. The benchmark’s own description of its tasks as “warm‑up research exercises” hints at this tension. An AI that can complete a pre‑defined problem may be an excellent calculator, but not yet a collaborator who can help formulate the problem in the first place.

A related concern crops up from the way correctness is judged. The autograder that scores the models checks only the final output—a number, a formula, a classification—and not the reasoning trace that led to it. The team designed the questions to be “guess‑resistant,” meaning that random guessing should perform no better than chance. Yet the possibility remains that a model could, through a combination of pattern‑matching and lucky numerical flukes, produce the right answer for reasons that a physicist would deem insufficient or even mistaken. We are left asking not just whether the machine got it right, but whether it understood why it was right. That distinction—between correctness and comprehension—matters enormously when we imagine AI guiding experimental design or theory development.

The benchmark’s portrait of model limitations is further sharpened by what it omits. Several state‑of‑the‑art models that explicitly incorporate deliberative “thinking” modes, such as Qwen3, were not tested. In a separate line of work, researchers have shown that structured, multi‑turn reasoning—where a model learns to pause, verify intermediate steps, and self‑correct—can lift performance on complex tasks. Whether such an approach would move the needle on CritPt is an open question, but its absence leaves a gap that future studies will need to fill. For now, the 5.7% ceiling must be read as an upper bound only for the specific class of models that rely on single‑pass generation, not as a universal limit on what AI‑assisted reasoning might one day achieve.

Despite these caveats, CritPt is a landmark contribution to the AI‑for‑science community—not because it demonstrates that current models are bad at physics (we suspected that), but because it provides a reproducible, standardised yardstick for tracking progress. The checkpoint decomposition, which breaks each full challenge into nearly two hundred simpler intermediate steps, acts like a high‑resolution diagnostic. It reveals precisely where the chain of reasoning snaps: perhaps a model can set up the equations correctly but cannot navigate the algebraic thicket that follows, or it can apply a conservation law but fails to notice that a symmetry has been broken by boundary conditions. That granularity turns a simple score into a flashlight that can guide the next generation of training strategies.

The lead author, Minhui Zhu at Argonne, and the large collaboration of researchers from the University of Illinois Urbana‑Champaign, Virginia Tech, Ohio State University, and independent contributors did not set out to embarrass the AI industry. Their aim was to create a testbed that mirrors—as faithfully as possible given the constraints of machine evaluation—the kind of reasoning demanded at the physics frontier. In that, they have succeeded. The paper is honest about its boundaries: it does not claim to measure creativity, hypothesis generation, or the ability to discern which problems are worth solving. It tests only the capacity to reason through questions that, once posed, have a definite answer. That is a crucial skill, but it is only one thread in the rich tapestry of scientific discovery.

Perhaps the most valuable thing CritPt offers is a shift in perspective. For years, headline‑grabbing AI benchmarks have featured high‑school competition problems or coding tasks with clean inputs and outputs. The real‑world research environment is messier. A physicist does not receive a problem statement with all necessary context; they must assemble the relevant theory from memory, decide which approximations are justifiable, and often wrestle with incomplete information. The gap between textbook elegance and laboratory reality is vast. By grounding its evaluation in genuine research tasks—albeit tightly curated—CritPt forces the field to acknowledge that gap honestly.

In the end, a score of 5.7% is not a verdict on AI’s potential. It is a call to reimagine what we mean by “reasoning” in physics. The true test may not be whether a machine can solve the puzzles we already know how to answer, but whether it can ask questions we haven’t thought to ask. The student in my opening allegory did not fail because she was stupid; she failed because she was measured against a standard—independent curiosity and creative synthesis—that no amount of pattern‑matching can replicate. What CritPt illuminates is that reasoning at the frontier is as much about what you choose to see as about what you manage to compute. And that child, the one who doesn’t know how to ask, may already be pointing toward the physics we have yet to imagine.
