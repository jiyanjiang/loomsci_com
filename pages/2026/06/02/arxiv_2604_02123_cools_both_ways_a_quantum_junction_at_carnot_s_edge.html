Cools Both Ways: A Quantum Junction at Carnot’s Edge

June 02, 2026 Matter Science Source: arXiv:2604.02123

What if a heat engine could not only inch toward the Carnot limit—the theoretical ceiling of all thermal machines—but also, with a flick of a voltage, turn itself into a refrigerator that cools either of its two reservoirs? That is the surprising and, in some ways, humbling question posed by a new theoretical study from the University of Pisa. In a preprint (arXiv:2604.02123), Leonardo Lucchesi and Federico Paolucci show that a simple sandwich of a superconductor, an insulator, and a two-dimensional electron gas—a construction no more elaborate than a transistor—can generate thermoelectricity via a strongly nonlinear mechanism, reach efficiencies scraping 92% of the Carnot ideal, and steer heat in both directions on command. The proposal is not merely an exercise in thermodynamics; it sketches a platform that could, one day, serve as an on-chip refrigerator for fragile qubits or as a detector so sensitive that it might feel the whisper of a dark-matter particle.

The heart of the device is a tunnel junction where a superconductor and a two-dimensional electron gas (2DEG) are separated by a thin insulating barrier. A 2DEG is a sheet of electrons confined so tightly that they can only move in a plane, and at low temperatures they behave like a nearly perfect metal. The superconductor, of course, brings with it an energy gap: no single electron can occupy states inside that forbidden zone. When the two sides are at different temperatures, the junction behaves like a turnstile that admits passengers in one direction only. Thermal excitations create quasielectrons above the gap in the hotter superconductor; those energetic carriers can tunnel across the barrier into the continuum of states in the 2DEG. But their opposite numbers—quasiholes, the absence of an electron in the filled sea below the gap—find the door slammed shut, because the 2DEG’s band structure can be tuned to present no states at the appropriate energy. The result is a net electrical current driven purely by heat, with no applied voltage whatsoever.

That one-way traffic, the team argues, is what gives the SI2DEG junction its extraordinary punch. When the circuit is left open, the migrating charge piles up and develops a voltage, the Seebeck potential, that can grow far beyond the simple linear response familiar from textbook thermocouples. By adjusting the electron population of the 2DEG—effectively rotating a knob that shifts its band alignment relative to the superconductor’s gap—the researchers found several distinct operating regimes. In one, the voltage is modest and the behavior almost ohmic; in another, the voltage soars to over six times the superconducting gap energy, a nonlinear magnification that conventional thermoelectric materials cannot match. The Seebeck coefficient itself, which in ordinary metals is measured in microvolts per kelvin, here reaches values dozens of times larger than the gap’s energy divided by temperature—a number that sounds dry but means the junction is extraordinarily sensitive to the faintest thermal gradients.

It is this sensitivity that turns the SI2DEG into a near-perfect heat engine. When Lucchesi and Paolucci connected an electrical load to extract power, they found that the junction could deliver useful work at efficiencies that hug the Carnot bound. In some parameter regimes the efficiency topped out at 96% of Carnot, and at more experimentally accessible temperatures it hovered around 92%. Crucially, this was not an empty figure achieved at vanishing output—a common trick of idealized models where efficiency climbs only when the current drops to zero. The device produces a non-negligible power, roughly one tenth of a natural scale set by the junction's conductance and the gap energy, though at a higher temperature. The intriguing twist is that the engine achieves its highest efficiencies in a lower-temperature regime where the current–voltage curve flattens near the open-circuit point—allowing it to operate near the Carnot bound at maximum power. Classical thermoelectrics, with their gently sloping characteristics, are forced to trade efficiency for output; the quantum turnstile appears to negotiate that dilemma with unusual grace.

But the strangest feature of the SI2DEG junction is not its efficiency; it is its ambivalence. Apply a bias voltage across the device while both reservoirs sit at the same temperature, and it becomes a refrigerator that can pump heat in either direction. By changing the sign of the bias, you can cool the superconductor while warming the 2DEG, or the other way around. The authors call this bidirectional cooling, and it arises because the bias shifts the electrochemical potential so that the dominant tunneling process either absorbs heat from one side or the other. In a world where most refrigerators can only pull heat from a cold body and dump it into a hot one, this dual personality feels almost like a violation of the second law. But it is not. The work done by the voltage source supplies the necessary entropy; the direction of heat flow is merely a choice. The junction is less a one-purpose machine than a thermal switch, able to route heat according to the needs of a circuit.

Further tinkering reveals yet another trick: bistability. For certain choices of band alignment and temperature, the same temperature gradient across the junction can support two distinct stable voltages. It is as if the device’s state diagram develops a fold, a hysteresis loop where the voltage does not simply climb with the temperature difference but can inhabit one branch or another depending on its history. In a practical sense, that means the SI2DEG could act as a memory element—binary states written and read by heat pulses—or as a threshold detector for small temperature excursions. That a stack of unprepared materials, governed by nothing more than the Schrödinger equation and the BCS pairing potential, can exhibit such plasticity is a reminder that the space between simple models and technological relevance is often narrower than we imagine.

All of this emerges from a proposal that is, in its bones, a return to first principles. The equations the Pisa team solves are a Landauer-like tunneling formula—a standard framework for superconducting interfaces—here enriched with a realistic treatment of the 2DEG's density of states and the self-consistent potential that develops when charge builds up. There are no exotic materials, no esoteric fabrication steps, no appeal to Majorana fermions or topological protection. The discovery is conceptual: that by marrying a superconductor to a deliberately misaligned semiconductor, you can choreograph a thermal ballet that is at once highly efficient and acutely controllable. It is, in a sense, a manifesto for what quantum thermodynamics can achieve when it stops chasing abstract limits and starts interrogating specific, messy junctions.

If the SI2DEG turns out to be realizable—and the ingredients are, by today’s standards, almost mundane—the implications ripple outward. In quantum computing, the coldest stage of a dilution refrigerator is an expensive, crowded real estate where every millikelvin matters. An on-chip refrigerator that can selectively cool a qubit while rejecting heat into a warmer bus could transform the architecture of large-scale processors. In cosmology, the search for dark matter has grown increasingly thermal: particle candidates such as axions or light dark photons, if they interact ever so weakly with ordinary matter, would deposit minuscule amounts of heat when they scatter in a crystal. A detector built around an SI2DEG junction, with its enormous nonlinear Seebeck response, could potentially amplify that infinitesimal signal into a measurable voltage. The same device that in one mode drives a quantum engine might, in another, listen for the heat signature of a passing relic from the early universe.

One might say that the junction is an invitation to rethink what a heat engine looks like. Sadi Carnot, when he conceived his ideal cycle in 1824, imagined a frictionless piston driven by a falling temperature difference, entirely reversible and yet, in the end, nothing more than a thought experiment. For two centuries, physicists have been learning how to approach that elusive limit without sacrificing power. The SI2DEG proposal is a fresh chapter in that history. It does not promise a revolutionary new source of energy; rather, it reveals that near-Carnot performance and functional multitasking can coexist in a structure compact enough to be integrated into a chip. It dissolves the wall between engine and sensor, between work and measurement.

It also nudges us to reconsider what we mean by “heat” in a quantum context. The simple picture—jiggling atoms, random motion, irreversibility—is a coarse-grained description that ignores the quiet order possible when energies are quantized and carriers filtered. The turnstile analogy, like all analogies, has a boundary: a real turnstile discriminates by mechanical shape, not by the statistics of quantum occupations. This junction’s gatekeeping is not a trick of geometry but of band structure and the Pauli principle. The deconstruction is essential because it reminds us that the cleanliness of the idea rests on a fragile quantum choreography. Real devices will have smeared gaps, impurity states, and thermal leakage; none of these are likely to kill the effect, but they will certainly blur the clean lines of the preprint’s calculations. The team is already exploring the robustness of their predictions, and the roadmap from equations to an experimental demonstration is, for once, refreshingly short.

Lucchesi and Paolucci have not built this junction—at least not yet. But they have laid out a theoretical foundation that is unusually complete, backed by parametric sweeps that show the key features should survive in realistic conditions. The preprint ends with a discussion of the figures of merit and the voltage ranges at which bistability and bidirectional cooling appear, always with an eye toward materials that might do the job: Al for the superconductor, AlOx for the tunnel barrier, GaAs or perhaps graphene for the 2DEG. The specific choices matter less than the principle, which is that quantum thermodynamics can be engineered as deliberately as any electronic circuit.

Perhaps one day, when an experimentalist cools such a stack to a few hundred millikelvin and watches a voltage jump in response to a heat pulse a million times smaller than a single candle’s warmth, they will not simply be confirming a theoretical prediction. They will be witnessing the emergence of a new kind of thermal tool—a device that speaks the language of both work and refrigeration, of engine and detector, and that whispers of a future where quantum machines handle heat with the same finesse that transistors handle charge. The ghost of Carnot, who saw the ideal machine as an abstract limit, might finally find a concrete body in a thin wafer of superconductor and semiconductor, bending but never breaking the second law.
