The Algorithm That Unifies Decision‑Making — From Games to Economics — Under One Mathematical Roof

Drafted June 01, 2026 · Published June 02, 2026 Artificial Intelligence Source: arXiv:2605.30694

Universal decision learners. The phrase may sound like a promise from a futurist’s TED talk, but the idea that wildly different forms of reasoning — playing chess, pricing a stock option, intervening on a gene — might share a deep common structure is not new. For decades, researchers have glimpsed it in the matrix equations of reinforcement learning, the recursive recursions of planning, and the fixed‑point arguments of game theory. Yet every glimpse came wrapped in its own notation, its own software stack, its own assumptions about what counts as a “state” or an “action.” What was missing was a grammar that could say this is the same thing across all those dialects.

A preprint (arXiv:2605.30694) by Sridhar Mahadevan, a research professor at the University of Massachusetts Amherst who also works at Adobe Research, fills that gap with an unlikely tool: Kan extensions, a concept from a branch of mathematics called category theory that studies how structures are preserved when you move from one context to another. Mahadevan’s central claim is not that every decision algorithm should be replaced by a single piece of code. It is that nearly all of them solve the same abstract problem — extend what you know locally into what you should do globally — and that this problem always admits two canonical forms, a left one and a right one. The paper shows how Bellman equations, the linear‑equation solvers at the heart of reinforcement learning, are right Kan extensions; how planning roll‑outs are left Kan extensions; how causal interventions, online regret, and Nash equilibria all fall into place as special cases of the same universal construction.

The puzzle that every decision system must solve

To see why this matters, picture a traveler mapping an unknown landscape. She has charted a few connected valleys and knows which paths lead to water. Now she stands at the edge of the known world, facing uncharted terrain. How should she extend her incomplete map? One strategy is to forge ahead boldly, trusting that the patterns she has already observed continue in the simplest way: a valley that looks like the last one probably has water in the same place. Another strategy is to move only with extreme caution — to admit into the map nothing that cannot be reconciled with every scrap of data she already possesses.

These two impulses — optimistic extrapolation versus cautious consistency — are not just the traveler’s dilemma. They are the mathematical tension at the heart of every decision‑making algorithm, whether it is playing Go, scheduling a factory floor, or setting a thermostat. Mahadevan’s framework captures them as the two universal solutions to a single abstract task: extend a partially defined decision functor. A decision functor is any mapping from contexts — states of a game, configurations of a market, positions of a robot — to choices or values. You know it on some subset of contexts (your training data, your past experience). The universal problem is to extend it to all contexts without losing coherence.

Category theory gives two canonical answers. The left Kan extension is the most optimistic, “free” extension: it takes the known data and rolls it forward into the unknown, generating candidates even when there is no evidence. The right Kan extension is the most conservative, “coherent” extension: it demands that every new assignment be consistent with everything you already know, often resulting in a fixed‑point equation that must be satisfied. Both are universal in the precise sense that any other reasonable extension factors uniquely through them. If you have a way of turning local knowledge into global behavior that respects the structure of your problem, that way is essentially the left or the right Kan extension dressed in different clothes.

This is not merely an analogy for how decision‑making “feels.” Mahadevan proves the corresponding categorical theorems. He defines a notion of Kan‑invariant behavioral equivalence — two decision systems are the same if their extension diagrams commute — and shows that minimal abstractions, the simplest representations that capture the essential decision logic, emerge naturally from the universal property. The mathematics is deep, but its payoff is a shift in viewpoint: coherence is not a side effect of good decision‑making; it is the definition of it.

Bellman, equilibria, and the fixed‑point at the heart of everything

The most famous equation in reinforcement learning is the Bellman equation. It says that the value of a state is the immediate reward plus the discounted value of the best next state, a recursion that is usually solved by dynamic programming. Under Mahadevan’s lens, the Bellman equation is exactly a right Kan extension in a category where contexts are states and transitions are morphisms. The fixed‑point that the equation demands — a value function that remains unchanged under the Bellman operator — is the universal coherent extension of the reward signal to all states. In other words, the Bellman equation is not a clever hack; it is the only way to extend local rewards to a globally consistent value function while respecting the structure of the state space.

This is the pattern that recurs across the paper’s many examples. Planning: the frontier expansion of a search algorithm is a left Kan extension (it grows the tree optimistically), while the min‑max backup that evaluates a node is a right Kan extension (it enforces consistency with the leaf values). Causal inference: the effect of an intervention is computed by a left Kan extension that rolls forward the consequences of a local change, while causal discovery, which demands that the graph be consistent with observed conditional independencies, is a right Kan extension. Online learning: the minimax regret formulation is a right Kan extension that finds a strategy consistent with the worst‑case history, while the optimistic follow‑the‑leader algorithm is a left Kan extension that trusts recent performance. Game theory: Nash equilibria, Baysian equilibria, and correlated equilibria are all fixed‑points that result from right Kan extensions in appropriate categories.

The framework does not claim that these algorithms arose from category theory. They were all invented piecemeal. What it does claim, and demonstrate mathematically, is that the same categorical scaffolding underlies them all. It is as if, without knowing it, each subfield of AI discovered one room in the same building and then named its furniture in its own local language. Mahadevan’s paper provides the floor plan and the universal grammar.

The road ahead

The paper is not the end of a story; it is the beginning of a new way of looking. The formal framework invites further questions. If the left and right Kan extensions are the two canonical forms, what lies in between? Mahadevan sketches a spectrum of hybrid extensions, reminiscent of the trade‑off between exploration and exploitation, which may yield new types of decision algorithms that systematically blend optimism and caution. The definition of behavioral equivalence via Kan invariance also opens the door to a principled theory of abstraction in AI: two representations are the same if they can be mapped to each other without breaking the universal extension property.

Perhaps the most intriguing implication is a change in what we mean by “intelligence.” The standard narrative frames intelligence as optimization — maximizing a reward, minimizing a loss. Mahadevan’s framework reframes it as extension: taking the local, partial picture of the world that an agent has and building from it the most globally coherent possible guide to action. Coherence is the currency, not utility. That shift may sound philosophical, but it has practical teeth. If the fundamental problem of decision‑making is to extend local information canonically, then algorithms that enforce global consistency — even at the expense of short‑term reward — may turn out to be more robust, more transferable, and more human‑like.

No single paper can deliver a ready‑to‑use universal decision learner that outperforms AlphaZero. Mahadevan’s contribution is more conceptual. It hands the community a new language in which to ask questions, a set of theorems that connect previously isolated islands of research, and a proof that the search for a universal grammar of decision‑making is not a philosopher’s daydream — it is a precise mathematical project that, for the first time, has a concrete formulation and a clear roadmap.
