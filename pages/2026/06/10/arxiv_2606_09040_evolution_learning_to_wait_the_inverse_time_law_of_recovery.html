Evolution Learning to Wait: The Inverse-Time Law of Recovery

Drafted June 09, 2026 · Published June 10, 2026 Bioscience Source: arXiv:2606.09040

We think of evolution as a relentless climb: mutation after mutation, each nudging a population toward a peak on some fitness landscape. But when a catastrophe wipes out the old niches — a sudden antibiotic, a climate crash — the survivors face a landscape that is both familiar and foreign. They must adapt, and fast. The question is: does natural selection, in that frantic moment, simply sprint up the steepest slope it can find? A new preprint (arXiv:2606.09040) from a team at the University of Chicago says no. It reveals that post‑catastrophic evolution obeys a law of surprising simplicity: the average fitness cost decays inversely with time, and the rate is set not by the strength of selection but by the number of traits that matter. More striking still, the underlying algorithm is not the greedy gradient ascent many of us learned to expect, but something closer to what mathematicians call Levenberg–Marquardt optimization — an algorithm that is, in a precise sense, optimistic rather than short‑sighted.

This is not a metaphor. The inverse‑time law falls out of the mathematics of selection on standing variation, and the team — Jesse Young Lin, Omer Granek, Joshua Sodicoff, Seppe Kuehn, David Pincus, and Vincenzo Vitelli, drawn from several departments at the University of Chicago — confirms it against two independent antibiotic‑resistance datasets in E. coli. Their work does for evolutionary dynamics what calorimetry did for thermodynamics: it counts the effective degrees of freedom.

The Landscape After the Fire

Imagine a city after a fire. The old neighbourhoods are gone; survivors gather in the rubble. Their skills — the traits that served them before — are now tested against a new environment. A baker may still bake, but the recipes that once prospered may now fail. In evolutionary terms, the catastrophe has collapsed the population into a small region of trait space, and the fitness landscape around them suddenly matters. Selection on the standing genetic variation then pushes the population toward a new peak.

This picture, known as selection on standing variation, is exactly what the Chicago team captured mathematically. In their model, the fitness of each variant depends on several independent traits, collectively denoted "z₁, z₂, …". The key insight is that the geometry of the fitness landscape — how it curves, how many directions matter — leaves a fingerprint on the pace of recovery.

To test this, the team analysed two massive static fitness assays: one by Otto et al. that surveyed over 4000 variants of an E. coli gene using gene‑repression, and a second by Papkou et al. that examined a staggering 261,000 variants of the folA gene. Both datasets independently pointed to a landscape shaped by exactly two traits, with a monotonic, single‑peaked structure. It is not a chaotic tangle but a clean topographic map.

Bacteria evolve antibiotic resistance through changes in only two essential traits. This simplicity lets scientists predict how populations rebound after a devastating antibiotic shock. (Source: arXiv:2606.09040)

The Inverse‑Time Law and Fitness Calorimetry

The mathematical centrepiece of the paper is elegantly simple. Let D(t) be the mean fitness cost — the gap between the average fitness and the theoretical peak — as a function of time after the catastrophe. The theory predicts that, for a sufficiently large and well‑positioned initial population,

D(t) ~ n/Qt,

Where n is the number of independent traits under selection and Q is a measure of how sharply the fitness peak curves around its summit. The 1/t decay is universal; it emerges from the mathematics of replicator dynamics acting on a trait distribution near the fitness peak. The prefactor n/Q is the diagnostic: it tells you how many directions evolution can move in, and how forgiving the peak is in each direction.

An arresting analogy runs through the paper. In thermodynamics, the specific heat of a di‑atomic gas rises in stepwise plateaus — one for translational motion, another for rotation, then vibration — as temperature climbs. Each plateau reveals a degree of freedom that is “thawing out.” The team found a similar phenomenon in their trait‑counting: the decay of the mean fitness cost, when plotted on a log‑log scale, shows transient plateaus whose height encodes n/Q. They call this “fitness calorimetry.” This is not a decorative metaphor; the mathematics of Laplace transforms links the static distribution of fitness costs (the calorimetry analog) to the kinetic decay (the dynamics analog). The two measurements are inter‑convertible, just as heat capacity and energy relate.

A power-law decay in the fitness cost’s slope tells us how many traits evolution is sculpting. This helps researchers decode the hidden shape of the fitness landscape from genetic data after a catastrophe. (Source: arXiv:2606.09040)

The real‑world data matched the prediction without a single free parameter. Both antibiotic‑stress assays gave n/Q = 2, confirming a two‑trait, monotonic landscape. This convergence, across vastly different genetic screens, suggests that the inverse‑time law is not a quirk of one system but a robust signature of post‑catastrophic selection.

When Greed Fails: The Levenberg–Marquardt Connection

If the fitness landscape is a hill, the simplest algorithm for climbing it is gradient ascent: at each step, take the direction of steepest gain. This is the equivalent of a greedy strategy that never looks ahead. For a symmetrical, gently curved peak, gradient ascent works well — until you get close to the top. There, the slope becomes shallow, and greed can become inefficient.

Newton’s method offers a more sophisticated alternative. It considers not just the local gradient but also the curvature — the Hessian matrix of the landscape. By inverting the curvature, Newton steps account for the fact that some directions are steeper than others, re‑weighting the path so that it takes shorter, better‑aimed strides near the summit. For a narrow ridge, gradient ascent might spend too much time zigzagging up the steep sides; Newton’s method cuts across.

The catch is that Newton’s method is unreliable far from the peak, where the curvature can be misleading. The Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm interweaves the two: on the slopes it follows the gradient, but as it approaches the peak it blends in Newton‑like corrections, effectively adjusting the step length to match the local geometry.

Here the Chicago paper lands its most provocative finding. When the team simulated stochastic birth‑death dynamics on a model two‑trait landscape, the highest‑frequency trait trajectories did not obey pure gradient ascent. Instead, they matched the predictions of Levenberg–Marquardt evolution exactly. Evolutionary dynamics, it seems, already know this trick.

The reason lies in the statistics of standing variation. After a catastrophe, the population distribution of traits is typically peaked near a point that is not at the fitness maximum. The subsequent selection process acts on the entire distribution, shifting its mean and contracting its width. The team proved that the resulting mean‑trait trajectory satisfies a deterministic equation that, in the limit of large populations and gentle curvature, reduces to a continuous‑time version of the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm. Crucially, the algorithm’s parameter — the blending factor between gradient and Newton steps — is not externally tuned. It emerges from the population’s variance in trait space, which itself shrinks over time. The system adaptively becomes more Newton‑like as the peak draws near.

Optimism as a Strategy

What makes this mechanism so compelling is its built‑in caution. Near the peak, the Levenberg–Marquardt dynamics often chooses a step that is less fit in the immediate generation, preferring instead to reposition the population for a larger gain in the next. The authors call this “optimistic” selection. It is not altruism; it is a consequence of selection acting on a distribution of traits, where the less common variants can, in the right circumstances, act as scouts.

This pattern of examining the experimental data and the mathematical theory in parallel — running them like two optical paths that, when re‑aligned, reveal the same hidden object — is a narrative tool the team uses throughout. It allows them to transform what might have been a dry optimization problem into a story about how living systems, simply by playing the odds of natural selection, discover algorithms that human mathematicians later formalise.

Underlying the entire edifice is a deep physical analogy. Just as the equipartition of energy allows physicists to count the number of excited degrees of freedom in a molecule, the inverse‑time law allows evolutionary biologists to count the number of traits under selection. The paper’s “trait counting” is therefore more than a neat trick; it is an operational definition of biological complexity in a fitness landscape — and a tool that might generalise far beyond bacteria.

The two‑trait result from the E. coli experiments is especially satisfying because it lands squarely between the trivial (one trait) and the intractable (many traits). With two dimensions, the landscape can be visualised, the Levenberg–Marquardt trajectories can be plotted, and the theoretical predictions can be tested without ambiguity. The team shows, for instance, that the static distribution of fitness costs follows a prediction that Σ(ρ) ~ ρn/Q, and that this indeed falls onto a clean ρ2 line for both datasets — no fitting required.

The Road Ahead

Every law of nature opens more doors than it closes. The 1/t relaxation is expected to hold only so long as the population is polymorphic and the landscape is roughly parabolic near the peak. Once near fixation, other forces — drift, new mutations — will take over. The team points out that their framework can be extended to landscapes with multiple peaks, time‑dependent fitness, or even co‑evolving traits. The Levenberg–Marquardt connection, in particular, suggests that the algorithmic character of evolution may be more than a coincidence; it may be a signature of any selection process that acts on a distribution of continuous variation.

For researchers poring over genomic data after an antibiotic course, a pandemic outbreak, or even a climate‑driven extinction event, the inverse‑time law offers a ruler. By fitting a simple decay curve, one can extract the effective number of traits under selection — and hence, indirectly, the complexity of the adaptive challenge. This is more than academic. Knowing that recovery is two‑dimensional, not ten, may alter how we design treatment regimens or conservation strategies.

The Chicago team’s approach, grounded in experimental validation and physical reasoning, builds a bridge between the abstract world of optimization theory and the messy, living reality of a Petri dish. It reminds us that the most fundamental laws are often the simplest — and that evolution, in the wake of catastrophe, knows how to wait.
