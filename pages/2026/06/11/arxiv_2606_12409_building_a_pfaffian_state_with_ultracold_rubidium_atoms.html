Building a Pfaffian State with Ultracold Rubidium Atoms

June 11, 2026 Matter Science Source: arXiv:2606.12409

In a laboratory at Harvard University, three atoms of rubidium‑87 float in a vacuum chamber, chilled to a fraction of a degree above absolute zero. Lasers weave them into a checkerboard of light — an optical lattice — while a synthetic magnetic field, stitched together from precisely timed laser pulses, bends their motion into tiny, curved arcs. Within this carefully controlled miniature universe, the three atoms settle into a quantum state that condensed‑matter physicists have been chasing for more than thirty years: the Pfaffian quantum Hall state. A preprint (arXiv:2606.12409) from a team led by Joyce Kwan and Markus Greiner at Harvard reports the first realization of this elusive phase with ultracold atoms, opening a new route to engineer the kind of topological order that might one day underpin a fault‑tolerant quantum computer.

To understand why this matters, it helps to recall what a quantum Hall state is. In a strong magnetic field, electrons confined to two dimensions can organize themselves into a liquid‑like collective in which the electrical current flows only around the sample’s edge with remarkable precision. The simplest version of this phenomenon — the integer quantum Hall effect — is already mind‑bending, but the fractional quantum Hall effect is stranger still: here, the collective motion behaves as if the electron had split into pieces carrying fractions of the elementary charge. Among these fractional states, the one proposed by Gregory Moore and Nicholas Read in 1991 stands out. Its wavefunction describes electrons (or bosons) that pair up in a p‑wave fashion, much like the Cooper pairs in a superconductor, but with a crucial twist: the paired state hosts quasiparticles whose exchange statistics are non‑Abelian. Braiding two such quasiparticles around each other does not just multiply the wavefunction by a sign; it transforms the system between different quantum states. That property could be used to store and manipulate quantum information in a way that is intrinsically protected against local noise.

Realising the Moore–Read Pfaffian state in a real material, however, has been extraordinarily difficult. In two‑dimensional electron gases, the required strong magnetic fields and cryogenic temperatures conspire to make the pairing fragile and hard to probe. The Harvard team took a dramatically different route. Instead of electrons, they used neutral rubidium‑87 atoms; instead of a real magnetic field, they created a synthetic one by shaking the optical lattice with laser beams (a technique called Floquet engineering). By tuning the shaking, they could dial in an effective magnetic flux per plaquette that mimics the conditions of a fractional quantum Hall system. And because the atoms are bosons, not fermions, the state they sought was a bosonic Pfaffian — the analogue of the Moore–Read state for particles that like to cluster together.

The payoff of working with ultracold atoms is an extraordinary degree of control. The team could place exactly three atoms on the lattice and then slowly — over hundreds of milliseconds — deform the trapping potential to coax the system into the desired quantum state. The deformation followed a ramp determined not by human intuition but by a Bayesian optimization algorithm, which learned from thousands of numerical simulations how to avoid exciting the atoms out of the ground state. Even then, the remaining disorder in the lattice created a challenge: the gap that protects the Pfaffian state can close, making the ramp diabatic and ruining the fidelity. By carefully measuring and incorporating the local potential disorder, the researchers were able to rescue the preparation. The final state showed the strong three‑body correlations that are the fingerprint of the Pfaffian. When the atoms were imaged site‑by‑site, the probability of finding three atoms bunched together at short distances was dramatically suppressed — just what one expects from a paired state in which two of the three particles form a composite boson while the third stays apart.

The quantitative evidence is compelling. The measured many‑body ground state overlapped with the exact three‑particle Pfaffian wavefunction by ninety-four percent, while the overlap with the corresponding unpaired normal state was negligible. The temperature, estimated at about a quarter of the tunneling energy, was low enough that thermal smearing could not account for the observed correlations. These numbers, combined with the pattern of two‑ and three‑point connected correlations, build a sharp picture: the system is not a thermal mixture of trivial configurations, but a quantum state with genuine Pfaffian pairing.

An important theoretical question is whether three-body suppression alone could emerge from ordinary repulsive interactions in a small lattice, without invoking any exotic pairing. The Harvard team checked this possibility by comparing the Pfaffian correlations with those of a normal state at the same temperature and density; the normal state shows much weaker suppression. Moreover, the high wavefunction overlap would be impossible if the observed state were a generic interacting phase. So while the system is undeniably tiny — three particles on a small optical lattice — the internal evidence strongly favours the Pfaffian interpretation.

Even more striking is the transport response. When the team applied a weak force along one direction of the lattice, the atom cloud’s centre of mass drifted sideways, exactly as a quantum Hall fluid should. The drift velocity corresponds to a Hall conductivity of roughly 0.8 times the conductance quantum, a value consistent with the topological nature of the Pfaffian state. Earlier theoretical studies of Hall drift in fractional quantum Hall systems had predicted that small systems would show plateaus in the conductivity, and this measurement, although performed at the edge of the stability region, lines up with those expectations.

Of course, a true demonstration of non‑Abelian statistics — braiding quasiparticles and reading out the accumulated quantum transformation — remains a distant goal. The present experiment is, in a sense, a proof of principle: it shows that synthetic quantum matter can host the required topological order, and that the tools of ultracold atom physics can prepare and probe it with exquisite precision. The next generation of experiments will need more particles, larger lattices, and protocols that can move quasiparticles around and reveal their braiding statistics. There is also an open question about how the Pfaffian pairing survives as the system size grows; numerical evidence for four particles on a slightly larger lattice is encouraging, but extending to many‑body systems will require new ideas.

The Harvard group’s achievement resonates with a broader effort in quantum simulation. Their laser‑based synthetic magnetic field, borrowed from the toolbox of quantum gas microscopy, is of the same general type that has been used to study the Hofstadter butterfly and other topological band structures. The innovation here is not just the creation of the field but the marriage of that field with a carefully tailored many‑body interaction, turning an optical lattice into a playground for fractional quantum Hall physics. The Bayesian‑optimized ramp stands as a reminder that the boundary between experimental physics and machine learning is now thoroughly blurred: the algorithm found a path through a complex energy landscape that would have been painfully slow to map by hand.

What we have, then, is a new platform. The bosonic Pfaffian state is a first, specific target; the same methodology could, in principle, be extended to other exotic topological phases — the Read–Rezayi series, for example, or states supporting Fibonacci anyons that can perform universal quantum computation. The ability to prepare, image, and manipulate such states in a cold‑atom laboratory, away from the constraints of solid‑state materials, brings the dream of topological quantum information processing one step closer. But the path is steep, and the current three‑particle demonstration is like building a single transistor before the age of integrated circuits.

Still, the feeling in the community is one of cautious excitement. For decades, fractional quantum Hall states seemed almost miraculous — emergent phenomena that appear in high‑mobility semiconductors under extreme conditions. Now we have begun to build them, atom by atom, from the bottom up. The Pfaffian state, with its promise of non‑Abelian anyons, stands as a milestone on that journey.
