The Cathedral Built from Sand: How a Simple Fermion Model May Weave Spacetime Itself

Drafted May 13, 2026 · Published June 18, 2026 Cosmos Source: arXiv:2603.27824

Imagine you are told that a breathtaking cathedral stands in a distant city, its soaring vaults and intricate stained glass visible only to those who study the architect’s singular, dazzling blueprints. The building seems inseparable from the genius who imagined it — no one believes it could be constructed with ordinary stones and the hands of local masons.

For more than a quarter-century, the AdS/CFT correspondence has been that cathedral. Discovered by Juan Maldacena in 1997, it is a precise mathematical dictionary that translates problems in a curved, higher-dimensional spacetime (anti-de Sitter space, or AdS) into the language of a quantum field theory without gravity living on its boundary — a conformal field theory, or CFT. It is a tool of immense beauty, a jewel of string theory, but it came to us as a gift from the top down: you build a special string-theoretic universe, and holography emerges as a property of that universe. The question that has hung in the air for decades is whether the cathedral can be erected from the bottom up — whether geometry and gravity can spring from the collective dance of simple, non-gravitational building blocks.

A preprint posted this week (arXiv:2603.27824) by Laith H. Haddad at the Colorado School of Mines suggests that they can. Haddad begins not with strings, branes, or 11-dimensional supergravity, but with one of the humblest models in the theoretical physicist’s toolbox: the Gross-Neveu model in one space and one time dimension, a world inhabited by fermions that interact only when they meet at the same point. From these meagre ingredients, and with no explicit geometric input whatsoever, he claims to recover the full bosonic sector of the AdS₃/CFT₂ correspondence — a higher-spin tower, the AdS metric itself, D-branes, and ultimately linearised Einstein gravity. It is an audacious, bottom-up construction that, if it holds up, rewrites the origin story of holographic spacetime.

In a field where theories of everything often seem to come from the heavens, this is a builder who wants to show you that the cathedral can rise from the sand beneath your feet.

The Master Architect’s Blueprint, and the Brickmakers’ Rebellion

First, let us set the scene. The AdS/CFT correspondence is simultaneously a computational miracle and an ontological puzzle. It tells us that a quantum theory of gravity in a certain curved space is completely equivalent to an ordinary quantum field theory on its boundary; every question about black holes or gravitational waves in the bulk can be rephrased as a question about the boundary theory without gravity. This has become a pillar of modern theoretical physics, fuelling everything from condensed-matter to heavy-ion collisions.

But the canonical derivation of AdS/CFT relies on the full apparatus of string theory, in particular the near-horizon geometry of D-branes. It is a top-down proof that works if you already accept the string-theoretic universe. An alternative, more democratic route has long been sought: can gravity “emerge” from a system that has no notion of geometry to begin with? The past decade has seen tantalising progress: entanglement entropy seems to reconstruct spatial geometry like a cosmic CT scanner; tensor networks encode bulk geometry in patterns of quantum information; and the SYK model, a quantum mechanical system of randomly interacting fermions, captures some aspects of AdS₂ holography. Yet a complete, bottom-up derivation of AdS/CFT from a simple, well-defined field theory without topological or supersymmetric scaffolding has remained out of reach.

This is the gap that Haddad’s paper attempts to bridge with an almost defiantly plain set of tools. “We derive the bosonic sector of the AdS₃/CFT₂ correspondence from the (1+1)-dimensional Gross-Neveu model with N fermion species and a local quartic interaction, with no stringy or geometric input,” the preprint announces. The claim is as straightforward as it is breathtaking.

Locking Eyes Across the Dance Floor: The Emergence of Geometry

To understand how a flat, one-dimensional line of fermions can generate a curved, two-dimensional bulk, we need to watch the fermions pair up.

The Gross-Neveu model is a theory of self-interacting fermions. When the interactions are attractive, the ground state rearranges itself: fermion-antifermion pairs condense, much as Cooper pairs do in a superconductor. These condensates are not just inert backdrops; they carry spin and can compete with one another. Haddad identifies two key players: a spin-0 (chiral) condensate that wants to align the fermions’ internal arrows in a particular way, and a spin-1 condensate that wants to align them differently. The ratio of these two condensates — how much the system is leaning toward one pairing versus the other — turns out to be a number that can vary across the theory’s parameter space.

What Haddad realises is that this ratio behaves exactly like a spatial coordinate. As the preprint puts it, “Competition between spin-0 (chiral) condensation and spin-1 pairing defines an emergent radial coordinate; local fluctuations of this condensate ratio, tracked by a comoving derivative, generate the AdS₃ line element.” The “radial” direction of the emergent bulk — the dimension that takes you from the boundary into the depths of the spacetime — is nothing but the shifting balance of two competing ways in which fermions can pair.

To see why this is not just a formal trick, one must appreciate the mathematical scaffolding that supports it. Haddad employs a Bargmann-Wigner fusion scheme: just as fusing two spin-1/2 particles in ordinary quantum mechanics can yield a spin-1 particle, fusing operator bilinears in the field theory systematically produces an infinite tower of higher-spin composite fields. Remarkably, these fields arrange themselves along a linear Regge trajectory — the slope of the line that relates a composite’s spin to its mass squared is an emergent property of the condensates. The whole tower of higher-spin gauge fields that string theory associates with the excited states of a fundamental string appears here as a purely field-theoretic effect, with no string worldsheet in sight.

But a tower of fields does not a curved spacetime make. To obtain the AdS metric, Haddad shows that the comoving derivative that tracks the local ratio of the two condensates satisfies the same algebraic relations as the AdS isometry generators. In fact, “the full SO(2,2) bulk isometry group, whose special conformal generators mix z with the boundary GN coordinates, emerges from local symmetries of the boundary condensates,” the preprint notes. The symmetries of the bulk — the very shape of the spacetime — are inherited from how the condensates rearrange themselves under transformations of the original fermion fields.

Now take the limit where the number of fermion species N becomes very large. The abstract ratio-parameter, previously just a label for a particular choice of couplings, “promotes z from a parameter to a genuine bulk dimension.” The many-fermion sum makes the coordinate continuous, like a stack of infinitely many sheets that merge into a solid. And the physics deep in the bulk, far from the boundary, turns out to be interpretable as N D1-branes — string-theoretic objects — with the correct mathematical structure of open strings stretched between them. The effective Newton constant that emerges in the linearised gravity sector scales as the reciprocal of the square of the number of fermion species; the central charge of the boundary theory, a measure of how many degrees of freedom it carries, likewise grows like N squared. These are hallmark signatures of holographic duality that the construction reproduces without ever invoking a string.

The Thermal Landscape: When Geometry Unravels

Perhaps the most visually suggestive feature of the preprint is its thermal phase diagram, which maps three distinct physical transitions in the fermion model to three well-known temperatures in the bulk gravitational picture.

In the boundary Gross-Neveu model, as one raises the temperature, spins decohere in a specific order: first the spin-2 sector loses coherence, then the spin-1, and finally chiral symmetry is restored — the fermions stop pairing altogether. Haddad’s analysis shows that these three transitions correspond, step for step, to the Hawking-Page temperature (the point at which an AdS black hole becomes thermodynamically favourable), the Hagedorn temperature (associated with an exponentially growing density of string states), and the Planck temperature (where the very notion of classical geometry breaks down). They form a layered radial profile: the nearer you are to the boundary, the higher the temperature at which that layer’s condensate dissolves. In the bulk, this means that as you plunge deeper into the emergent radial coordinate, you encounter progressively “hotter” phases of matter — a hierarchy that is pleasingly consistent with the idea that the deep infrared of holography should be inescapably quantum.

A deeper structural insight also emerges. The spin-1 condensate that governs the Hagedorn transition is shown to possess a profile that solves a nonlinear Ginzburg-Landau equation, with a core region where the condensate is depleted — the profile of a vortex. This vortex gives a concrete, field-theoretic picture of the “Hagedorn depth” in the bulk, the location beyond which the AdS geometry itself evaporates. It is a striking visual: the usual AdS disk, with its concentric rings of D1-branes and stretched strings, is slowly eaten away from the centre by a vortex that devours the condensate that sustains it.

A Blueprint with Unbuilt Foundations

The ambition of this construction demands a sober accounting of what has and has not been shown.

One of the most pressing questions, prompted by previous work on emergent geometry, is whether the large-N limit is truly necessary to obtain a smooth radial dimension. The paper argues that the species sum promotes z to a continuum, but it does not rigorously prove that a finite-N version would fail or that the large-N limit is the only route to a geometric bulk. An important line of inquiry, sharpened by earlier studies of celestial holography (Strominger 2021; Raclariu 2021), concerns the role of the local symmetries that give rise to the bulk isometries: if those symmetries survive for any N, then one might hope that an emergent geometry could appear even in a finite system, perhaps with some discretisation. Haddad’s construction points intriguingly in this direction, but the question remains open.

A second challenge, and one that the paper itself does not fully resolve, is the explicit derivation of the AdS kinetic term from the comoving derivative. The preprint states that “local fluctuations of this condensate ratio, tracked by a comoving derivative, generate the AdS₃ line element,” but the algebraic steps that connect the ratio’s fluctuations to the Einstein-Hilbert action, with the correct coefficient, are sketched rather than systematically expanded. A reader hoping to see the emergence of the full dynamics — not just the symmetry group — will find the bridge incomplete. This is not a fatal flaw; it is a gap that future work, perhaps with more detailed analysis of the condensate effective action, can fill.

A third natural question emerges from the mapping to D-branes. The identification of the deep-bulk physics with N D1-branes carrying U(N) Chan-Paton factors is elegant, but the relationship between the emergent AdS scale — the radius of curvature — and the string scale that would govern the excitations of those D-branes is not derived from the microscopic parameters of the GN model. In string theory, these scales are inputs; here, one would like to see them emerge from the fermion dynamics. Whether the construction can be pushed to determine this ratio, and hence to make predictions about the relative strength of stringy and gravitational effects, is a question that hangs over the paper’s most provocative claims.

Finally, the mapping of phase transitions, while suggestive, intersects with a delicate point recently highlighted by works on the hadron-to-quark-gluon-plasma crossover (Fujimoto et al. 2025). In those studies, the Hagedorn temperature in large-N gauge theories is often associated with a deconfinement transition whose relation to gravity is subtle. Haddad locates the Hagedorn transition squarely in the spin-1 decoherence, an identification that may need to be reconciled with the conventional understanding of how confinement and deconfinement map to bulk phase transitions. That reconciliation, if successful, could deepen both sides of the correspondence.

Sand That May Yet Become Marble

What makes this preprint worth the attention it demands is not that it delivers a finished cathedral, but that it demonstrates, with surprising concreteness, that the stones are there for the taking. A single researcher, working with a model that could be written on an index card, has produced a blueprint that reproduces an astonishing number of the AdS/CFT correspondence’s structural features without invoking string theory, supersymmetry, or pre-existing geometry. The derivation recovers the correct symmetry group, the scaling of Newton’s constant and the central charge, the spectrum of higher-spin fields, and a physically coherent phase diagram that lines up with known gravitational thermodynamics.

The gaps are real, but they are also precisely the kinds of gaps that invite further work. The derivation of the kinetic term can be completed; the necessity of large N can be probed by studying the finite-N GN model numerically or via analytical bounds; the relation between the AdS scale and the string scale can be extracted by a careful analysis of the condensate effective action near the boundary. None of these appears to be an insurmountable conceptual obstacle.

For a community that has long sought to understand how spacetime could be woven from the threads of quantum mechanics, this work offers a new kind of loom. It suggests that the answer may not lie in the complexities of string field theory, but in the simple, collective behaviour of fermion pairs choosing how to align. The cathedral, it turns out, may have been waiting all along in the sand.
