The Tower of Zeta: Black Holes' Hidden Tidal Code

June 18, 2026 Cosmos Source: arXiv:2606.19281

Every gravitational wave that reaches our detectors carries a story. Most of that story is about the cataclysmic merger that produced the wave — two neutron stars colliding, a pair of black holes spiraling into each other. But embedded in the signal, if you know where to look, is a quieter narrative: how each object responded to its partner's gravity before the final plunge. It is the story of tides raised not on oceans but on the fabric of spacetime itself, and for decades, the part of that story involving black holes has been missing.

The missing piece has a name that sounds almost sentimental. Physicists call them Love numbers — after the mathematician Augustus Edward Hough Love, who a century ago quantified how Earth's rocky body flexed under the Moon's gravitational pull. Their generalization to black holes, where spacetime itself does the flexing, began with a startling discovery in the 1980s: for a stationary black hole in a static external field, the Love numbers vanish. The tides raise no permanent deformation. The black hole refuses to respond.

But nature is rarely so simple. Tides in the real universe are not static — they are dynamical, ringing the black hole at frequencies set by the orbital dance of the binary. And when physicists began calculating these dynamical Love numbers over the past few years, something remarkable emerged from the mathematics: a hidden structure that, according to a new preprint (arXiv:2606.19281) from Mikhail P. Solon at UCLA's Mani L. Bhaumik Institute for Theoretical Physics, can now be written down in a single closed-form formula valid for every kind of tidal perturbation, regardless of spin or multipole.

To appreciate what Solon has achieved, it helps to understand the problem he was facing. Calculating the tidal response of a black hole is, in the language of theoretical physics, a problem of matching. Close to the horizon, the black hole's geometry dictates a particular solution to the equations governing perturbations of spacetime. Far away, in the wave zone where gravitational-wave detectors do their listening, a different description takes over — one organized around the expansion parameter G (Newton's constant), with higher powers of G corresponding to increasingly subtle relativistic corrections. Bridging these two regimes has been the central challenge.

The usual strategy, developed over a series of papers by multiple groups, relies on effective field theory — a technique that organizes physics by length scale, allowing theorists to compute systematically without needing to solve the full equations at every intermediate distance. The results, computed order by order in what is called the post-Minkowskian expansion, revealed an intriguing pattern: the coefficients multiplying each power of G were not arbitrary numbers but seemed to be building a familiar mathematical object, the logarithm, dressed with values of the Riemann zeta function — those mysterious numbers (like the famous sum 1 + 1/4 + 1/9 + 1/16 + …, which equals pi²/6) that appear throughout mathematics wherever deep structure lurks.

Solon's insight was to recognize that these zeta values do not represent new physics. They are, in a precise sense, the Newtonian phase in disguise. The same mathematical structure — the analytically continued harmonic number derived from the digamma function, whose argument depends on the frequency of the tide — that governs how particles scatter in the far zone of a Coulomb-like potential also controls how the logarithmic terms sum themselves into finite answers. What earlier calculations had seen as a tower of new numbers was actually the unfolding of a single, familiar object.

The result is a formula of striking economy. The dynamical response factorizes into three ingredients. First, there is a hard matching coefficient fixed by the physics near the black hole horizon. Second, an anomalous dimension — a quantity borrowed from renormalization group theory that encodes how a system's effective description changes as you probe at different scales — captures the near-zone physics. And third, a dressed logarithm carries all the far-zone information, with its argument shifted by exactly that tower of zeta values. "The dressed logarithm tau = log(RS/R) – 2∑_{k≥2} zetak eta^{k–1} is universal across all multipoles and spins," Solon writes, with eta proportional to i times the frequency times the Schwarzschild radius.

This factorization is not merely aesthetically pleasing. It makes a concrete prediction: compute the dynamical Love number for any spin and any multipole, and the same three-part structure will emerge. Solon verified this claim up to the fifteenth order in the post-Minkowskian expansion using a technique called shell effective field theory — a method he himself helped develop — finding exact agreement for scalar, electromagnetic, and gravitational perturbations. The verification is not a proof in the strict mathematical sense, but it constitutes evidence of a kind that theoretical physicists find deeply convincing.

An important question raised by earlier work on this subject is whether the scheme-independence claimed by Solon is as robust as it appears. The authors of one preceding study (arXiv:2511.02372) had constructed the dynamical response using a specific regularization procedure, raising the possibility that some of the structure Solon identifies might depend on how one defines the intermediate steps. Solon addresses this directly: his result, he argues, is independent of the renormalization scheme because the dressed logarithm captures the physical content while the scheme-dependence is absorbed into the definition of the renormalization scale. "The scheme-independent form of the dynamical response is the central result of this paper," he writes.

This sits in an interesting tension with another recent calculation (arXiv:2511.12580), which independently verified the zeta-tower structure but questioned whether the cancellation of certain near-zone contributions — contributions that in principle could spoil the clean factorization — had been fully demonstrated for individual spin-weight solutions rather than just their averages. Solon's paper does not resolve this question in exhaustive detail, but the fifteen-order verification provides substantial circumstantial evidence that the factorization holds at the level of individual modes.

From a broader perspective, the significance of this result extends beyond the specific context of black hole tides. What Solon has demonstrated is a principle that may apply to any system where a long-range potential (like the Newtonian gravitational potential) shapes both the near and far zones simultaneously. The dressing of the logarithm by zeta values is, in this view, a fingerprint of Coulomb-like physics — something that would appear whenever you have a 1/r potential governing the asymptotic behavior. Black holes happen to provide the cleanest laboratory for seeing this fingerprint, because the absence of a material surface means there is no additional physics to obscure it.

There is also a practical dimension. Future gravitational-wave detectors — the proposed space-based LISA mission, next-generation ground-based observatories like the Einstein Telescope — will be sensitive enough that the subtle imprint of dynamical tides on inspiral waveforms may become measurable. A theory that can compute those tides efficiently, without recomputing order by order in G each time, becomes a practical tool rather than a formal exercise. Solon's formula is exactly such a tool: plug in the frequency, the spin, the multipole, and the closed form delivers the answer.

The independent assessment must be honest about what this work does not do. Solon's formula applies to Schwarzschild black holes — those without rotation. Real astrophysical black holes spin, sometimes at speeds approaching the theoretical limit. Extending the result to the Kerr geometry is the obvious next step, and it is not a small one. Rotation breaks symmetries that the Schwarzschild analysis relies on, and the factorization that appears so clean in the non-rotating case may require generalization. The author acknowledges this: the paper is explicitly about the Schwarzschild case, and the road to Kerr is a separate investigation.

What this work does achieve is something rarer than a solution to a specific technical problem. It reveals a principle — a reason why the numbers came out the way they did across independent calculations by different groups. The zeta values were not coincidences. They were messages, and Solon has decoded them. The dynamical tidal response of a black hole turns out to be not an accident of perturbative expansion but a consequence of how the far zone remembers the Newtonian limit, how the near zone encodes the horizon, and how the renormalization group stitches the two together.

Perhaps, in the years ahead, when gravitational-wave astronomers search for deviations from general relativity in the ringdown phase of black hole mergers, they will find themselves using Solon's formula — or its Kerr generalization — as a null hypothesis: this is what Einstein's theory predicts, calculated not as an approximation but in closed form. Any measured deviation would then be not a failure of computation but a window into new physics.

The black hole that once seemed so reluctant to respond to its partner's gravity — its static Love numbers stubbornly zero — turns out to speak in precise mathematical sentences once you ask the right question. Its dynamical response is not complex. It is simply structured, and we are only now learning to read its grammar.
