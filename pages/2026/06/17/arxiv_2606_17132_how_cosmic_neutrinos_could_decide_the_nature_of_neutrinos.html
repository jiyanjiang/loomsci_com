How Cosmic Neutrinos Could Decide the Nature of Neutrinos

June 17, 2026 Cosmos Source: arXiv:2606.17132

What if the only way we have been trying to answer the deepest question about neutrinos has been listening with one ear covered? For decades, the search for proof that neutrinos are their own antiparticles has focused on an ultra‑rare nuclear decay, neutrinoless double beta decay. The logic is direct: if a nucleus can shed two electrons without emitting any antineutrinos, the neutrino must be capable of merging with itself, a feat only possible if it is a Majorana fermion. But that logic has a built‑in blind spot. The decay probes only the electron flavor—the neutrino’s interaction with electrons—while two entire sectors, the muon and the tau, remain out of reach. If the neutrino’s Majorana nature is hidden behind a muon‑ or tau‑flavoured door, conventional experiments would never see it.

Now, a team of physicists proposes a radically different route. In a preprint (arXiv:2606.17132), Gabriela Barenboim of the Universitat de València, Mauricio Bustamante, and Qinrui Liu show that the same question can be approached from two completely different directions—and that a correlated answer from both would settle the matter. Their idea is to combine a fixed‑target experiment that will hunt for a heavy neutral lepton, the hypothetical heavier sibling of the neutrino, with an observation of the flavour of neutrinos arriving from the most violent corners of the cosmos. If the two experimental windows reveal a concordant pattern, neutrinos are Majorana. If they show no such link, the simpler Dirac picture—neutrino and antineutrino as distinct entities—may be the truth.

The stakes are far from academic. Whether the neutrino is Dirac or Majorana touches the origin of mass, the asymmetry between matter and antimatter in the universe, and the structure of the Standard Model itself. In the seesaw mechanism, a favoured explanation for why neutrinos are so much lighter than other fermions, heavy Majorana neutrinos naturally suppress the masses of their lightweight cousins. Prove that neutrinos are Majorana, and the seesaw becomes more than an elegant conjecture; prove they are Dirac, and particle physicists must rethink the hierarchy of forces.

The team’s proposal rests on a subtle but widely studied phenomenon: a heavy neutral lepton, or HNL, can mix with the known neutrinos. In the seesaw framework, the mixing is tiny—the heavy partner can be orders of magnitude heavier than the known neutrinos—but its influence is not zero. If neutrinos are Dirac, the mixing merely adds a new, heavy mass eigenstate to the neutrino spectrum, leaving the mixing among the three known flavours unchanged. If they are Majorana, however, the heavy partner also contributes a threshold correction: just above the HNL mass, the mixing angles that govern how electron, muon, and tau neutrinos interconvert receive a sharp, flavour‑dependent shift. It is as if, at a certain energy, a new radio station begins broadcasting, and its carrier frequency subtly bends the tuning dials of the three stations we already know.

To see why this shift is so powerful, imagine that every flavour of neutrino is a distinct colour in a cosmic messenger’s palette. At the source—a gamma‑ray burst, a blazar, a supernova—the messenger paints a picture with an unknown mixture of these colours. By the time the light reaches Earth, the colours have transformed through vacuum oscillations, and we measure the changed palette with neutrino telescopes. But if a heavy neutral lepton exists, it has already reshuffled the mixing rules, adding a ghostly signature that cannot be erased by the source’s original composition. For a muon‑ or tau‑philic HNL, the shift in the atmospheric mixing angle—the one that governs the muon‑to‑tau balance—can be several percent, and for an electron‑philic HNL the solar and reactor angles move. No matter what the source looked like, the Earth‑bound palette carries a watermark.

The fixed‑target side of the experiment is entrusted to SHiP, the Search for Hidden Particles, a future beam‑dump facility at CERN designed to catch long‑lived particles in the GeV mass range. SHiP will look for HNLs directly: when a proton beam slams into a target, it can produce these heavy neutrinos, which then decay weakly inside a downstream decay volume. By counting the decays and tracking which charged leptons emerge—electrons, muons, or taus—SHiP can map the HNL’s mixing with each flavour. In that map, the team sees a fingerprint. A muon‑philic HNL, for example, will produce a characteristic distortion in the atmospheric mixing angle, one that later must show up in the astrophysical flavour ratios. A discovery at SHiP that is later echoed by KM3NeT or IceCube‑Gen2 would be the smoking gun.

This is where the dialectical tension sharpens. A sceptic might ask: why should we trust such a delicate calculation when the HNL mass approaches the electroweak scale, where quantum chromodynamics becomes non‑perturbative? Earlier work on GeV‑scale HNL phenomenology—notably by Bondarenko and collaborators—raised precisely this concern. The threshold correction derived in the paper assumes that the operator responsible for mixing can be treated as a small perturbation, but if the coupling runs strong near the HNL mass, the expansion can break down. Barenboim’s team addresses this by working in a region where the correction remains safely below the solar and atmospheric mass splittings, but the tension remains a live issue. The same anxiety surfaces regarding the so‑called “level crossing” regime at masses around 4 to 6 GeV, where the HNL mass becomes comparable to the intermediate boson masses and simple perturbative formulas require careful renormalization. The SHiP collaboration’s own sensitivity analysis has noted that the experimental reach in that window is robust, but whether the theoretical prediction can be trusted with the same confidence is a question that will need dedicated lattice gauge theory work to resolve.

A separate, perhaps more fundamental, question comes from the very definition of the threshold correction. In a renormalization‑group framework, one can sometimes shift a physical effect between the coupling and the mass matrices. How much of the Majorana shift is a genuine physical prediction, and how much is an artifact of the chosen subtraction scheme? In a detailed study of the status of heavy neutral leptons, Abdullahi and colleagues cautioned that the physical consequence of a threshold correction must survive when all observables are expressed in terms of physical masses and mixing angles at low energies. Barenboim's analysis, by expressing the shift directly in terms of the measurable mixing angles that would be inferred by a neutrino telescope, makes the case that this finite matching condition is scheme-independent and physically distinct from renormalization-group kinks—a distinction the paper makes explicit. Still, the debate underscores the intellectual tightrope that must be walked when a novel signal is predicted through a chain of perturbation theory.

If the challenges can be met, the payoff is immense. The figures in the paper tell the story without words: for a muon‑philic HNL inside the SHiP discovery window, the change in the atmospheric mixing angle reaches about fifteen percent—well above the projected sensitivity of the next‑generation multi‑detector network by 2050. For a tau‑philic HNL, the same angle bends even more dramatically, in some regions exceeding twenty percent. These shifts are not merely statistical fluctuations; they carry a distinct flavour topology that no source‑composition uncertainty can mimic. By profiling over all possible initial flavour fractions, the team demonstrates that the discrimination between the Majorana and Dirac hypotheses stays robust, even when the astrophysical source is completely unknown.

What makes the scheme so compelling is its sharp division of labour. SHiP measures the HNL’s mass and its mixing pattern with electrons, muons, and taus. With that information, the theory predicts, with no further free parameters, exactly how much the neutrino mixing angles should be altered at TeV–PeV energies. Neutrino telescopes then observe the flavour composition of astrophysical neutrinos at those energies. If the observed shift matches the prediction, the chain of reasoning locks: an HNL discovered in the laboratory has left a detectable mark on the cosmic neutrino sky, a mark that can only exist if neutrinos are Majorana. If, on the contrary, the flavour composition remains stubbornly identical to the standard‑mixing expectation, the HNL—if it exists at all—must be of Dirac type.

The dialectical elegance of this test is that it does not ask nature to pronounce a verdict in a single courtroom. It summons two independent witnesses—laboratory and cosmos—and demands that their testimonies agree. A positive signal in both is hard to dismiss as a statistical fluke. A negative result from either, on the other hand, remains ambiguous: perhaps the HNL mass lies outside the detection range, or the mixing to flavours is smaller than expected. But that very ambiguity is a strength, because it forces the field to confront the full parameter space rather than hiding behind a single, vulnerable technique.

What does all this imply for the philosophy of the neutrino? It reminds us that the simplest questions—What is a neutrino? Is it the same as its opposite?—demand multilayered answers. The universe does not hand out labels. We must extract them through an intricate interplay of high‑precision collider physics, astrophysical survey, and theoretical inference. The proposal by Barenboim, Bustamante, and Liu is a vivid illustration of that interplay: a laboratory experiment on Earth, paired with a telescope that peers into the farthest quasars, may one day answer a question that sits at the very foundation of the material world. The road will be long, the systematic errors many, and the ultimate confirmation may take a generation. Yet the conceptual clarity of the correlative test stands as a monument to the power of linking the small and the large, the engineered and the accidental, in the service of truth.
