When Quasars Learn to Breathe Fire: Extreme Outflows and the Death of Galaxies

Drafted May 07, 2026 · Published June 07, 2026 Cosmos Source: arXiv:2509.08793
Early quasars drive outflows so powerful they can blow away a galaxy's star-forming gas, halting growth within a billion years of the Big Bang.

What kills a galaxy? Not a supernova, not a collision with a neighbour — at least, not directly. The most efficient killer may be its own core: the supermassive black hole that sits at the centre of nearly every large galaxy, growing by devouring gas and, in the process, releasing energy that can blow away the fuel for future stars. For years, this idea — quasar feedback — has been a theoretical favorite, the go-to explanation for why some galaxies seem to shut down star formation almost completely. But theory is not evidence. Now, a new study brings some of the strongest evidence yet that such feedback was already at work in the universe's first billion years.

The puzzle is this: the James Webb Space Telescope has found massive galaxies that appear to have stopped forming stars when the cosmos was barely a toddler. In our standard picture, galaxies grow slowly, through mergers and accretion, taking billions of years to build their stellar populations. Yet here are objects that seem to have packed it in after less than two billion years — as if someone had turned off the gas supply. Cosmological simulations have long pointed to a single suspect: the energy released by an actively feeding supermassive black hole, a quasar. But was it actually happening?

A team led by Weizhe Liu at the Steward Observatory, together with Mingyu Li and colleagues across multiple institutions, decided to hunt for the smoking gun. Using the JWST's NIRSpec instrument in integral field unit mode, they observed luminous quasars at redshifts five and six — meaning we see them as they were less than a billion years after the Big Bang. They looked for broad, blueshifted emission from doubly ionised oxygen, a telltale sign that gas is being pushed outward at extreme speeds.

What they found was striking. Nearly a quarter of the twenty-seven quasars in their sample showed clear signatures of material moving away from the galactic centre at thousands of kilometres per second — far faster than simple gravitational motions would allow. The fastest outflow, in a quasar catalogued as J1620+5202, clocked in at roughly 8,400 kilometres per second. This is not a gentle breeze. It is a hurricane, blowing at three percent of the speed of light, capable of reaching well beyond the galaxy itself, into the circumgalactic medium and perhaps even into the intergalactic void.

Compared to quasars at lower redshifts — the more familiar, older cousins — the frequency of such extreme outflows was dramatically higher. In the z ~ 1.5–3.5 comparison sample, none of the 50 quasars showed such extreme outflows; in the z < 1 sample, only 3 out of 119 did. Among the youngest quasars in this study, the proportion was at least 3.9 times higher. Something about the early universe seems to favour these violent episodes.

The energetics are staggering. The kinetic energy carried by these outflows can exceed the total radiative output of the quasar itself — in some cases, by more than two and a half times. That is like a lamp whose heat output is greater than its light output, which sounds absurd until you consider that the gas being blasted away is also dense and clumpy, so that a large fraction of the energy goes into shoving material rather than shining. For a typical quasar in this sample, the average kinetic energy injection rate is orders of magnitude higher than what is seen in comparable objects at later cosmic epochs.

This is not merely a numerical curiosity. The threshold for "negative feedback" — the point at which an outflow is strong enough to suppress star formation in its host galaxy — is thought to be when the kinetic energy outflow rate reaches about one percent of the quasar's bolometric luminosity. The outflows in this study exceed that threshold by factors of a hundred or more. They are not just strong enough to stop star formation; they are strong enough to obliterate any cold gas reservoir within reach, perhaps for hundreds of millions of years.

An important question raised by earlier work on this topic, such as the study by Marshall and colleagues of a z≈6 quasar caught in a spectacular merger system, is whether these outflows are truly driven by quasar activity or by gravitational interactions during a galaxy collision. Mergers can stir up gas and produce winds of their own. The team acknowledges this ambiguity and has taken steps to rule out merger-dominated kinematics, but the sample is still small — only six extreme outflows — and the spatial resolution of the JWST data, while superb, does not resolve the outflow region well enough to confirm the driving mechanism in every case. As Zhu and colleagues have shown in their work on possible links between nuclear winds and cold gas outflows at similar redshifts, the connection between what happens on sub-parsec scales near the black hole and what we see on kiloparsec scales remains tentative.

This is where the science gets interesting. The team's results do not claim to have solved the problem of early galaxy quenching. Instead, they provide a clear observational benchmark: if half of all luminous quasars at these epochs produce winds this powerful, then models of galaxy formation must incorporate such feedback as a standard, not exceptional, process. Liu and colleagues, who earlier presented the Q-IFU survey on which this work builds, have already laid the groundwork for a larger statistical sample. More data are coming.

The deeper implication touches a question that is as much philosophical as physical. We think of galaxies as stable structures — slow, majestic, eternal. But the first galaxies may have been anything but. They may have burned bright and fast, their brief stellar glory extinguished by the very black holes that their gas fed. The mystery remains, but the new findings provide clear observational benchmarks. But we now have the first real evidence that the answer lies in these extreme winds, blowing at the edge of time.

The universe is not gentle with its children. And perhaps that is why we are here at all: because some galaxies died young, and the recycling of their gas seeded the next generation. The story of galaxy evolution is also a story of violence and loss — and, when a quasar breathes fire, of transformation.
